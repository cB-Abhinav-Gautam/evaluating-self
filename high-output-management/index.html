26High Output Management

Andrew S. Grove, High Output Management, Random House, 1983; Vintage edition with a new introduction, 1995

Linked to 37 chapters: supports 22 · contradicts 9 · mechanism 18 · same claim 6 · pairing 8

Grove wrote this book in 1983 after twenty years of running things at Fairchild and Intel, and its method is to treat every kind of work as production. A breakfast, a hire, a compiler, a criminal conviction and a middle manager's day are all flows with a limiting step, time offsets, inspection points and a rule about where to catch defects. The book's centre is a single sentence, which Grove later called the most important in it: a manager's output is the output of the organisational units under that manager's supervision or influence. Everything a manager does personally, the meetings, the reports, the decisions, is activity, and activity counts only through its leverage on other people's output. From that sentence the book derives its instruments. Indicators, paired so that no measure is gamed without its counterweight. Meetings as the medium in which managerial work is done, not an interruption to it. Decisions made by free discussion, a clear call and full support. Planning that produces today's tasks. An organisation that is always a hybrid of mission and function, held together by dual reporting and by culture. And, in the last part, the two levers a manager has on an individual, training and motivation, with the subordinate's maturity in the task at hand setting how closely to supervise. Grove's later introduction added a motto for the faster world of the 1990s: "Let chaos reign, then rein in chaos."

The breakfast factory

The book opens with a waiter who must deliver a three-minute egg, buttered toast and hot coffee at the same moment. Production's charter is to build and deliver to demand, on schedule, at acceptable quality, at lowest cost, and the way to plan it is to find the limiting step, the egg, and offset every other step backwards from delivery. Grove moves the same idea at once to recruiting a graduate, where the expensive plant visit is the limiting step and a phone screen raises offers per visit, and to writing a compiler, where each piece takes a unit test before assembly and the whole takes a system test before shipment. Every production has three operations, process, assembly and test, and a rule about rejects: catch the bad egg at delivery, the weak candidate on campus and the faulty module at unit test, because value added along the flow is what a late rejection throws away. When the waiter's toaster queue makes the egg a six-minute egg, the trade-offs appear, more staff, more equipment or standing inventory, and when volume justifies a continuous egg boiler the factory buys uniformity and low cost at the price of flexibility. His closing example is the criminal justice system, a funnel from report to jail in which a conviction costs over a million dollars and the limiting step is the number of cells, so that the largest investment is wasted at the last stage.

The factory manager's day begins with indicators, and Grove's rules for them follow from the fact that indicators steer attention as a cyclist steers where he looks. Any measure beats none. Measure output, not activity: orders, not sales calls. Measure something physical and countable. And pair every indicator with its counter-effect, inventory against shortages, vouchers processed against errors found, so that driving one down does not silently drive the other up. The organisation is a black box, and the manager cuts windows in it: a linearity chart that shows in April whether June's hiring target is reachable, trend charts against a standard, and the stagger chart, a forecast re-issued monthly and displayed against all its earlier versions, which Grove thought the best trend indicator he knew and wished on every economist. Control of future output means building to order or to forecast, and forecasting is done twice, by manufacturing and by sales, each held to its own prediction. Quality is inspected at receiving, in process and at the exit, and the choice between a gate, where everything waits for the test, and a monitor, where a sample is taken and the flow continues, is a choice between throughput and risk; for the same money one can monitor far more than one can gate, so Grove leans to monitoring wherever experience says big problems are unlikely, and to varying inspection with the quality observed. His example is the American embassy in London, checking a million visa applications a year of which 98 per cent are approved, where sampling would have cleared the queue.

Managerial leverage

Asked what a manager's output is, middle managers answer with activities: judgments, direction, training, planning. Grove gives outputs instead, processed wafers, working designs, educated students, a healed patient, and states the equation. Output equals the sum of activities each multiplied by its leverage, and productivity rises three ways: doing an activity faster, raising its leverage, or shifting time from low-leverage to high-leverage activities. He prints a day of his own calendar to show that a manager's work has no pattern, ends when he is tired rather than finished, and takes place two-thirds in meetings, which are therefore the medium and not the enemy. Information gathering is the base of everything else, and its best source is verbal and sketchy, like a headline; written reports exist to make the writer precise, which is why writing them matters more than reading them. Leverage is high when one person affects many, when a brief act affects someone for a long time, or when a person supplies knowledge that others depend on; it is negative when a manager arrives unprepared, waffles so that no green light becomes a red light, or spreads depression through an organisation. Delegation without monitoring is abdication, and the monitor should be applied, like inspection, at the lowest-value stage, the rough draft rather than the polished report. A supervising manager should have six to eight reports, and interruptions are handled by batching them into scheduled one-on-ones.

The meetings chapter separates process meetings, which are scheduled, from mission meetings, called to decide. The one-on-one belongs to the subordinate, who sets its agenda and prepares its outline; it lasts at least an hour, happens as often as the subordinate's maturity in the task requires, and its rule is to ask one more question until both are at the bottom of the problem. Ninety minutes every two weeks, Grove calculates, can lift the quality of two weeks of the subordinate's work. The staff meeting is where peers learn to decide together, and the operation review is where people several levels apart teach each other. A mission meeting costs money, ten managers for two hours at a hundred dollars an hour is two thousand dollars, and its chairman does most of the work before it begins; more than eight people and it cannot decide. Decisions themselves follow a model of free discussion, a clear decision and full support, where support means commitment rather than agreement. Grove's diagnosis of why groups fail to decide is the peer-group syndrome, peers circling for fear of going against the group until a senior person, the peer plus one, gives them the confidence to conclude; his remedy for surprise vetoes is six questions settled in advance, what is being decided, when, by whom, with whom consulted, who ratifies, and who is told. Planning is the same three steps as the factory: what the environment will demand, what the work already under way will deliver, and what to do to close the gap. Its output is not the bound plan but the tasks it causes, and management by objectives is the same process applied to the quarter, with few objectives and dated key results, set high enough that even hard effort has only even odds.

A team of teams

When the breakfast factory franchises nationally, every question of centralisation gets a different answer: eggs are bought regionally, machinery centrally, wages locally, tableware centrally with regional warehouses. Grove generalises. Every organisation can be drawn as wholly mission-oriented, each unit doing everything for itself, or wholly functional, each function serving every unit; the first has one advantage, responsiveness, and the second has all the others, economies of scale, re-allocable resources and the leverage of specialists. Every large organisation with a common business purpose ends up as a hybrid, a claim he calls Grove's law, and the task of the hybrid is allocating shared resources and settling the conflicts that allocation creates. That cannot be done at the top, which he illustrates from the Hungarian central planners of his youth who could not get high-contrast film to shops in winter, so it falls to middle managers through dual reporting. The founding case at Intel was plant security: the corporate security manager knew the standards, the plant manager knew whether the guards turned up, and the guards reported to both. As careers progress, a general manager comes to supervise functions he does not know, so technical supervision must come from a functional chief or, as often, from a council of peers, which requires each member to surrender some individual decision-making. That surrender requires trust, and trust is not an organisational principle but a matter of culture.

Culture, in turn, is one of three modes of control, and Grove introduces them through a drive to work. Buying tyres, you act on self-interest and the market controls the transaction. Stopping at a red light, you honour a contract that a policeman monitors. Arriving at an accident, you forget both and help strangers at some risk. Most work cannot be priced by the piece, so the employment contract buys general authority to monitor and correct; where the environment changes faster than rules can be written, only shared values will do. Which mode fits depends on two variables, whether the person's motivation runs to self-interest or group interest, and how complex, uncertain and ambiguous the environment is, a composite Grove calls the CUA factor. Self-interest in a simple environment is the market's domain; group interest in a simple one is the contract's; group interest in a complex one is culture's; and self-interest in a complex environment admits no control at all, which is why a senior outside hire brought in to fix a troubled unit so often fails. New employees should start in low-CUA jobs and be promoted into ambiguity as shared experience teaches them the values.

The players

A person who is not doing the job either cannot or will not, and Grove's test is whether they could do it if their life depended on it. That yields the manager's two levers, training for capability and motivation for the will, and the last part of the book takes them in turn. Motivation cannot be done to someone; it comes from within, and a manager can only build an environment in which motivated people flourish, judged by performance and not by anyone's report of feeling motivated. Grove takes Maslow's hierarchy because his observation of working life confirms it: physiological, safety and social needs get people to show up, esteem and self-actualisation make them perform, and only the last is limitless, whether it is driven by competence, the violinist who keeps practising, or by achievement, the ring-tosser who stands just far enough from the peg to make it hard. Money is a necessity low in the hierarchy and a measure high in it, and the test is whether a person cares about the absolute size of a raise or its size relative to others'. Fear, at the upper levels, becomes fear of failure, which makes achievers conservative, so objectives are set where even hard effort has a fifty-fifty chance. The sports analogy follows. People who are indifferent at the office run marathons to beat a clock, so give work the character of a competitive sport, with rules and a racetrack. Intel's facilities upkeep improved almost at once when a senior manager in each building began scoring it and the scores were compared; nothing else changed. The manager's role in this is the coach, who takes no credit, is tough on the team, and once played the game.

There is no best management style. Intel rotated managers between similar groups and found that neither the managers nor the groups kept their high or low output; results came from particular pairings. The variable that decides is the subordinate's task-relevant maturity, a combination of achievement orientation, readiness to take responsibility, education, training and experience, all specific to this task in this environment. A star sales manager moved into a plant became overwhelmed, because his general maturity had not changed and his task-relevant maturity was near zero, and Grove notes that this should have been predictable. When maturity is low the effective style is structured, telling the subordinate what, when and how; at medium it is individual-oriented, two-way communication and support; at high it is minimal, agreeing objectives and monitoring. None of these is nicer than another; they are effective or not. Structure moves over time from being imposed to being internal, as it does between a parent and a growing child, and the progression depends on shared operational values, which the supervisor is responsible for transmitting. A subordinate's mistakes are paid for by the organisation's customers, so letting him make his own is not teaching. Maturity can also fall: the sergeant of a quiet camp who reverts to barking orders when a jeep of the enemy comes over the hill is doing the right thing, and the mode managers most dislike is the one they most often reach for too late.

Managers misjudge their own style. Asked to rate their supervisors, subordinates described far more structure than the supervisors themselves reported; about nine in ten supervisors saw themselves as more communicating or delegating than their people did. Friendship helps the communicating style and makes the structured one harder to return to, and Grove's test is to imagine delivering a tough review to the friend.

The review is the most important form of feedback a manager gives and, he says, among the things managers do worst. Its purpose is to improve performance, first by finding missing skills and second by raising motivation at the same skill level. Assessing means deciding in advance what was expected, weighing output measures against internal measures whose proper ratio can shift month by month, and allowing for the time offset between a manager's work and the results it produces. Grove's own error was a manager whose superb year was the light of distant stars, work done years before, and whose collapse the following year came as his real performance improved. The trap he names is potential: a manager's rating cannot be higher than his organisation's, whatever his form, or the company learns that acting like a manager is what counts. Delivering means levelling, listening with every sense until the message has arrived, and leaving oneself out. A review should carry the few messages a person can absorb, not everything the reviewer has observed. In a review of a serious problem the subordinate moves from ignoring to denying to blaming others to accepting responsibility, and the step from blame to responsibility is emotional while the step to a solution is merely intellectual; any outcome that includes a commitment to act is acceptable, and agreement is not required. Managers write their best reviews for their stars and their most useful ones for their weak performers, which Grove thinks is backwards, since improving a star is the higher-leverage act.

The remaining chapters cover hiring, where the interviewer must for once judge potential and can only improve the odds; the resignation, which must be dealt with the moment it is announced; and compensation as feedback, with a performance bonus rising as a share of pay and the honest observation that merit pay requires someone to come last. Grove's answer to the Peter principle is that achievers alternate between meeting and exceeding requirements as they are promoted, and that a person promoted too far should be openly returned to the job he did well rather than pushed out. The book ends on training, which Grove insists is the manager's own job and one of the highest-leverage things a manager can do. The arithmetic is four lectures, three hours of preparation for each hour taught, twelve hours in all, for ten people who will work twenty thousand hours in the coming year; a one per cent improvement returns two hundred hours. Training must be tied to how things are actually done, must be a process and not an event, and must be given by someone the students see as a practising authority, which a hired trainer cannot be.

Since 1983

The book was reissued in 1995 with an introduction in which Grove described what had changed: the Japanese memory makers who forced Intel out of the business it was founded on, the arrival of e-mail, and a workplace that had become faster and less kind, in which every employee competes with anyone anywhere who can do the same job and owns their career as a sole proprietor. The three basic ideas, he wrote, had not changed. It was reissued again in 2015, when a generation of founders who had been handed it by their investors made it the most recommended management book in the technology industry, and its management by objectives, taught by Grove to John Doerr at Intel, became the objectives and key results system used across that industry. Its debts and its dated parts are both visible now. Task-relevant maturity is Hersey and Blanchard's situational leadership with the name changed; Maslow's hierarchy, adopted because it matched Grove's observation, has not held up as psychology, though the distinction between needs that get people to work and needs that make them perform survives without it. Forced comparative ranking has been abandoned by most of the companies that adopted it. The book assumes a shared building, a long career in one firm and a manager who is always he. What has lasted is the frame: output over activity, leverage as the measure of a manager's time, the pairing of indicators, monitoring as the line between delegating and abdicating, the meeting as a medium, and the rule that the style of supervision is set by the person's maturity in this task and not by the manager's preference.

A group of equals stalls until someone senior joins the decision

Grove found that a meeting of people at the same level goes round in circles without settling anything until a senior person steps in. Stepping in helps at the moment of deciding and harms during the discussion.

Becauseobserved at Intel and offered as a remedy; the timing rule is a judgement about when authority helps, never tested against a control.
LimitsThe same senior presence that unlocks a decision is what Edmondson's managers responded to by withholding corrections when the boss's boss was present. Grove's rule says which stage helps but gives no way to tell, inside the room, which stage you are at.
PracticePut a senior person in the room for the decision and keep them out of the discussion, and settle the six questions — what, when, by whom, consulted, ratified, told — before the meeting starts.
After AIA model in the discussion is a peer with no standing, so it does not break the circling. Asked to arbitrate, it agrees with whoever framed the question, which is the peer-group syndrome with a more confident voice.

A proven worker needs close direction again when the job changes

How ready someone is to work unsupervised applies to one task, not to the person. When the task or the pace changes, that readiness drops, and a manager who had stepped back has to go back to close, structured direction.

Becausetwo illustrative cases, offered as something that should have been predictable, with nothing measured.
LimitsGrove notes that the mode managers most dislike is the one they most often reach for too late, and that friendship makes the return to structure harder. The evidence is two anecdotes chosen to make the point.
PracticeKeep the structured mode available instead of treating a move to minimal involvement as permanent, and keep enough hands-on contact to use it. Manual flying hours is what makes the return possible rather than merely intended.
After AIA model's competence shifts with releases and with what is in its context, so the fall in maturity is not announced by a visible change of job. The manager has to detect it from the output, which is the thing the delegation was meant to stop requiring.

Check delegated work at the rough draft, not the finished version

Grove argues that you should check work you have handed over at its cheapest stage, the rough draft rather than the polished report, because that is where a mistake costs least to catch.

Becausecarried over by analogy from factory inspection, and the London embassy case shows what sampling saves rather than what it catches.
LimitsThe gate is right where a defect is catastrophic rather than merely expensive, and Grove says so. A rough draft from a delegate whose competence is unknown also carries less information than a finished one.
PracticeAsk for the outline before the document and the plan before the diff, and spend the review budget there. Failure-mode review turns that early look into a fixed list of what to look for.
After AIA model produces a polished draft at the first stage, so the cheap early signal Grove relies on no longer exists in the work's natural shape. The low-value stage has to be created deliberately — an outline, a plan, a stated approach — before there is anything to monitor.
opinionManagerial leverage (the output equation, figure 26.4)

Grove counts a manager's output as what his teams produce

Andrew Grove defines a manager's output as the output of the teams he supervises or influences. So an activity that raises nobody else's output is worth nothing, however well it is done.

Becauseit is a definition Grove derived from running Intel, not a finding anyone tested.
LimitsIt is an accounting identity rather than a measurement: it gives no way to attribute an output among several influences, and the time offset between a manager's work and its result can be years. Grove's 1995 introduction concedes that the shared building and the long career the model assumes have gone.
PracticeStop counting hours spent and start asking which of them raised someone else's output. The stays-human list is where the answer gets written down for the firm rather than for one manager.
After AIWhen part of the unit is a model, the output still counts as the manager's, but the leverage term now includes work nobody on the team did. Reviewing a draft the model wrote is activity that raises no one's output unless it raises the next draft.

Grove rates no manager higher than the team's actual result

Andrew Grove holds that a manager's performance rating must never be higher than the result his organisation achieved, however well he personally performed. Otherwise the company learns that acting like a manager is what counts.

Becausea rule Grove adopted after misrating a manager whose superb year was work done years earlier; one case, stated as policy.
LimitsThe time offset Grove names cuts both ways: this year's result may be the light of distant stars in either direction. Rating on the organisation's result alone punishes sound judgement that met bad luck, which is Edmondson's objection to rewarding outcomes.
PracticeRate the result, then ask separately what part of it was this year's work — and write the prediction down first so the offset is visible. Calibration log is that habit applied to any judgement you will later rely on.
After AIThe offset has shortened — a team's output can move within a quarter of adopting a tool — so a rating tracks the tool's arrival rather than the manager's work, and the distant-stars error runs in reverse.

Grove says every measure needs a second measure beside it

Grove argues that any number a team is pushed to improve needs a second number beside it that catches the damage. Cut inventory without counting shortages and the shortages rise unseen.

Becausea rule of thumb from running a factory; the examples — inventory against shortages, vouchers processed against errors found — show the failure mode without saying how often it occurs.
LimitsPairing assumes the counter-effect can be named in advance, which is exactly what is hard about a new measure. Self-determination evidence adds that the pair is itself a contingency, and contingent measurement shifts regulation from autonomous to controlled whatever it counts.
PracticeNever introduce a measure without naming, in the same sentence, the thing it will be gamed against. Failure-mode review supplies the counter-measure for delegated work: what got through, not how much got produced.
After AIOutput generated per person is the easiest thing to count and the counter-indicator — defects reaching production, hours spent verifying work nobody wrote — is the hard one. Without the pair, what gets reported is the gain people already believed they were getting.

Grove says managers should teach their own people, not hire trainers

Andy Grove argues that a manager should run the training himself instead of hiring a trainer. Only someone the team sees as doing the work day to day can tie the lessons to how the job is actually done.

Becausethe arithmetic establishes leverage — twelve hours against ten people's twenty thousand working hours — and the authority claim is asserted from experience.
LimitsWeinberg's own classroom result cuts against the emphasis on the teacher: lecturing on the errors his students made moved them from 87 per cent to 83, and the exercises did the rest. The one per cent gain in his arithmetic is never measured.
PracticeTeach the thing yourself, as a series rather than an event, using this week's work as the material. Dreyfus mapping and tutor-only rule decides who needs which part.
After AIWhat a manager now owes his team is training in how to use and check a model, the one domain where he is least likely to be a practising authority. The arithmetic still holds and the authority does not.

Grove says shared values are the only control when work is uncertain

Grove argues that where work is complicated, uncertain and unclear, the only thing that keeps people acting well is a set of values they share. Rules and contracts cannot be written fast enough to cover what comes up.

Becausea two-by-two derived from a commuting anecdote; the empty quadrant is an inference about why senior outside hires fail, not a count of them.
LimitsCulture needs shared experience to transmit, which is why Grove starts new employees in low-CUA jobs; where turnover is fast there is no time to build it. For the fourth quadrant — self-interest in a complex environment — his own answer is that no control works at all.
PracticeWhere the work outruns the rules, spend the effort on what the group will not do and why, rather than on another procedure. The stays-human list is that statement in its shortest form.
After AIAn agent has no shared values to fall back on when the contract runs out. What it has is a specification, which is the mode Grove says fails in exactly this quadrant, and that is the alignment problem stated in management vocabulary.

Grove sets targets a person reaches only half the time

Grove says goals should be set so that even someone working hard reaches them about half the time. High achievers left to themselves pick targets they are already certain of, because at that level the fear is of failing.

Becausethe rule rests on Maslow's hierarchy, which Grove adopted because his observation of working life confirmed it and which has not held up as psychology, though the distinction between needs that get people to work and needs that make them perform survives without it.
LimitsSelf-determination evidence says a target set for someone rather than with them shifts regulation to controlled whatever its difficulty. A fifty-fifty objective inside a company that rates on attainment is the potential trap Grove names two chapters later.
PracticeSet the target where failure is a real possibility, and then make sure nothing in the rating system punishes the person for taking it.
After AIWhen the delegate is a model, failure costs it nothing, so the even-odds rule loses its point for the agent and keeps it for the person who has to review the half that fails.

How closely to supervise depends on the person's experience at that task

Grove says how closely a manager should supervise is set by how experienced the person is at that particular task, not by what the manager prefers. None of the three styles is kinder than the others.

BecauseIntel's rotation of managers between similar groups was suggestive rather than controlled, and the construct is Hersey and Blanchard's situational leadership renamed.
LimitsMaturity is specific to the task and falls when the job or the pace changes. Managers also misjudge their own position on the dial: about nine in ten saw themselves as more delegating than their people did.
PracticeSet the dial per task rather than per person, and write the mapping down so it can be argued with. Dreyfus mapping and tutor-only rule does exactly that.
After AIA model's competence is jagged across tasks and can move with a release, so the dial cannot be carried per delegate the way Grove carries it per person. It has to be reset for each task, and reset again when the version changes.

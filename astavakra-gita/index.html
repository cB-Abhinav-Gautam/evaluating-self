64अष्टावक्रगीता · Aṣṭāvakra Gītā

anonymous, c. 500–800 · translations by Swami Nityaswarupananda, Advaita Ashrama, 1940, and Thomas Byrom, The Heart of Awareness, Shambhala, 1990

Linked to 20 chapters: supports 10 · contradicts 12 · mechanism 1 · same claim 5 · pairing 2

Janaka, king of Mithilā, opens with three questions: how is knowledge got, how does liberation come, how is dispassion reached. The sage Aṣṭāvakra answers all three by denying that anything is got at all. You are not earth, water, fire, air or space, he says; you are the witness of these and awareness itself, and if you set the body aside and rest in awareness you are free this moment, not later. Bondage is nothing but the thought of bondage: one who holds himself free is free, one who holds himself bound stays bound. Desire is not an obstacle on the way to the world, it is the world, so there is nothing to renounce except wanting. And since you never acted, there is no practice that could make you free, and the attempt to meditate your way there is itself named as a chain. Two hundred and ninety-eight verses in twenty chapters hold that position without once retreating from it. The text is a dialogue only in form. Janaka asks in the first verse and then, from the second chapter on, speaks as a man who has already understood, so that the book is less an argument than a pair of voices saying the same thing from the two ends of it — the teacher who states the case and the pupil who reports what the case feels like from inside. The Sanskrit is plain, the metre the ordinary śloka of the epics, and the same half-dozen images come back so often that they work as a vocabulary: rope and snake, shell and silver, ocean and wave, gold and bangle, clay and pot, a jar of space inside the space outside it. Nityaswarupananda's word-by-word edition is the one to read for the literal sense and the commentary; Byrom's free verse is the one to read for the tone, which is nearer to song than to argument. The renderings below are made from the Sanskrit and are meant to be plain.

The text names no author and gives no date. Aṣṭāvakra is a character borrowed from the forest book of the Mahābhārata, where a boy corrects his father's recitation of the Vedas from inside the womb, is cursed to be born bent in eight places, and at twelve walks into Janaka's court to defeat the scholar Bandin who had drowned his father in debate. The name means eight bends. Brockington, who revised Byrom's translation, notes that this etymological legend explains a name that otherwise has no explanation, and that nothing in the later stories about Aṣṭāvakra helps at all with his role here. What the borrowing does is set the scene: a contest about brahman, at a court, between a king and a boy whose body is a joke. The moral the stories carry, that the most misshapen form is still filled with the same light, is the text's own claim in narrative form.

The three questions

Chapter 1 is the only sustained instruction in the book. Aṣṭāvakra speaks nineteen verses and does not speak at length again until the fifteenth chapter.

कथं ज्ञानमवाप्नोति कथं मुक्तिर्भविष्यति ।
वैराग्यं च कथं प्राप्तमेतद् ब्रूहि मम प्रभो ॥ १ ॥
kathaṃ jñānamavāpnoti kathaṃ muktirbhaviṣyati
vairāgyaṃ ca kathaṃ prāptametad brūhi mama prabho

1.1How is knowledge got? How does liberation come? How is dispassion reached? Tell me this, my lord.

मुक्तिमिच्छसि चेत्तात विषयान् विषवत्त्यज ।
क्षमार्जवदयातोषसत्यं पीयूषवद् भज ॥ २ ॥
muktimicchasi cettāta viṣayān viṣavattyaja
kṣamārjavadayātoṣasatyaṃ pīyūṣavad bhaja

1.2If you want to be free, child, shun what the senses bring as poison, and take forbearance, honesty, kindness, contentment and truth as nectar.

न पृथ्वी न जलं नाग्निर्न वायुर्द्यौर्न वा भवान् ।
एषां साक्षिणमात्मानं चिद्रूपं विद्धि मुक्तये ॥ ३ ॥
na pṛthvī na jalaṃ nāgnirna vāyurdyaurna vā bhavān
eṣāṃ sākṣiṇamātmānaṃ cidrūpaṃ viddhi muktaye

1.3You are not earth, not water, not fire, not air, not space. Know yourself as the witness of these and as awareness itself, and be free.

यदि देहं पृथक् कृत्य चिति विश्राम्य तिष्ठसि ।
अधुनैव सुखी शान्तो बन्धमुक्तो भविष्यसि ॥ ४ ॥
yadi dehaṃ pṛthak kṛtya citi viśrāmya tiṣṭhasi
adhunaiva sukhī śānto bandhamukto bhaviṣyasi

1.4If you hold the body apart and rest in awareness, you will be happy, at peace and out of bondage this moment.

The second verse is the one concession to ordinary discipline in the whole text, and Byrom records that some commentators think it spurious for that reason. It does not read as an interruption so much as a floor: the poet is willing to name the virtues once, and then never again. By the third verse the method is already in place, and it is subtraction. The five elements make the body; the body is seen; what is seen is not the seer. Nityaswarupananda's note puts the consequence plainly, that we identify the self with the elements and become miserable, and that the self is the witness and eternally distinct from what it witnesses. The fourth verse adds the word the poet returns to more than any other: adhunā, now. Not after a life of practice, not at death, but at once.

न त्वं विप्रादिको वर्णो नाश्रमी नाक्षगोचरः ।
असङ्गोऽसि निराकारो विश्वसाक्षी सुखी भव ॥ ५ ॥
na tvaṃ viprādiko varṇo nāśramī nākṣagocaraḥ
asaṅgo'si nirākāro viśvasākṣī sukhī bhava

1.5You are of no caste and no stage of life, and no eye can find you. Unattached, without form, the witness of everything: be happy.

धर्माधर्मौ सुखं दुःखं मानसानि न ते विभो ।
न कर्तासि न भोक्तासि मुक्त एवासि सर्वदा ॥ ६ ॥
dharmādharmau sukhaṃ duḥkhaṃ mānasāni na te vibho
na kartāsi na bhoktāsi mukta evāsi sarvadā

1.6Right and wrong, pleasure and pain, are of the mind and not of you, who are everywhere. You neither act nor enjoy. You have always been free.

एको द्रष्टासि सर्वस्य मुक्तप्रायोऽसि सर्वदा ।
अयमेव हि ते बन्धो द्रष्टारं पश्यसीतरम् ॥ ७ ॥
eko draṣṭāsi sarvasya muktaprāyo'si sarvadā
ayameva hi te bandho draṣṭāraṃ paśyasītaram

1.7You are the one seer of everything and free always. This alone is your bondage, that you take the seer for something else.

अहं कर्तेत्यहंमानमहाकृष्णाहिदंशितः ।
नाहं कर्तेति विश्वासामृतं पीत्वा सुखं चर ॥ ८ ॥
ahaṃ kartetyahaṃmānamahākṛṣṇāhidaṃśitaḥ
nāhaṃ karteti viśvāsāmṛtaṃ pītvā sukhaṃ cara

1.8Bitten by the great black snake of thinking I am the doer, drink the nectar of the trust I am not the doer, and be happy.

Caste and the four stages of life go in the fifth verse, which is not a social claim but a consequence: whatever cannot be predicated of the witness carries no duty with it. The sixth removes merit and sin along with pleasure and pain, and the seventh names the single error the book will attack for three hundred verses — taking the seer to be one of the things seen. The eighth gives it an image. Egoism is a black snake, and its venom is the sentence ahaṃ kartā, I am the doer; the antidote is not an action but a conviction, nāhaṃ kartā, I am not the doer.

एको विशुद्धबोधोऽहमिति निश्चयवह्निना ।
प्रज्वाल्याज्ञानगहनं वीतशोकः सुखी भव ॥ ९ ॥
eko viśuddhabodho'hamiti niścayavahninā
prajvālyājñānagahanaṃ vītaśokaḥ sukhī bhava

1.9Set fire to the thicket of ignorance with the certainty that I am one and unstained awareness; be rid of grief and be happy.

यत्र विश्वमिदं भाति कल्पितं रज्जुसर्पवत् ।
आनन्दपरमानन्दः स बोधस्त्वं सुखं भव ॥ १० ॥
yatra viśvamidaṃ bhāti kalpitaṃ rajjusarpavat
ānandaparamānandaḥ sa bodhastvaṃ sukhaṃ bhava

1.10Here, where this world appears, imagined as a snake is imagined in a rope, there is joy and the height of joy. You are that awareness. Be happy.

मुक्ताभिमानी मुक्तो हि बद्धो बद्धाभिमान्यपि ।
किंवदन्तीह सत्येयं या मतिः सा गतिर्भवेत् ॥ ११ ॥
muktābhimānī mukto hi baddho baddhābhimānyapi
kiṃvadantīha satyeyaṃ yā matiḥ sā gatirbhavet

1.11One who holds himself free is free; one who holds himself bound stays bound. The old saying is true here: as the thought, so the way.

आत्मा साक्षी विभुः पूर्ण एको मुक्तश्चिदक्रियः ।
असङ्गो निःस्पृहः शान्तो भ्रमात्संसारवानिव ॥ १२ ॥
ātmā sākṣī vibhuḥ pūrṇa eko muktaścidakriyaḥ
asaṅgo niḥspṛhaḥ śānto bhramātsaṃsāravāniva

1.12The self is witness, everywhere, whole, one, free, aware, without action, unattached, without longing, still. Through a mistake it seems to be caught in the world.

The rope and the snake enter at the tenth verse and never leave. The figure is older than this text and standard in Advaita: a coil of rope in bad light is taken for a snake, and the fear is real while it lasts, but the rope never became a snake and nothing has to be done to it when the snake goes. The eleventh verse is the most quoted line in the book and the one Nityaswarupananda calls the formula of escape. It is not a piece of encouragement. It is the claim that bondage has no cause outside the belief in it, which is why the text can treat the belief as the only thing to be removed.

कूटस्थं बोधमद्वैतमात्मानं परिभावय ।
आभासोऽहं भ्रमं मुक्त्वा भावं बाह्यमथान्तरम् ॥ १३ ॥
kūṭasthaṃ bodhamadvaitamātmānaṃ paribhāvaya
ābhāso'haṃ bhramaṃ muktvā bhāvaṃ bāhyamathāntaram

1.13Meditate on the self as unmoving, aware, without a second, dropping the delusion I am the reflection and every notion outer and inner.

देहाभिमानपाशेन चिरं बद्धोऽसि पुत्रक ।
बोधोऽहं ज्ञानखड्गेन तन्निकृत्य सुखी भव ॥ १४ ॥
dehābhimānapāśena ciraṃ baddho'si putraka
bodho'haṃ jñānakhaḍgena tannikṛtya sukhī bhava

1.14You have been tied a long while, my son, by the rope of taking yourself for the body. Cut it with the sword of the knowledge I am awareness, and be happy.

निःसङ्गो निष्क्रियोऽसि त्वं स्वप्रकाशो निरञ्जनः ।
अयमेव हि ते बन्धः समाधिमनुतिष्ठसि ॥ १५ ॥
niḥsaṅgo niṣkriyo'si tvaṃ svaprakāśo nirañjanaḥ
ayameva hi te bandhaḥ samādhimanutiṣṭhasi

1.15You are unattached, without action, shining of yourself, and unstained. This alone is your bondage, that you sit down to meditate.

त्वया व्याप्तमिदं विश्वं त्वयि प्रोतं यथार्थतः ।
शुद्धबुद्धस्वरूपस्त्वं मा गमः क्षुद्रचित्तताम् ॥ १६ ॥
tvayā vyāptamidaṃ viśvaṃ tvayi protaṃ yathārthataḥ
śuddhabuddhasvarūpastvaṃ mā gamaḥ kṣudracittatām

1.16You pervade this world and this world is strung upon you. By nature you are pure awareness. Do not shrink into small-mindedness.

Two verses apart the poet tells Janaka to meditate on the self and then tells him that meditating is his bondage. The contradiction is not an oversight and it is not resolved anywhere in the text; it is the shape of the whole book. Verse 13 uses paribhāvaya, turn it over, dwell on it, which is a movement of understanding; verse 15 attacks samādhi, the practice of absorption, on the ground that anyone who sits down to attain something has already granted that he lacks it. Nityaswarupananda's note is blunt: such attempts presuppose that we are bound, and while we go on thinking ourselves bound, freedom is impossible. The poet will return to this at least a dozen times, and it is the position for which the book is either loved or thought irresponsible.

निरपेक्षो निर्विकारो निर्भरः शीतलाशयः ।
अगाधबुद्धिरक्षुब्धो भव चिन्मात्रवासनः ॥ १७ ॥
nirapekṣo nirvikāro nirbharaḥ śītalāśayaḥ
agādhabuddhirakṣubdho bhava cinmātravāsanaḥ

1.17You depend on nothing, alter in nothing, carry no weight, are cool in disposition, unfathomable in understanding and unshaken. Want awareness and nothing else.

साकारमनृतं विद्धि निराकारं तु निश्चलम् ।
एतत्तत्त्वोपदेशेन न पुनर्भवसम्भवः ॥ १८ ॥
sākāramanṛtaṃ viddhi nirākāraṃ tu niścalam
etattattvopadeśena na punarbhavasambhavaḥ

1.18Know that what has form is untrue and the formless does not change. Taught this truth, you will not be born again.

यथैवादर्शमध्यस्थे रूपेऽन्तः परितस्तु सः ।
तथैवाऽस्मिन् शरीरेऽन्तः परितः परमेश्वरः ॥ १९ ॥
yathaivādarśamadhyasthe rūpe'ntaḥ paritastu saḥ
tathaivā'smin śarīre'ntaḥ paritaḥ parameśvaraḥ

1.19As a mirror is both within the image it holds and outside it, so the lord is within this body and outside it.

एकं सर्वगतं व्योम बहिरन्तर्यथा घटे ।
नित्यं निरन्तरं ब्रह्म सर्वभूतगणे तथा ॥ २० ॥
ekaṃ sarvagataṃ vyoma bahirantaryathā ghaṭe
nityaṃ nirantaraṃ brahma sarvabhūtagaṇe tathā

1.20As one and the same space is inside a jar and outside it, so the everlasting and all-pervading brahman is in all things.

The chapter closes on two images that do different work. The mirror holds an appearance that occupies no space and leaves no mark, and Nityaswarupananda reads it as superimposition. The jar is the other move: the space inside a jar is not a portion of space, and nothing is done to it when the jar breaks. Byrom notes that readers have long disagreed about which of the two the nineteenth verse intends, and prefers the reading that makes it a statement of pervasion rather than of illusion. The text is content to leave both standing.

Adoration to myself

The second chapter is Janaka's, twenty-five verses of it, and nothing in the text prepares for the speed of the turn. He was asking how knowledge is got; one chapter later he is describing what it is like to have it.

अहो निरञ्जनः शान्तो बोधोऽहं प्रकृतेः परः ।
एतावन्तमहं कालं मोहेनैव विडम्बितः ॥ १ ॥
aho nirañjanaḥ śānto bodho'haṃ prakṛteḥ paraḥ
etāvantamahaṃ kālaṃ mohenaiva viḍambitaḥ

2.1I am unstained, still, awareness beyond nature. All this time I was cheated by illusion.

यथा प्रकाशयाम्येको देहमेनं तथा जगत् ।
अतो मम जगत्सर्वमथवा न च किञ्चन ॥ २ ॥
yathā prakāśayāmyeko dehamenaṃ tathā jagat
ato mama jagatsarvamathavā na ca kiñcana

2.2As I alone light up this body, so I light up the world. Either the whole world is mine or nothing is.

स शरीरमहो विश्वं परित्यज्य मयाधुना ।
कुतश्चित् कौशलाद् एव परमात्मा विलोक्यते ॥ ३ ॥
sa śarīramaho viśvaṃ parityajya mayādhunā
kutaścit kauśalād eva paramātmā vilokyate

2.3Having let go of the world along with the body, by some skill I now see the supreme self.

Chapter 1 is addressed throughout to a second person: you are not earth, you are the witness, be happy. Chapter 2 says the same things in the first person and does not leave it again. Nothing has been added between the two, the predicates are the same words, and nothing is claimed to have happened except that a description has become a report. Janaka's first word is aho, an exclamation that runs through the chapter, and his verb for the years before it is viḍambita, made a fool of rather than punished.

यथा न तोयतो भिन्नास्तरङ्गाः फेनबुद्बुदाः ।
आत्मनो न तथा भिन्नं विश्वमात्मविनिर्गतम् ॥ ४ ॥
yathā na toyato bhinnāstaraṅgāḥ phenabudbudāḥ
ātmano na tathā bhinnaṃ viśvamātmavinirgatam

2.4As wave and foam and bubble are not other than water, so the world that streams out of the self is not other than the self.

तन्तुमात्रो भवेद् एव पटो यद्वद् विचारितः ।
आत्मतन्मात्रमेवेदं तद्वद् विश्वं विचारितम् ॥ ५ ॥
tantumātro bhaved eva paṭo yadvad vicāritaḥ
ātmatanmātramevedaṃ tadvad viśvaṃ vicāritam

2.5Take cloth apart and it is thread only. Look closely at this world and it is the self only.

यथैवेक्षुरसे क्लृप्ता तेन व्याप्तैव शर्करा ।
तथा विश्वं मयि क्लृप्तं मया व्याप्तं निरन्तरम् ॥ ६ ॥
yathaivekṣurase klṛptā tena vyāptaiva śarkarā
tathā viśvaṃ mayi klṛptaṃ mayā vyāptaṃ nirantaram

2.6As the sugar made in the juice of the cane is soaked through by the juice, so the world made in me is soaked through by me.

The images arrive in a run, and they are not decoration. Each states the same relation: an appearance that has no material of its own borrows the material of what it appears in. Water, thread, juice, and later clay and gold, are all the same argument said five ways, and the modern term for it is the one Nityaswarupananda uses, material cause. Verse 3 contains the book's most modest word about how any of this happened. Aṣṭāvakra gave no method, and Janaka reports that it came kutaścit kauśalāt, by some skill or other, which Byrom renders as a gift and which is as close as the text ever comes to admitting that it cannot say how.

आत्माज्ञानाज्जगद्भाति आत्मज्ञानान्न भासते ।
रज्ज्वज्ञानादहिर्भाति तज्ज्ञानाद् भासते न हि ॥ ७ ॥
ātmājñānājjagadbhāti ātmajñānānna bhāsate
rajjvajñānādahirbhāti tajjñānād bhāsate na hi

2.7Not knowing the self, the world appears; knowing it, the world does not. Not seeing the rope, the snake appears; seeing it, the snake does not.

प्रकाशो मे निजं रूपं नातिरिक्तोऽस्म्यहं ततः ।
यदा प्रकाशते विश्वं तदाहं भास एव हि ॥ ८ ॥
prakāśo me nijaṃ rūpaṃ nātirikto'smyahaṃ tataḥ
yadā prakāśate viśvaṃ tadāhaṃ bhāsa eva hi

2.8Light is my nature and I am nothing else. When the world shows itself, it is I who am shining.

अहो विकल्पितं विश्वमज्ञानान्मयि भासते ।
रूप्यं शुक्तौ फणी रज्जौ वारि सूर्यकरे यथा ॥ ९ ॥
aho vikalpitaṃ viśvamajñānānmayi bhāsate
rūpyaṃ śuktau phaṇī rajjau vāri sūryakare yathā

2.9The world appears in me as a thing imagined, as silver in mother-of-pearl, a snake in a rope, water in the glare of the sun.

मत्तो विनिर्गतं विश्वं मय्येव लयमेष्यति ।
मृदि कुम्भो जले वीचिः कनके कटकं यथा ॥ १० ॥
matto vinirgataṃ viśvaṃ mayyeva layameṣyati
mṛdi kumbho jale vīciḥ kanake kaṭakaṃ yathā

2.10As a pot returns to clay, a wave to water, a bangle to gold, so the world that came out of me will dissolve into me.

अहो अहं नमो मह्यं विनाशो यस्य नास्ति मे ।
ब्रह्मादिस्तम्बपर्यन्तं जगन्नाशोऽपि तिष्ठतः ॥ ११ ॥
aho ahaṃ namo mahyaṃ vināśo yasya nāsti me
brahmādistambaparyantaṃ jagannāśo'pi tiṣṭhataḥ

2.11How fine I am. Adoration to myself, who do not decay, and who survive when the world perishes from Brahmā down to a tuft of grass.

अहो अहं नमो मह्यमेकोऽहं देहवानपि ।
क्वचिन्न गन्ता नागन्ता व्याप्य विश्वमवस्थितः ॥ १२ ॥
aho ahaṃ namo mahyameko'haṃ dehavānapi
kvacinna gantā nāgantā vyāpya viśvamavasthitaḥ

2.12How fine I am. Adoration to myself, who though I have a body am one, who go nowhere and come from nowhere, and who stand pervading the world.

अहो अहं नमो मह्यं दक्षो नास्तीह मत्समः ।
असंस्पृश्य शरीरेण येन विश्वं चिरं धृतम् ॥ १३ ॥
aho ahaṃ namo mahyaṃ dakṣo nāstīha matsamaḥ
asaṃspṛśya śarīreṇa yena viśvaṃ ciraṃ dhṛtam

2.13How fine I am. Adoration to myself. No one else is so able, for I have carried the world for ever without touching it with a body.

अहो अहं नमो मह्यं यस्य मे नास्ति किञ्चन ।
अथवा यस्य मे सर्वं यद् वाङ्मनसगोचरम् ॥ १४ ॥
aho ahaṃ namo mahyaṃ yasya me nāsti kiñcana
athavā yasya me sarvaṃ yad vāṅmanasagocaram

2.14How fine I am. Adoration to myself, who own nothing, or who own everything that speech and mind can reach.

Four verses of self-adoration in a row are the hardest passage in the book for a reader raised on the idea that humility is a virtue. Byrom is careful about it: this is spiritual rapture and austere discrimination speaking with one voice, and next to the litanies of conventional devotion it can sound inflated. It is not praise of Janaka. It is praise of what is common to Janaka and everything else, offered by the one place from which such praise is not a boast.

ज्ञानं ज्ञेयं तथा ज्ञाता त्रितयं नास्ति वास्तवम् ।
अज्ञानाद् भाति यत्रेदं सोऽहमस्मि निरञ्जनः ॥ १५ ॥
jñānaṃ jñeyaṃ tathā jñātā tritayaṃ nāsti vāstavam
ajñānād bhāti yatredaṃ so'hamasmi nirañjanaḥ

2.15Knowledge, the knower and the knowable do not exist. I am the stainless one in whom these three appear through not knowing.

द्वैतमूलमहो दुःखं नान्यत्तस्याऽस्ति भेषजम् ।
दृश्यमेतन् मृषा सर्वमेकोऽहं चिद्रसोमलः ॥ १६ ॥
dvaitamūlamaho duḥkhaṃ nānyattasyā'sti bheṣajam
dṛśyametan mṛṣā sarvameko'haṃ cidrasomalaḥ

2.16Duality is the root of misery. There is no remedy but to see that everything seen is untrue, and that I am one, pure, awareness and joy.

बोधमात्रोऽहमज्ञानाद् उपाधिः कल्पितो मया ।
एवं विमृशतो नित्यं निर्विकल्पे स्थितिर्मम ॥ १७ ॥
bodhamātro'hamajñānād upādhiḥ kalpito mayā
evaṃ vimṛśato nityaṃ nirvikalpe sthitirmama

2.17I am awareness only. Through ignorance I laid a limit on myself. Turning this over steadily, I rest where there are no divisions.

न मे बन्धोऽस्ति मोक्षो वा भ्रान्तिः शान्ता निराश्रया ।
अहो मयि स्थितं विश्वं वस्तुतो न मयि स्थितम् ॥ १८ ॥
na me bandho'sti mokṣo vā bhrāntiḥ śāntā nirāśrayā
aho mayi sthitaṃ viśvaṃ vastuto na mayi sthitam

2.18I have no bondage and no release. The illusion has lost its support and stopped. The world stands in me and does not in truth stand at all.

सशरीरमिदं विश्वं न किञ्चिदिति निश्चितम् ।
शुद्धचिन्मात्र आत्मा च तत्कस्मिन् कल्पनाधुना ॥ १९ ॥
saśarīramidaṃ viśvaṃ na kiñciditi niścitam
śuddhacinmātra ātmā ca tatkasmin kalpanādhunā

2.19I am sure now that body and world are nothing and the self is awareness only. On what can anything now be imagined?

शरीरं स्वर्गनरकौ बन्धमोक्षौ भयं तथा ।
कल्पनामात्रमेवैतत् किं मे कार्यं चिदात्मनः ॥ २० ॥
śarīraṃ svarganarakau bandhamokṣau bhayaṃ tathā
kalpanāmātramevaitat kiṃ me kāryaṃ cidātmanaḥ

2.20Body, heaven and hell, bondage and release, and fear as well, are all imagining. What have I to do with them, whose nature is awareness?

The fifteenth verse takes apart the arrangement that every theory of knowledge depends on: knower, known, and the act of knowing. Nityaswarupananda's note is exact about why this matters — we take ourselves to be the first of the three, and the position of the text is that we are not a member of the triad but the ground it appears on. The sixteenth names duality itself as the disease, which is the reason the book keeps refusing remedies: a remedy is a second thing.

अहो जनसमूहेऽपि न द्वैतं पश्यतो मम ।
अरण्यमिव संवृत्तं क्व रतिं करवाण्यहम् ॥ २१ ॥
aho janasamūhe'pi na dvaitaṃ paśyato mama
araṇyamiva saṃvṛttaṃ kva ratiṃ karavāṇyaham

2.21I do not find two anywhere. A crowd of people has become a wilderness to me. To what should I attach myself?

नाहं देहो न मे देहो जीवो नाहमहं हि चित् ।
अयमेव हि मे बन्ध आसीद्या जीविते स्पृहा ॥ २२ ॥
nāhaṃ deho na me deho jīvo nāhamahaṃ hi cit
ayameva hi me bandha āsīdyā jīvite spṛhā

2.22I am not the body, nor is the body mine. I am not a separate creature. I am awareness. My bondage was the thirst to go on living.

अहो भुवनकल्लोलैर्विचित्रैर्द्राक् समुत्थितम् ।
मय्यनन्तमहाम्भोधौ चित्तवाते समुद्यते ॥ २३ ॥
aho bhuvanakallolairvicitrairdrāk samutthitam
mayyanantamahāmbhodhau cittavāte samudyate

2.23In me, the shoreless ocean, the wind of the mind rises and at once the many worlds are made, like waves.

मय्यनन्तमहाम्भोधौ चित्तवाते प्रशाम्यति ।
अभाग्याज्जीववणिजो जगत्पोतो विनश्वरः ॥ २४ ॥
mayyanantamahāmbhodhau cittavāte praśāmyati
abhāgyājjīvavaṇijo jagatpoto vinaśvaraḥ

2.24In me, the shoreless ocean, when the wind of the mind falls, the trader who is the separate self and his ship the world go down.

मय्यनन्तमहाम्भोधावाश्चर्यं जीववीचयः ।
उद्यन्ति घ्नन्ति खेलन्ति प्रविशन्ति स्वभावतः ॥ २५ ॥
mayyanantamahāmbhodhāvāścaryaṃ jīvavīcayaḥ
udyanti ghnanti khelanti praviśanti svabhāvataḥ

2.25How fine it is. In me, the shoreless ocean, the waves that are separate selves rise, strike one another, play a while, and go, each after its own kind.

The wilderness of verse 21 is not loneliness. Nityaswarupananda glosses it as the aloneness of one who is in a crowd and conscious only of the one. The ocean enters here and will carry the rest of the book: the mind is a wind, worlds are waves, the separate self is a merchant whose ship is the world, and his cargo is his own action. Byrom points out that the ocean and the wave may have come to Advaita from Aśvaghoṣa, an early Mahāyāna teacher, and that the same figure recurs in chapters 6, 7 and 15.

The test, and the four ways

Aṣṭāvakra's reply to all this is to doubt it. Chapters 3 and 4 test the pupil; 5 to 8 give him four ways of dissolving the world and then a fifth position from which none of them is needed.

Chapter 3: the anomalies

अविनाशिनमात्मानमेकं विज्ञाय तत्त्वतः ।
तवात्मज्ञानस्य धीरस्य कथमर्थार्जने रतिः ॥ १ ॥
avināśinamātmānamekaṃ vijñāya tattvataḥ
tavātmajñānasya dhīrasya kathamarthārjane ratiḥ

3.1Knowing yourself as truly indestructible and one, how is it that you, serene and a knower of the self, are attached to getting money?

आत्माज्ञानादहो प्रीतिर्विषयभ्रमगोचरे ।
शुक्तेरज्ञानतो लोभो यथा रजतविभ्रमे ॥ २ ॥
ātmājñānādaho prītirviṣayabhramagocare
śukterajñānato lobho yathā rajatavibhrame

3.2Just as greed arises from the illusion of silver caused by not recognising the shell, so attachment arises to what is illusory from not knowing the self.

विश्वं स्फुरति यत्रेदं तरङ्गा इव सागरे ।
सोऽहमस्मीति विज्ञाय किं दीन इव धावसि ॥ ३ ॥
viśvaṃ sphurati yatredaṃ taraṅgā iva sāgare
so'hamasmīti vijñāya kiṃ dīna iva dhāvasi

3.3Knowing yourself as that in which the world rises like waves on the sea, why do you run about like a wretch?

श्रुत्वापि शुद्धचैतन्य आत्मानमतिसुन्दरम् ।
उपस्थेऽत्यन्तसंसक्तो मालिन्यमधिगच्छति ॥ ४ ॥
śrutvāpi śuddhacaitanya ātmānamatisundaram
upasthe'tyantasaṃsakto mālinyamadhigacchati

3.4Having heard that you are awareness and past all beauty, how can a man still be dirtied by running after lust?

सर्वभूतेषु चात्मानं सर्वभूतानि चात्मनि ।
मुनेर्जानत आश्चर्यं ममत्वमनुवर्तते ॥ ५ ॥
sarvabhūteṣu cātmānaṃ sarvabhūtāni cātmani
munerjānata āścaryaṃ mamatvamanuvartate

3.5It is strange that the sense of ownership persists in a sage who has known the self in all and all in the self.

आस्थितः परमाद्वैतं मोक्षार्थेऽपि व्यवस्थितः ।
आश्चर्यं कामवशगो विकलः केलिशिक्षया ॥ ६ ॥
āsthitaḥ paramādvaitaṃ mokṣārthe'pi vyavasthitaḥ
āścaryaṃ kāmavaśago vikalaḥ keliśikṣayā

3.6It is strange that a man settled in the supreme non-duality and set on being free is still shaken by the games of love.

उद्भूतं ज्ञानदुर्मित्रमवधार्यातिदुर्बलः ।
आश्चर्यं काममाकाङ्क्षेत् कालमन्तमनुश्रितः ॥ ७ ॥
udbhūtaṃ jñānadurmitramavadhāryātidurbalaḥ
āścaryaṃ kāmamākāṅkṣet kālamantamanuśritaḥ

3.7It is strange that a man who knows for certain that desire is the enemy of knowledge, and who is worn out and near his last day, should still want his pleasures.

इहामुत्र विरक्तस्य नित्यानित्यविवेकिनः ।
आश्चर्यं मोक्षकामस्य मोक्षाद् एव विभीषिका ॥ ८ ॥
ihāmutra viraktasya nityānityavivekinaḥ
āścaryaṃ mokṣakāmasya mokṣād eva vibhīṣikā

3.8It is strange that a man loose from this world and the next, who tells the lasting from the passing and longs for freedom, is afraid of freedom itself.

The eight verses of the test are the most unexpected pages in the book, because the poet who says there is nothing to do spends them listing what a man who has understood should no longer be doing. Nityaswarupananda's note draws the line exactly where it belongs: verses 1 to 8 describe the half-hearted aspirant, and from verse 9 the mind and conduct of the one who knows are described. The last of the eight is the sharpest — a man may want freedom and be afraid of it, and the commentary supplies the reason, that he is afraid of losing what he has.

धीरस्तु भोज्यमानोऽपि पीड्यमानोऽपि सर्वदा ।
आत्मानं केवलं पश्यन् न तुष्यति न कुप्यति ॥ ९ ॥
dhīrastu bhojyamāno'pi pīḍyamāno'pi sarvadā
ātmānaṃ kevalaṃ paśyan na tuṣyati na kupyati

3.9Feasted or tormented, the serene man always sees the absolute self and is neither pleased nor angry.

चेष्टमानं शरीरं स्वं पश्यत्यन्यशरीरवत् ।
संस्तवे चापि निन्दायां कथं क्षुभ्येत् महाशयः ॥ १० ॥
ceṣṭamānaṃ śarīraṃ svaṃ paśyatyanyaśarīravat
saṃstave cāpi nindāyāṃ kathaṃ kṣubhyet mahāśayaḥ

3.10The great soul watches his own body act as if it were another's. How then can praise or blame disturb him?

मायामात्रमिदं विश्वं पश्यन् विगतकौतुकः ।
अपि सन्निहिते मृत्यौ कथं त्रस्यति धीरधीः ॥ ११ ॥
māyāmātramidaṃ viśvaṃ paśyan vigatakautukaḥ
api sannihite mṛtyau kathaṃ trasyati dhīradhīḥ

3.11Seeing this world as illusion and losing all curiosity about it, how can a man of steady mind fear death when it comes?

Verse 11 makes the claim the chapter has been working towards. A man who has stopped finding the world interesting has nothing left for death to take. The word for the world here is māyāmātram, illusion and nothing besides, and the loss of curiosity, vigatakautuka, is given as a consequence of seeing rather than as a discipline to be kept up. Verse 12 adds the harder case, the man whose hopes have failed and who wants nothing even so.

निःस्पृहं मानसं यस्य नैराश्येऽपि महात्मनः ।
तस्यात्मज्ञानतृप्तस्य तुलना केन जायते ॥ १२ ॥
niḥspṛhaṃ mānasaṃ yasya nairāśye'pi mahātmanaḥ
tasyātmajñānatṛptasya tulanā kena jāyate

3.12With whom shall we compare the great soul who is content with the knowledge of the self and wants nothing even when his hopes fail?

स्वभावाद् एव जानानो दृश्यमेतन्न किञ्चन ।
इदं ग्राह्यमिदं त्याज्यं स किं पश्यति धीरधीः ॥ १३ ॥
svabhāvād eva jānāno dṛśyametanna kiñcana
idaṃ grāhyamidaṃ tyājyaṃ sa kiṃ paśyati dhīradhīḥ

3.13Why should a man of steady mind, who knows that what is seen is in its nature nothing, hold one thing fit to take and another fit to refuse?

अन्तस्त्यक्तकषायस्य निर्द्वन्द्वस्य निराशिषः ।
यदृच्छयागतो भोगो न दुःखाय न तुष्टये ॥ १४ ॥
antastyaktakaṣāyasya nirdvandvasya nirāśiṣaḥ
yadṛcchayāgato bhogo na duḥkhāya na tuṣṭaye

3.14To one whose mind has let attachment go, who is past the pairs, and who is free of desire, whatever comes of itself brings neither pleasure nor pain.

Verse 10 gives the book its most usable sentence. The great soul watches his own body act as if it were somebody else's, and the point the commentary draws from it is that praise and blame attach to conduct, conduct is bodily, and a man for whom bodily action is somebody else's is out of the reach of both.

Chapter 4: the game

हन्तात्मज्ञानस्य धीरस्य खेलतो भोगलीलया ।
न हि संसारवाहीकैर्मूढैः सह समानता ॥ १ ॥
hantātmajñānasya dhīrasya khelato bhogalīlayā
na hi saṃsāravāhīkairmūḍhaiḥ saha samānatā

4.1The knower of the self who plays the game of living is nothing like the deluded beasts of burden of this world.

यत् पदं प्रेप्सवो दीनाः शक्राद्याः सर्वदेवताः ।
अहो तत्र स्थितो योगी न हर्षमुपगच्छति ॥ २ ॥
yat padaṃ prepsavo dīnāḥ śakrādyāḥ sarvadevatāḥ
aho tatra sthito yogī na harṣamupagacchati

4.2The yogi is not elated to stand in that state which Indra and all the gods pine for and are wretched without.

तज्ज्ञस्य पुण्यपापाभ्यां स्पर्शो ह्यन्तर्न जायते ।
न ह्याकाशस्य धूमेन दृश्यमानापि सङ्गतिः ॥ ३ ॥
tajjñasya puṇyapāpābhyāṃ sparśo hyantarna jāyate
na hyākāśasya dhūmena dṛśyamānāpi saṅgatiḥ

4.3Virtue and vice do not touch the heart of one who knows that, as smoke does not touch the sky it seems to stain.

The yogi of verse 2 is not elated to stand where Indra and the gods are wretched to stand outside, and Nityaswarupananda gives the reason: elation belongs to what is unaccustomed, and this is what he has always been. Verse 3 supplies the mechanism of the chapter in one image. Smoke appears to stain the sky and does not touch it, and merit and demerit stand in that relation to the man who knows: apparent contact, and no contact at all.

आत्मैवेदं जगत्सर्वं ज्ञातं येन महात्मना ।
यदृच्छया वर्तमानं तं निषेद्धुं क्षमेत कः ॥ ४ ॥
ātmaivedaṃ jagatsarvaṃ jñātaṃ yena mahātmanā
yadṛcchayā vartamānaṃ taṃ niṣeddhuṃ kṣameta kaḥ

4.4Who can stop the great soul who has known this whole world as the self alone from living exactly as he pleases?

आब्रह्मस्तम्बपर्यन्ते भूतग्रामे चतुर्विधे ।
विज्ञस्यैव हि सामर्थ्यमिच्छानिच्छाविवर्जने ॥ ५ ॥
ābrahmastambaparyante bhūtagrāme caturvidhe
vijñasyaiva hi sāmarthyamicchānicchāvivarjane

4.5Of the four kinds of creature, from Brahmā down to a tuft of grass, only the wise man is strong enough to drop both wanting and refusing.

आत्मानमद्वयं कश्चिज्जानाति जगदीश्वरम् ।
यद् वेत्ति तत्स कुरुते न भयं तस्य कुत्रचित् ॥ ६ ॥
ātmānamadvayaṃ kaścijjānāti jagadīśvaram
yad vetti tatsa kurute na bhayaṃ tasya kutracit

4.6Rare is the man who knows the self as one without a second and as the lord of the world. He does what he knows, and he fears nothing from any quarter.

The fourth verse is the text's licence and its scandal. Nityaswarupananda spends most of a page making sure it is not read as permission, and adds the sentence that has to be added: the one who knows does not stoop to evil, because the tendencies that would produce it are gone before the state is reached. The image he gives for it is Ramakrishna's, that an expert dancer never puts a foot wrong.

Chapters 5 to 8: four ways, and then none

न ते सङ्गोऽस्ति केनापि किं शुद्धस्त्यक्तुमिच्छसि ।
सङ्घातविलयं कुर्वन्नेवमेव लयं व्रज ॥ १ ॥
na te saṅgo'sti kenāpi kiṃ śuddhastyaktumicchasi
saṅghātavilayaṃ kurvannevameva layaṃ vraja

5.1You have no contact with anything. Being pure, what would you renounce? Undo the knot of body and mind and go to your rest.

उदेति भवतो विश्वं वारिधेरिव बुद्बुदः ।
इति ज्ञात्वैकमात्मानमेवमेव लयं व्रज ॥ २ ॥
udeti bhavato viśvaṃ vāridheriva budbudaḥ
iti jñātvaikamātmānamevameva layaṃ vraja

5.2The world rises out of you as bubbles rise out of the sea. Know the self as one, and go to your rest.

प्रत्यक्षमप्यवस्तुत्वाद् विश्वं नास्त्यमले त्वयि ।
रज्जुसर्प इव व्यक्तमेवमेव लयं व्रज ॥ ३ ॥
pratyakṣamapyavastutvād viśvaṃ nāstyamale tvayi
rajjusarpa iva vyaktamevameva layaṃ vraja

5.3Though it stands before the senses, the world is not in you, who are pure, being unreal as a snake in a rope. Go to your rest.

समदुःखसुखः पूर्ण आशानैराश्ययोः समः ।
समजीवितमृत्युः सन्नेवमेव लयं व्रज ॥ ४ ॥
samaduḥkhasukhaḥ pūrṇa āśānairāśyayoḥ samaḥ
samajīvitamṛtyuḥ sannevameva layaṃ vraja

5.4You are whole and the same in misery and happiness, in hope and despair, in living and dying. Go to your rest.

Four verses, four refrains, four routes to the same place, and the commentary sets them out as a list: be unattached; see the world as the same reality as the self; see it as illusory; and remain unmoved by anything inner or outer. Any one of them will do, which is the first sign that the text does not think of itself as prescribing a method.

आकाशवदनन्तोऽहं घटवत् प्राकृतं जगत् ।
इति ज्ञानं तथैतस्य न त्यागो न ग्रहो लयः ॥ १ ॥
ākāśavadananto'haṃ ghaṭavat prākṛtaṃ jagat
iti jñānaṃ tathaitasya na tyāgo na graho layaḥ

6.1I am boundless space, and the world a jar. This is knowledge. It is not to be renounced, not to be accepted, not to be dissolved.

महोदधिरिवाहं स प्रपञ्चो वीचिसन्निभः ।
इति ज्ञानं तथैतस्य न त्यागो न ग्रहो लयः ॥ २ ॥
mahodadhirivāhaṃ sa prapañco vīcisannibhaḥ
iti jñānaṃ tathaitasya na tyāgo na graho layaḥ

6.2I am the ocean, and the world a wave. This is knowledge. It is not to be renounced, not to be accepted, not to be dissolved.

अहं स शुक्तिसङ्काशो रूप्यवद् विश्वकल्पना ।
इति ज्ञानं तथैतस्य न त्यागो न ग्रहो लयः ॥ ३ ॥
ahaṃ sa śuktisaṅkāśo rūpyavad viśvakalpanā
iti jñānaṃ tathaitasya na tyāgo na graho layaḥ

6.3I am the mother-of-pearl, and the world a streak of silver. This is knowledge. It is not to be renounced, not to be accepted, not to be dissolved.

अहं वा सर्वभूतेषु सर्वभूतान्यथो मयि ।
इति ज्ञानं तथैतस्य न त्यागो न ग्रहो लयः ॥ ४ ॥
ahaṃ vā sarvabhūteṣu sarvabhūtānyatho mayi
iti jñānaṃ tathaitasya na tyāgo na graho layaḥ

6.4I am in all beings and all beings are in me. This is knowledge. It is not to be renounced, not to be accepted, not to be dissolved.

Chapter 6 withdraws chapter 5. Dissolving was the instruction one page earlier; here even dissolving is refused, because the wish to dissolve the world is still a verdict against it. Nityaswarupananda's gloss is Ramakrishna's staircase: until you have reached the roof you treat the stairs as something other than the roof, and when you have reached it you find the stairs were made of the same material. Byrom adds that the refrain of holding and letting go echoes Gauḍapāda, whose asparśa yoga the poet is refining.

मय्यनन्तमहाम्भोधौ विश्वपोत इतस्ततः ।
भ्रमति स्वान्तवातेन न ममास्त्यसहिष्णुता ॥ १ ॥
mayyanantamahāmbhodhau viśvapota itastataḥ
bhramati svāntavātena na mamāstyasahiṣṇutā

7.1In me, the boundless ocean, the ship of the world runs here and there on the wind of its own nature. I am not put out by it.

मय्यनन्तमहाम्भोधौ जगद्वीचिः स्वभावतः ।
उदेतु वास्तमायातु न मे वृद्धिर्न च क्षतिः ॥ २ ॥
mayyanantamahāmbhodhau jagadvīciḥ svabhāvataḥ
udetu vāstamāyātu na me vṛddhirna ca kṣatiḥ

7.2In me, the boundless ocean, let the wave of the world rise or vanish of itself. I neither grow nor shrink by it.

मय्यनन्तमहाम्भोधौ विश्वं नाम विकल्पना ।
अतिशान्तो निराकार एतदेवाहमास्थितः ॥ ३ ॥
mayyanantamahāmbhodhau viśvaṃ nāma vikalpanā
atiśānto nirākāra etadevāhamāsthitaḥ

7.3In me, the boundless ocean, the world is imagined. I am altogether still and formless. In this alone I rest.

नात्मा भावेषु नो भावस्तत्रानन्ते निरञ्जने ।
इत्यसक्तोऽस्पृहः शान्त एतदेवाहमास्थितः ॥ ४ ॥
nātmā bhāveṣu no bhāvastatrānante nirañjane
ityasakto'spṛhaḥ śānta etadevāhamāsthitaḥ

7.4The self is not in the object, nor is the object in that which is endless and unstained. It is unattached, without desire, at rest. In this alone I rest.

अहो चिन्मात्रमेवाहमिन्द्रजालोपमं जगत् ।
इति मम कथं कुत्र हेयोपादेयकल्पना ॥ ५ ॥
aho cinmātramevāhamindrajālopamaṃ jagat
iti mama kathaṃ kutra heyopādeyakalpanā

7.5I am awareness only, and the world a conjuror's show. Where in me, and how, could there be any taking or refusing?

Indrajāla, the conjuror's show of verse 5, is the last of the chapter's names for the world, after the ship, the wave and the thing merely imagined. It settles what the others leave open. A conjuror's trick is not a mistake about what is there; it is a performance that goes on while it is watched and takes nothing from the watcher. The question in the second half of the verse follows from it: there is nowhere in such a thing for acceptance or refusal to get a hold.

तदा बन्धो यदा चित्तं किञ्चिद् वाञ्छति शोचति ।
किञ्चिन् मुञ्चति गृह्णाति किञ्चिद्धृष्यति कुप्यति ॥ १ ॥
tadā bandho yadā cittaṃ kiñcid vāñchati śocati
kiñcin muñcati gṛhṇāti kiñciddhṛṣyati kupyati

8.1It is bondage when the mind wants something, grieves at something, drops something, holds something, is pleased or is angry.

तदा मुक्तिर्यदा चित्तं न वाञ्छति न शोचति ।
न मुञ्चति न गृह्णाति न हृष्यति न कुप्यति ॥ २ ॥
tadā muktiryadā cittaṃ na vāñchati na śocati
na muñcati na gṛhṇāti na hṛṣyati na kupyati

8.2It is release when the mind does not want and does not grieve, does not drop and does not hold, is not pleased and is not angry.

तदा बन्धो यदा चित्तं सक्तं कास्वपि दृष्टिषु ।
तदा मोक्षो यदा चित्तमसक्तं सर्वदृष्टिषु ॥ ३ ॥
tadā bandho yadā cittaṃ saktaṃ kāsvapi dṛṣṭiṣu
tadā mokṣo yadā cittamasaktaṃ sarvadṛṣṭiṣu

8.3It is bondage when the mind hangs on anything the senses bring. It is release when it hangs on none of them.

यदा नाहं तदा मोक्षो यदाहं बन्धनं तदा ।
मत्वेति हेलया किञ्चिन्मा गृहाण विमुञ्च मा ॥ ४ ॥
yadā nāhaṃ tadā mokṣo yadāhaṃ bandhanaṃ tadā
matveti helayā kiñcinmā gṛhāṇa vimuñca mā

8.4Where there is no I, there is release; where there is I, there is bondage. Consider this, and easily hold nothing and refuse nothing.

Chapter 8 is four verses long and is the definition the whole book rests on. Bondage and liberation are not states of the world, nor stages of a career, nor rewards; they are the presence or absence of a movement in the mind, and the movement is desire in its two directions, wanting and refusing. Nothing is said about how long it takes, because on this definition duration does not enter: the mind either is moving that way at this moment or it is not.

Desire alone is the world

Chapters 9 to 14 alternate: Aṣṭāvakra on detachment, quiet and understanding; Janaka on what all this has done to him. The verses grow shorter and more epigrammatic, and the argument stops advancing and starts circling.

Chapters 9 to 11: detachment, quiet, understanding

कृताकृते च द्वन्द्वानि कदा शान्तानि कस्य वा ।
एवं ज्ञात्वेह निर्वेदाद् भव त्यागपरोऽव्रती ॥ १ ॥
kṛtākṛte ca dvandvāni kadā śāntāni kasya vā
evaṃ jñātveha nirvedād bhava tyāgaparo'vratī

9.1Duties done and left undone, and the pairs of opposites — when do they end, and for whom? Knowing this, be indifferent to the world, given to renunciation and keeping no observance.

कस्यापि तात धन्यस्य लोकचेष्टावलोकनात् ।
जीवितेच्छा बुभुक्षा च बुभुत्सोपशमं गताः ॥ २ ॥
kasyāpi tāta dhanyasya lokaceṣṭāvalokanāt
jīvitecchā bubhukṣā ca bubhutsopaśamaṃ gatāḥ

9.2Rare indeed, my child, is the blessed man whose wish to live, to enjoy and to know has been put out by watching the ways of people.

वासना एव संसार इति सर्वा विमुञ्च ताः ।
तत्त्यागो वासनात्यागात्स्थितिरद्य यथा तथा ॥ ८ ॥
vāsanā eva saṃsāra iti sarvā vimuñca tāḥ
tattyāgo vāsanātyāgātsthitiradya yathā tathā

9.8Old impressions alone are the world, so let go of them all. Letting go of the world follows from letting go of them. Now you may live wherever you are.

Verse 2 says what kind of man the chapter is addressed to, and it is a narrow class: one whose wish to live, to enjoy and to know has gone out from watching how people carry on. Nityaswarupananda notes the two ways of arriving there. Some read the hollowness of the world off the suffering of others; the rest get the lesson only by going in and finding out. The first verse asks for indifference and, in the same line, for no observance to be kept.

विहाय वैरिणं काममर्थं चानर्थसङ्कुलम् ।
धर्ममप्येतयोर्हेतुं सर्वत्रानादरं कुरु ॥ १ ॥
vihāya vairiṇaṃ kāmamarthaṃ cānarthasaṅkulam
dharmamapyetayorhetuṃ sarvatrānādaraṃ kuru

10.1Give up desire, which is the enemy, and gain, which brings trouble with it, and the good works that are the cause of both. Be indifferent to everything.

यत्र यत्र भवेत्तृष्णा संसारं विद्धि तत्र वै ।
प्रौढवैराग्यमाश्रित्य वीततृष्णः सुखी भव ॥ ३ ॥
yatra yatra bhavettṛṣṇā saṃsāraṃ viddhi tatra vai
prauḍhavairāgyamāśritya vītatṛṣṇaḥ sukhī bhava

10.3Know that wherever there is desire, there is the world. With firm detachment be free of desire and be happy.

तृष्णामात्रात्मको बन्धस्तन्नाशो मोक्ष उच्यते ।
भवासंसक्तिमात्रेण प्राप्तितुष्टिर्मुहुर्मुहुः ॥ ४ ॥
tṛṣṇāmātrātmako bandhastannāśo mokṣa ucyate
bhavāsaṃsaktimātreṇa prāptituṣṭirmuhurmuhuḥ

10.4Bondage is only desire, and its destruction is called release. By not clinging to the world alone is the constant joy of the self attained.

कृतं न कति जन्मानि कायेन मनसा गिरा ।
दुःखमायासदं कर्म तदद्याप्युपरम्यताम् ॥ ८ ॥
kṛtaṃ na kati janmāni kāyena manasā girā
duḥkhamāyāsadaṃ karma tadadyāpyuparamyatām

10.8With body, mind and speech, in how many births have you not done hard and painful work? Stop at least today.

The eighth verse of chapter 9 is the hinge of the middle of the book, and Byrom's note on the term it turns on is worth having. Vāsanā, which translators usually give as desires, is closer to impressions left by past experience, habits of mind, the grooves that make wanting automatic. On that reading the instruction is not to renounce the world, which would be one more transaction with it, but to renounce the habit, after which the world stops presenting itself as something to be renounced. The last verse of chapter 10 supplies the tone the poet reaches for when he is impatient. Stop, and then adya, today.

भावाभावविकारश्च स्वभावादिति निश्चयी ।
निर्विकारो गतक्लेशः सुखेनैवोपशाम्यति ॥ १ ॥
bhāvābhāvavikāraśca svabhāvāditi niścayī
nirvikāro gatakleśaḥ sukhenaivopaśāmyati

11.1Knowing that being, perishing and changing belong to the nature of things, a man is unshaken and untroubled, and comes easily to rest.

चिन्तया जायते दुःखं नान्यथेहेति निश्चयी ।
तया हीनः सुखी शान्तः सर्वत्र गलितस्पृहः ॥ ५ ॥
cintayā jāyate duḥkhaṃ nānyatheheti niścayī
tayā hīnaḥ sukhī śāntaḥ sarvatra galitaspṛhaḥ

11.5Knowing for certain that nothing but anxiety makes misery here, a man is rid of it, and is happy, at peace, and free of longing everywhere.

नाहं देहो न मे देहो बोधोऽहमिति निश्चयी ।
कैवल्यमिव सम्प्राप्तो न स्मरत्यकृतं कृतम् ॥ ६ ॥
nāhaṃ deho na me deho bodho'hamiti niścayī
kaivalyamiva samprāpto na smaratyakṛtaṃ kṛtam

11.6I am not the body, the body is not mine, I am awareness: knowing this for certain, a man forgets what he has done and left undone, as if he had reached the absolute.

नानाश्चर्यमिदं विश्वं न किञ्चिदिति निश्चयी ।
निर्वासनः स्फूर्तिमात्रो न किञ्चिदिव शाम्यति ॥ ८ ॥
nānāścaryamidaṃ viśvaṃ na kiñciditi niścayī
nirvāsanaḥ sphūrtimātro na kiñcidiva śāmyati

11.8Knowing for certain that this many-shaped and marvellous world is nothing, a man becomes free of desire and is awareness, and rests as if nothing were there.

Every verse of chapter 11 begins from a certainty and ends in a change of state, and the Sanskrit marks it with the same word each time, niścayī, one who has settled it. The claim is not that thinking these things will produce the state; it is that the state and the certainty are the same event. Nityaswarupananda's note on verse 5 is the practical one in the chapter: suffering comes and is inevitable, and it is by dwelling on it that we make it misery.

Chapters 12 to 14: the pupil reports

कायकृत्यासहः पूर्वं ततो वाग्विस्तरासहः ।
अथ चिन्तासहस्तस्माद् एवमेवाहमास्थितः ॥ १ ॥
kāyakṛtyāsahaḥ pūrvaṃ tato vāgvistarāsahaḥ
atha cintāsahastasmād evamevāhamāsthitaḥ

12.1First I became unable to bear bodily action, then long speech, and then thought itself. So it is that I stand.

समाध्यासादिविक्षिप्तौ व्यवहारः समाधये ।
एवं विलोक्य नियममेवमेवाहमास्थितः ॥ ३ ॥
samādhyāsādivikṣiptau vyavahāraḥ samādhaye
evaṃ vilokya niyamamevamevāhamāsthitaḥ

12.3Effort at concentration is made where there is distraction from what has been laid over the self. Seeing that this is the rule, so it is that I stand.

कर्मानुष्ठानमज्ञानाद् यथैवोपरमस्तथा ।
बुध्वा सम्यगिदं तत्त्वमेवमेवाहमास्थितः ॥ ६ ॥
karmānuṣṭhānamajñānād yathaivoparamastathā
budhvā samyagidaṃ tattvamevamevāhamāsthitaḥ

12.6Abstaining from action comes as much from not knowing as performing it does. Knowing this truth fully, so it is that I stand.

अचिन्त्यं चिन्त्यमानोऽपि चिन्तारूपं भजत्यसौ ।
त्यक्त्वा तद्भावनं तस्माद् एवमेवाहमास्थितः ॥ ७ ॥
acintyaṃ cintyamāno'pi cintārūpaṃ bhajatyasau
tyaktvā tadbhāvanaṃ tasmād evamevāhamāsthitaḥ

12.7Thinking on the unthinkable one, a man only takes up a form of thought. Giving up that thought, so it is that I stand.

Chapter 12 is Janaka's account of what dropped away and in what order: body, then speech, then thought. The seventh verse is the argument against meditation stated as cleanly as the text ever states it. To think about what is beyond thought is to make a shape in the mind, and the shape is not the thing. The commentary insists that the state indicated is higher than contemplation and not lower, which is the distinction the book depends on and the one its critics say it cannot police.

अकिञ्चनभवं स्वास्थ्यं कौपीनत्वेऽपि दुर्लभम् ।
त्यागादाने विहायास्मादहमासे यथासुखम् ॥ १ ॥
akiñcanabhavaṃ svāsthyaṃ kaupīnatve'pi durlabham
tyāgādāne vihāyāsmādahamāse yathāsukham

13.1The tranquillity that arises in one who has nothing is rare even in one who wears a loincloth; so, giving up both renouncing and accepting, I live happily.

कृतं किमपि नैव स्याद् इति सञ्चिन्त्य तत्त्वतः ।
यदा यत्कर्तुमायाति तत् कृत्वासे यथासुखम् ॥ ३ ॥
kṛtaṃ kimapi naiva syād iti sañcintya tattvataḥ
yadā yatkartumāyāti tat kṛtvāse yathāsukham

13.3Fully aware that nothing whatever is really done by the self, I do whatever comes to be done, and I live happily.

सुखादिरूपा नियमं भावेष्वालोक्य भूरिशः ।
शुभाशुभे विहायास्मादहमासे यथासुखम् ॥ ७ ॥
sukhādirūpā niyamaṃ bhāveṣvālokya bhūriśaḥ
śubhāśubhe vihāyāsmādahamāse yathāsukham

13.7Watching again and again how pleasure and the rest shift with conditions, I have given up good and bad, and I am happy.

Chapter 14 describes the same man from outside and names the state in the negative: kṣīṇasaṃsmaraṇa, one whose recollections are worn out. The commentary is precise about what the word covers, the life of the senses, the pairs of opposites, and the store of past action that would otherwise produce another birth. What is left is a mind empty by nature rather than emptied by an exercise, which thinks of things only by inattention and is awake in the way a sleeping man is awake.

प्रकृत्या शून्यचित्तो यः प्रमादाद् भावभावनः ।
निद्रितो बोधित इव क्षीणसंस्मरणो हि सः ॥ १ ॥
prakṛtyā śūnyacitto yaḥ pramādād bhāvabhāvanaḥ
nidrito bodhita iva kṣīṇasaṃsmaraṇo hi saḥ

14.1He has his old impressions exhausted whose mind is empty by nature, who thinks of things only through inattention, and who is as though awake in sleep.

विज्ञाते साक्षिपुरुषे परमात्मनि चेश्वरे ।
नैराश्ये बन्धमोक्षे च न चिन्ता मुक्तये मम ॥ ३ ॥
vijñāte sākṣipuruṣe paramātmani ceśvare
nairāśye bandhamokṣe ca na cintā muktaye mama

14.3Having realised the supreme self who is the witness and the lord, and having lost all desire for bondage and for liberation, I have no anxiety about being free.

अन्तर्विकल्पशून्यस्य बहिः स्वच्छन्दचारिणः ।
भ्रान्तस्येव दशास्तास्तास्तादृशा एव जानते ॥ ४ ॥
antarvikalpaśūnyasya bahiḥ svacchandacāriṇaḥ
bhrāntasyeva daśāstāstāstādṛśā eva jānate

14.4The conditions of one who within is without doubt and outside moves about as he pleases, like a deluded man, can be understood only by those like him.

The loincloth is a good example of how the poet argues by degrees. A man who owns one garment is still a man who owns something, and owning is not the trouble; wanting is. Chapter 13 makes contentment a condition of knowledge rather than a reward for it, and Byrom marks the refrain: happiness is not what the poet promises at the end but what he tells the reader to begin with.

The clear space of awareness

Chapter 15 is the second sustained instruction and the one most often anthologised. Nityaswarupananda calls it unsurpassed in the book for its language and its teaching, and it is where the doctrine reaches its furthest point.

यथातथोपदेशेन कृतार्थः सत्त्वबुद्धिमान् ।
आजीवमपि जिज्ञासुः परस्तत्र विमुह्यति ॥ १ ॥
yathātathopadeśena kṛtārthaḥ sattvabuddhimān
ājīvamapi jijñāsuḥ parastatra vimuhyati

15.1A man of pure understanding reaches his end by instruction given in passing; another may enquire all his life and remain bewildered.

मोक्षो विषयवैरस्यं बन्धो वैषयिको रसः ।
एतावदेव विज्ञानं यथेच्छसि तथा कुरु ॥ २ ॥
mokṣo viṣayavairasyaṃ bandho vaiṣayiko rasaḥ
etāvadeva vijñānaṃ yathecchasi tathā kuru

15.2Distaste for what the senses bring is liberation; liking for it is bondage. This is knowledge. Now do as you please.

वाग्मिप्राज्ञामहोद्योगं जनं मूकजडालसम् ।
करोति तत्त्वबोधोऽयमतस्त्यक्तो बुभुक्षभिः ॥ ३ ॥
vāgmiprājñāmahodyogaṃ janaṃ mūkajaḍālasam
karoti tattvabodho'yamatastyakto bubhukṣabhiḥ

15.3This knowledge of the truth makes a man who was eloquent, clever and busy into one who is mute, inert and idle; so those who wish to enjoy the world avoid it.

न त्वं देहो न ते देहो भोक्ता कर्ता न वा भवान् ।
चिद्रूपोऽसि सदा साक्षी निरपेक्षः सुखं चर ॥ ४ ॥
na tvaṃ deho na te deho bhoktā kartā na vā bhavān
cidrūpo'si sadā sākṣī nirapekṣaḥ sukhaṃ cara

15.4You are not the body, nor is the body yours; you are not the doer and not the enjoyer. You are awareness itself, the everlasting witness, free. Go about happily.

रागद्वेषौ मनोधर्मौ न मनस्ते कदाचन ।
निर्विकल्पोऽसि बोधात्मा निर्विकारः सुखं चर ॥ ५ ॥
rāgadveṣau manodharmau na manaste kadācana
nirvikalpo'si bodhātmā nirvikāraḥ sukhaṃ cara

15.5Liking and loathing are of the mind, and the mind is never yours. You are free of conflict, awareness itself, unchanging. Go about happily.

The second verse is the definition of chapter 8 restated in six words, and the half-line after it, yathecchasi tathā kuru, now do as you please, is the same line Krishna uses at the end of the Bhagavad Gītā when he has finished and leaves Arjuna to decide. Here it arrives in the middle. The third verse is the honest one: the poet knows what his teaching does to a competent man, and knows why competent men keep away from it.

सर्वभूतेषु चात्मानं सर्वभूतानि चात्मनि ।
विज्ञाय निरहङ्कारो निर्ममस्त्वं सुखी भव ॥ ६ ॥
sarvabhūteṣu cātmānaṃ sarvabhūtāni cātmani
vijñāya nirahaṅkāro nirmamastvaṃ sukhī bhava

15.6Knowing the self in all beings and all beings in the self, without I and without mine, be happy.

विश्वं स्फुरति यत्रेदं तरङ्गा इव सागरे ।
तत्त्वमेव न सन्देहश्चिन्मूर्ते विज्वरो भव ॥ ७ ॥
viśvaṃ sphurati yatredaṃ taraṅgā iva sāgare
tattvameva na sandehaścinmūrte vijvaro bhava

15.7You are that in which the world shows itself as waves show in the sea. You are awareness itself. Let the fever go.

श्रद्धस्व तात श्रद्धस्व नात्र मोहं कुरुष्व भोः ।
ज्ञानस्वरूपो भगवानात्मा त्वं प्रकृतेः परः ॥ ८ ॥
śraddhasva tāta śraddhasva nātra mohaṃ kuruṣva bhoḥ
jñānasvarūpo bhagavānātmā tvaṃ prakṛteḥ paraḥ

15.8Have faith, my son, have faith. Do not mislead yourself in this. You are knowledge itself, you are the lord, you are the self, and you are beyond nature.

The first half of verse 6 has appeared once already, at 3.5, where it was used against Janaka: strange that a man who has known the self in all and all in the self should go on saying mine. Here the same words are an instruction. A near neighbour of the line stands in the sixth chapter of the Bhagavad Gītā. What the poet does with it is his own, for from the same seeing he draws no conduct at all, only the dropping of the two words I and mine.

गुणैः संवेष्टितो देहस्तिष्ठत्यायाति याति च ।
आत्मा न गन्ता नागन्ता किमेनमनुशोचसि ॥ ९ ॥
guṇaiḥ saṃveṣṭito dehastiṣṭhatyāyāti yāti ca
ātmā na gantā nāgantā kimenamanuśocasi

15.9The body with its senses comes, stays and goes. The self neither comes nor goes. Why then mourn for it?

देहस्तिष्ठतु कल्पान्तं गच्छत्वद्यैव वा पुनः ।
क्व वृद्धिः क्व च वा हानिस्तव चिन्मात्ररूपिणः ॥ १० ॥
dehastiṣṭhatu kalpāntaṃ gacchatvadyaiva vā punaḥ
kva vṛddhiḥ kva ca vā hānistava cinmātrarūpiṇaḥ

15.10Let the body last to the end of the age or let it go today. What increase or decrease is there in you, who are awareness?

त्वय्यनन्तमहाम्भोधौ विश्ववीचिः स्वभावतः ।
उदेतु वास्तमायातु न ते वृद्धिर्न वा क्षतिः ॥ ११ ॥
tvayyanantamahāmbhodhau viśvavīciḥ svabhāvataḥ
udetu vāstamāyātu na te vṛddhirna vā kṣatiḥ

15.11Let the wave of the world rise or fall of itself in you, the boundless ocean. It is no gain and no loss to you.

The eighth verse is the only place where the poet asks for faith, and he asks twice in the same line. It is not faith in a person or a doctrine to be verified later; the commentary reads it as the teacher pressing the point when the pupil cannot yet believe that the description applies to him.

तात चिन्मात्ररूपोऽसि न ते भिन्नमिदं जगत् ।
अतः कस्य कथं कुत्र हेयोपादेयकल्पना ॥ १२ ॥
tāta cinmātrarūpo'si na te bhinnamidaṃ jagat
ataḥ kasya kathaṃ kutra heyopādeyakalpanā

15.12My son, you are awareness itself, and this world is not other than you. Who then accepts or refuses, and how, and where?

एकस्मिन्नव्यये शान्ते चिदाकाशेऽमले त्वयि ।
कुतो जन्म कुतो कर्म कुतोऽहङ्कार एव च ॥ १३ ॥
ekasminnavyaye śānte cidākāśe'male tvayi
kuto janma kuto karma kuto'haṅkāra eva ca

15.13For you who are one, unchanging, still, the pure space of awareness, where is birth, where is action, where even the sense of I?

यत्त्वं पश्यसि तत्रैकस्त्वमेव प्रतिभाससे ।
किं पृथक् भासते स्वर्णात् कटकाङ्गदनूपुरम् ॥ १४ ॥
yattvaṃ paśyasi tatraikastvameva pratibhāsase
kiṃ pṛthak bhāsate svarṇāt kaṭakāṅgadanūpuram

15.14Whatever you see, it is you alone who appear there. Do bangle, armlet and anklet appear as other than gold?

अयं सोऽहमयं नाहं विभागमिति सन्त्यज ।
सर्वमात्मेति निश्चित्य निःसङ्कल्पः सुखी भव ॥ १५ ॥
ayaṃ so'hamayaṃ nāhaṃ vibhāgamiti santyaja
sarvamātmeti niścitya niḥsaṅkalpaḥ sukhī bhava

15.15Give up entirely such distinctions as I am that and I am not this. Take everything for the self, be without desire, and be happy.

तवैवाज्ञानतो विश्वं त्वमेकः परमार्थतः ।
त्वत्तोऽन्यो नास्ति संसारी नासंसारी च कश्चन ॥ १६ ॥
tavaivājñānato viśvaṃ tvamekaḥ paramārthataḥ
tvatto'nyo nāsti saṃsārī nāsaṃsārī ca kaścana

15.16It is by your own ignorance that the world exists. In reality you alone are. There is no separate creature and no lord apart from you.

The fifteenth verse cancels the two sentences that Advaita practice is usually built out of. So'ham asmi, I am that, and neti neti, not this, not this, are the affirmative and negative repetitions of the tradition; the poet calls both distinctions and drops them. The sixteenth is the furthest the text goes, and Nityaswarupananda pauses to consider whether it commits the author to the doctrine that only one subject exists, and decides it is better read as speech from the absolute standpoint. Read either way it leaves no second party anywhere, including the god who was still standing three verses earlier.

भ्रान्तिमात्रमिदं विश्वं न किञ्चिदिति निश्चयी ।
निर्वासनः स्फूर्तिमात्रो न किञ्चिदिव शाम्यति ॥ १७ ॥
bhrāntimātramidaṃ viśvaṃ na kiñciditi niścayī
nirvāsanaḥ sphūrtimātro na kiñcidiva śāmyati

15.17Knowing for certain that the world is illusion and nothing, a man becomes free of desire and is pure awareness, and finds peace as though nothing existed.

एक एव भवाम्भोधावासीदस्ति भविष्यति ।
न ते बन्धोऽस्ति मोक्षो वा कृतकृत्यः सुखं चर ॥ १८ ॥
eka eva bhavāmbhodhāvāsīdasti bhaviṣyati
na te bandho'sti mokṣo vā kṛtakṛtyaḥ sukhaṃ cara

15.18In the ocean of the world, one alone was, is and will be. You have no bondage and no release. Live contented and happy.

मा सङ्कल्पविकल्पाभ्यां चित्तं क्षोभय चिन्मय ।
उपशाम्य सुखं तिष्ठ स्वात्मन्यानन्दविग्रहे ॥ १९ ॥
mā saṅkalpavikalpābhyāṃ cittaṃ kṣobhaya cinmaya
upaśāmya sukhaṃ tiṣṭha svātmanyānandavigrahe

15.19You who are awareness, do not disturb your mind with affirming and denying. Be still, and rest happily in yourself, who are joy itself.

त्यजैव ध्यानं सर्वत्र मा किञ्चिद् हृदि धारय ।
आत्मा त्वं मुक्त एवासि किं विमृश्य करिष्यसि ॥ २० ॥
tyajaiva dhyānaṃ sarvatra mā kiñcid hṛdi dhāraya
ātmā tvaṃ mukta evāsi kiṃ vimṛśya kariṣyasi

15.20Give up meditating altogether and hold nothing whatever in the mind. You are the self, and free. What will thinking do for you?

Chapters 16 and 17: forget, and the portrait begins

आचक्ष्व शृणु वा तात नानाशास्त्राण्यनेकशः ।
तथापि न तव स्वास्थ्यं सर्वविस्मरणाद् ऋते ॥ १ ॥
ācakṣva śṛṇu vā tāta nānāśāstrāṇyanekaśaḥ
tathāpi na tava svāsthyaṃ sarvavismaraṇād ṛte

16.1My child, you may recite and hear the various scriptures many times over, but you cannot be established in yourself unless you forget them all.

भोगं कर्म समाधिं वा कुरु विज्ञ तथापि ते ।
चित्तं निरस्तसर्वाशमत्यर्थं रोचयिष्यति ॥ २ ॥
bhogaṃ karma samādhiṃ vā kuru vijña tathāpi te
cittaṃ nirastasarvāśamatyarthaṃ rocayiṣyati

16.2Sage, you may enjoy, or work, or meditate; still your mind will long for that which is beyond all objects, in which every desire is extinguished.

आयासात्सकलो दुःखी नैनं जानाति कश्चन ।
अनेनैवोपदेशेन धन्यः प्राप्नोति निर्वृतिम् ॥ ३ ॥
āyāsātsakalo duḥkhī nainaṃ jānāti kaścana
anenaivopadeśena dhanyaḥ prāpnoti nirvṛtim

16.3All are unhappy because all exert themselves, and nobody knows it. The blessed man is set free by just this instruction.

व्यापारे खिद्यते यस्तु निमेषोन्मेषयोरपि ।
तस्यालस्य धुरीणस्य सुखं नान्यस्य कस्यचित् ॥ ४ ॥
vyāpāre khidyate yastu nimeṣonmeṣayorapi
tasyālasya dhurīṇasya sukhaṃ nānyasya kasyacit

16.4Happiness belongs to that lord of idlers for whom even the closing and opening of the eyelids is a burden, and to no one else.

हातुमिच्छति संसारं रागी दुःखजिहासया ।
वीतरागो हि निर्दुःखस्तस्मिन्नपि न खिद्यति ॥ ९ ॥
hātumicchati saṃsāraṃ rāgī duḥkhajihāsayā
vītarāgo hi nirduḥkhastasminnapi na khidyati

16.9A man attached to the world wants to renounce it in order to escape sorrow; a man without attachment has no sorrow and does not suffer even in the midst of it.

हरो यद्युपदेष्टा ते हरिः कमलजोऽपि वा ।
तथापि न तव स्वाथ्यं सर्वविस्मरणादृते ॥ ११ ॥
haro yadyupadeṣṭā te hariḥ kamalajo'pi vā
tathāpi na tava svāthyaṃ sarvavismaraṇādṛte

16.11Let Hara or Hari or the lotus-born Brahmā be your teacher: unless you forget everything, you will not be established in yourself.

The third verse of chapter 16 is the proposition on which the book stakes everything. Striving is the cause of unhappiness, not its cure, and nobody knows it because striving is what we do when we are unhappy. Byrom draws the practical distinction the verse needs and the text never quite draws itself: what the poet attacks is not practice but a strenuous indolence, the habit of substituting effort for understanding. The fourth verse is the joke that goes with it. The master idler finds blinking an imposition, and he is the only happy man in the book.

तेन ज्ञानफलं प्राप्तं योगाभ्यासफलं तथा ।
तृप्तः स्वच्छेन्द्रियो नित्यमेकाकी रमते तु यः ॥ १ ॥
tena jñānaphalaṃ prāptaṃ yogābhyāsaphalaṃ tathā
tṛptaḥ svacchendriyo nityamekākī ramate tu yaḥ

17.1He has gained the fruit of knowledge and the fruit of the practice of yoga who is contented, whose senses are purified, and who always enjoys being alone.

न कदाचिज्जगत्यस्मिन् तत्त्वज्ञो हन्त खिद्यति ।
यत एकेन तेनेदं पूर्णं ब्रह्माण्डमण्डलम् ॥ २ ॥
na kadācijjagatyasmin tattvajño hanta khidyati
yata ekena tenedaṃ pūrṇaṃ brahmāṇḍamaṇḍalam

17.2The knower of the truth is never miserable in this world, for he alone fills the whole of it.

बुभुक्षुरिह संसारे मुमुक्षुरपि दृश्यते ।
भोगमोक्षनिराकाङ्क्षी विरलो हि महाशयः ॥ ५ ॥
bubhukṣuriha saṃsāre mumukṣurapi dṛśyate
bhogamokṣanirākāṅkṣī viralo hi mahāśayaḥ

17.5Here in the world there are those who desire enjoyment and those who desire liberation; rare is the great soul who desires neither.

शून्या दृष्टिर्वृथा चेष्टा विकलानीन्द्रियाणि च ।
न स्पृहा न विरक्तिर्वा क्षीणसंसारसागरे ॥ ९ ॥
śūnyā dṛṣṭirvṛthā ceṣṭā vikalānīndriyāṇi ca
na spṛhā na viraktirvā kṣīṇasaṃsārasāgare

17.9In one for whom the ocean of the world has dried up there is neither attachment nor detachment. His look is vacant, his action without aim, his senses idle.

न जागर्ति न निद्राति नोन्मीलति न मीलति ।
अहो परदशा क्वापि वर्तते मुक्तचेतसः ॥ १० ॥
na jāgarti na nidrāti nonmīlati na mīlati
aho paradaśā kvāpi vartate muktacetasaḥ

17.10He is not awake and he is not asleep; he neither closes his eyes nor opens them. The liberated man is everywhere in the supreme condition.

सानुरागां स्त्रियं दृष्ट्वा मृत्युं वा समुपस्थितम् ।
अविह्वलमनाः स्वस्थो मुक्त एव महाशयः ॥ १४ ॥
sānurāgāṃ striyaṃ dṛṣṭvā mṛtyuṃ vā samupasthitam
avihvalamanāḥ svastho mukta eva mahāśayaḥ

17.14At the sight of a woman full of love or of death drawing near, the great soul is unperturbed and self-possessed. He is liberated indeed.

न हिंसा नैव कारुण्यं नौद्धत्यं न च दीनता ।
नाश्चर्यं नैव च क्षोभः क्षीणसंसरणे नरे ॥ १६ ॥
na hiṃsā naiva kāruṇyaṃ nauddhatyaṃ na ca dīnatā
nāścaryaṃ naiva ca kṣobhaḥ kṣīṇasaṃsaraṇe nare

17.16In the wise man whose worldly life is exhausted and who is no longer of human kind, there is neither wish to harm nor pity, neither insolence nor humility, neither wonder nor agitation.

मनःप्रकाशसंमोहस्वप्नजाड्यविवर्जितः ।
दशां कामपि सम्प्राप्तो भवेद् गलितमानसः ॥ २० ॥
manaḥprakāśasaṃmohasvapnajāḍyavivarjitaḥ
daśāṃ kāmapi samprāpto bhaved galitamānasaḥ

17.20A state past describing is attained by the sage whose mind has melted away and who is free of the mind's displays, of delusion, of dream and of dullness.

Verse 1 is the concession the book keeps making and then withdrawing: the fruit of practice is named alongside the fruit of knowledge, and Byrom's note takes it to mean that practice may be abandoned only after it has been done. Verse 16 is where the portrait turns severe. Compassion and humility go the way of pride and cruelty, because all four are still qualifications of a person, and the poet has just written the person out.

The knower

Chapter 18 is a hundred verses, a third of the text, and the description of the man who is free while still alive. Nityaswarupananda compares it to the eighteenth chapter of the Bhagavad Gītā, a summing-up, and observes that the language could only come from someone who had lived through it. It is also where every objection to the book gets its answer, and where the answers are hardest to tell apart from evasions.

यस्य बोधोदये तावत्स्वप्नवद् भवति भ्रमः ।
तस्मै सुखैकरूपाय नमः शान्ताय तेजसे ॥ १ ॥
yasya bodhodaye tāvatsvapnavad bhavati bhramaḥ
tasmai sukhaikarūpāya namaḥ śāntāya tejase

18.1Salutation to that which is bliss by nature, stillness and light, and at whose dawning every delusion becomes like a dream.

अर्जयित्वाखिलान् अर्थान् भोगानाप्नोति पुष्कलान् ।
न हि सर्वपरित्यागमन्तरेण सुखी भवेत् ॥ २ ॥
arjayitvākhilān arthān bhogānāpnoti puṣkalān
na hi sarvaparityāgamantareṇa sukhī bhavet

18.2A man gains abundant enjoyments by gaining all worldly things, and yet without renouncing all he cannot be happy.

भवोऽयं भावनामात्रो न किञ्चित् परमार्थतः ।
नास्त्यभावः स्वभावानां भावाभावविभाविनाम् ॥ ४ ॥
bhavo'yaṃ bhāvanāmātro na kiñcit paramārthataḥ
nāstyabhāvaḥ svabhāvānāṃ bhāvābhāvavibhāvinām

18.4This world is only a state of mind. In truth it is nothing. Those self-existent beings that recognise being and not-being never cease to be.

The chapter opens with the book's only salutation, and it is offered not to a teacher or a god but to whatever it is at whose dawning delusion becomes like a dream. Verse 2 states the exchange the rest of the chapter will refuse: a man gets enjoyments by getting things, and cannot be happy without giving all of them up. Nityaswarupananda sets Bhartṛhari's list beside it, in which enjoyment carries the fear of disease, wealth the fear of kings, beauty the fear of age, and the body the fear of death.

व्यामोहमात्रविरतौ स्वरूपादानमात्रतः ।
वीतशोका विराजन्ते निरावरणदृष्टयः ॥ ६ ॥
vyāmohamātraviratau svarūpādānamātrataḥ
vītaśokā virājante nirāvaraṇadṛṣṭayaḥ

18.6No sooner does illusion cease and the self come into view than those whose sight is unveiled live with their sorrow gone.

स्वाराज्ये भैक्षवृत्तौ च लाभालाभे जने वने ।
निर्विकल्पस्वभावस्य न विशेषोऽस्ति योगिनः ॥ ११ ॥
svārājye bhaikṣavṛttau ca lābhālābhe jane vane
nirvikalpasvabhāvasya na viśeṣo'sti yoginaḥ

18.11Sovereignty in heaven or beggary, gain or loss, society or the forest: they make no difference to the yogi whose nature is free of conditions.

कृत्यं किमपि नैवास्ति न कापि हृदि रञ्जना ।
यथा जीवनमेवेह जीवन्मुक्तस्य योगिनः ॥ १३ ॥
kṛtyaṃ kimapi naivāsti na kāpi hṛdi rañjanā
yathā jīvanameveha jīvanmuktasya yoginaḥ

18.13The yogi liberated while living has no duty and no attachment at heart. His actions in this world belong only to the life of the body.

The thirteenth verse carries the doctrine that makes the rest of the chapter possible. Actions go on after knowledge because the body is already in motion, driven by what the tradition calls prārabdha, the portion of past action that produced this life and has to run out. The one who knows is not doing them. Nityaswarupananda is careful to say that the explanation belongs to the observer and not to the man described: from where he stands there is no body and no residue of action to work out.

येन दृष्टं परं ब्रह्म सोऽहं ब्रह्मेति चिन्तयेत् ।
किं चिन्तयति निश्चिन्तो द्वितीयं यो न पश्यति ॥ १६ ॥
yena dṛṣṭaṃ paraṃ brahma so'haṃ brahmeti cintayet
kiṃ cintayati niścinto dvitīyaṃ yo na paśyati

18.16He who has seen the supreme brahman meditates I am brahman. What is one to think who has gone past thought and sees no second?

निर्वासनो निरालम्बः स्वच्छन्दो मुक्तबन्धनः ।
क्षिप्तः संस्कारवातेन चेष्टते शुष्कपर्णवत् ॥ २१ ॥
nirvāsano nirālambaḥ svacchando muktabandhanaḥ
kṣiptaḥ saṃskāravātena ceṣṭate śuṣkaparṇavat

18.21Blown by the wind of his old impressions, the desireless man, without support, free and liberated, moves about like a dry leaf.

कुत्रापि न जिहासास्ति नाशो वापि न कुत्रचित् ।
आत्मारामस्य धीरस्य शीतलाच्छतरात्मनः ॥ २३ ॥
kutrāpi na jihāsāsti nāśo vāpi na kutracit
ātmārāmasya dhīrasya śītalācchatarātmanaḥ

18.23The wise man whose delight is in the self and whose mind is calm and pure has nowhere any wish to renounce and nowhere any sense of loss.

अतद्वादीव कुरुते न भवेदपि बालिशः ।
जीवन्मुक्तः सुखी श्रीमान् संसरन्नपि शोभते ॥ २६ ॥
atadvādīva kurute na bhavedapi bāliśaḥ
jīvanmuktaḥ sukhī śrīmān saṃsarannapi śobhate

18.26The man liberated in life acts like one who would not say that he acts; he is not for that reason a fool. Though in the world, he looks happy and blessed.

असमाधेरविक्षेपान् न मुमुक्षुर्न चेतरः ।
निश्चित्य कल्पितं पश्यन् ब्रह्मैवास्ते महाशयः ॥ २८ ॥
asamādheravikṣepān na mumukṣurna cetaraḥ
niścitya kalpitaṃ paśyan brahmaivāste mahāśayaḥ

18.28Beyond absorption and beyond distraction, the tranquil man neither seeks liberation nor its opposite. Having settled that the world is a figment, he exists as brahman even while seeing it.

The sixteenth verse is the finest piece of reasoning in the text. The sentence ahaṃ brahmāsmi, I am brahman, belongs to a man who has seen brahman, which means it belongs to a man for whom there are still two things, himself and what he has seen. The sentence is therefore evidence that the work is not finished. Nothing replaces it, because the state it points at has nobody left in it to say anything. The dry leaf of verse 21 is the image the chapter is remembered for, and the wind is not fate but habit: the momentum of impressions already laid down.

तत्त्वं यथार्थमाकर्ण्य मन्दः प्राप्नोति मूढताम् ।
अथवा याति सङ्कोचममूढः कोऽपि मूढवत् ॥ ३२ ॥
tattvaṃ yathārthamākarṇya mandaḥ prāpnoti mūḍhatām
athavā yāti saṅkocamamūḍhaḥ ko'pi mūḍhavat

18.32A dull-witted man is bewildered on hearing the real truth; some sharp-witted man withdraws within himself and looks like the dull one.

एकाग्रता निरोधो वा मूढैरभ्यस्यते भृशम् ।
धीराः कृत्यं न पश्यन्ति सुप्तवत्स्वपदे स्थिताः ॥ ३३ ॥
ekāgratā nirodho vā mūḍhairabhyasyate bhṛśam
dhīrāḥ kṛtyaṃ na paśyanti suptavatsvapade sthitāḥ

18.33The ignorant constantly practise concentration and control of the mind. The wise, resting in the self like men asleep, find nothing to be done.

अप्रयत्नात् प्रयत्नाद् वा मूढो नाप्नोति निर्वृतिम् ।
तत्त्वनिश्चयमात्रेण प्राज्ञो भवति निर्वृतः ॥ ३४ ॥
aprayatnāt prayatnād vā mūḍho nāpnoti nirvṛtim
tattvaniścayamātreṇa prājño bhavati nirvṛtaḥ

18.34The ignorant man attains peace neither by inaction nor by action. The wise man becomes happy merely by ascertaining the truth.

नाप्नोति कर्मणा मोक्षं विमूढोऽभ्यासरूपिणा ।
धन्यो विज्ञानमात्रेण मुक्तस्तिष्ठत्यविक्रियः ॥ ३६ ॥
nāpnoti karmaṇā mokṣaṃ vimūḍho'bhyāsarūpiṇā
dhanyo vijñānamātreṇa muktastiṣṭhatyavikriyaḥ

18.36The ignorant man does not attain liberation by repeated practice. The blessed one stands free by knowledge alone, devoid of all such activity.

मूढो नाप्नोति तद् ब्रह्म यतो भवितुमिच्छति ।
अनिच्छन्नपि धीरो हि परब्रह्मस्वरूपभाक् ॥ ३७ ॥
mūḍho nāpnoti tad brahma yato bhavitumicchati
anicchannapi dhīro hi parabrahmasvarūpabhāk

18.37The ignorant man does not become brahman because he desires to. The wise man is of the nature of the supreme brahman without desiring anything.

न शान्तिं लभते मूढो यतः शमितुमिच्छति ।
धीरस्तत्त्वं विनिश्चित्य सर्वदा शान्तमानसः ॥ ३९ ॥
na śāntiṃ labhate mūḍho yataḥ śamitumicchati
dhīrastattvaṃ viniścitya sarvadā śāntamānasaḥ

18.39The fool desires peace and so does not attain it. The wise man has ascertained the truth and is ever of tranquil mind.

Six verses in a row set the fool against the wise man, and they are the text's argument against effortful practice put six ways. Wanting a state is evidence of not having it and is therefore a way of keeping it away; the one who has it never wanted it. Stated so, the position is airtight and useless, and the text knows this, because sixty verses later it says the opposite about the same man.

भावस्य भावकः कश्चिन् न किञ्चिद् भावकोपरः ।
उभयाभावकः कश्चिद् एवमेव निराकुलः ॥ ४२ ॥
bhāvasya bhāvakaḥ kaścin na kiñcid bhāvakoparaḥ
ubhayābhāvakaḥ kaścid evameva nirākulaḥ

18.42One man conceives being, another conceives nothing at all, and one conceives neither. That last is the undisturbed one.

विषयद्वीपिनो वीक्ष्य चकिताः शरणार्थिनः ।
विशन्ति झटिति क्रोडं निरोधैकाग्रसिद्धये ॥ ४५ ॥
viṣayadvīpino vīkṣya cakitāḥ śaraṇārthinaḥ
viśanti jhaṭiti kroḍaṃ nirodhaikāgrasiddhaye

18.45Seeing the tigers of sense-objects, the frightened ones at once run to a cave for the practice of control and concentration.

निर्वासनं हरिं दृष्ट्वा तूष्णीं विषयदन्तिनः ।
पलायन्ते न शक्तास्ते सेवन्ते कृतचाटवः ॥ ४६ ॥
nirvāsanaṃ hariṃ dṛṣṭvā tūṣṇīṃ viṣayadantinaḥ
palāyante na śaktāste sevante kṛtacāṭavaḥ

18.46Seeing the desireless lion, the elephants of sense-objects quietly slip away, and, unable to escape, attend on him like flatterers.

निवृत्तिरपि मूढस्य प्रवृत्ति रुपजायते ।
प्रवृत्तिरपि धीरस्य निवृत्तिफलभागिनी ॥ ६१ ॥
nivṛttirapi mūḍhasya pravṛtti rupajāyate
pravṛttirapi dhīrasya nivṛttiphalabhāginī

18.61With the deluded man, even inaction becomes action; with the wise man, even action results in the fruit of inaction.

निरोधादीनि कर्माणि जहाति जडधीर्यदि ।
मनोरथान् प्रलापांश्च कर्तुमाप्नोत्यतत्क्षणात् ॥ ७५ ॥
nirodhādīni karmāṇi jahāti jaḍadhīryadi
manorathān pralāpāṃśca kartumāpnotyatatkṣaṇāt

18.75No sooner does the man of dull intellect give up his practice of controlling the mind than he becomes prey to fancies and desires.

The forty-second verse is the one place where the poet pushes off from Buddhism by name of doctrine if not of school. Byrom reads it as a rejection of the Madhyamaka position: the one who knows asserts neither the existence nor the emptiness of the self. Verses 45 and 46 are the book's best pair of images and its clearest statement of what it thinks the senses are. Tigers, to a frightened man, and elephants who cannot get away, to a man with nothing to take from him. And verse 75, twenty-nine verses later, says the thing the whole chapter has been denying: drop the practice and the desires come straight back.

न स्वर्गो नैव नरको जीवन्मुक्तिर्न चैव हि ।
बहुनात्र किमुक्तेन योगदृष्ट्या न किञ्चन ॥ ८० ॥
na svargo naiva narako jīvanmuktirna caiva hi
bahunātra kimuktena yogadṛṣṭyā na kiñcana

18.80There is no heaven and no hell, and not even liberation while living. In short, in a yogi's vision nothing at all exists.

सुप्तोऽपि न सुषुप्तौ च स्वप्नेऽपि शयितो न च ।
जागरेऽपि न जागर्ति धीरस्तृप्तः पदे पदे ॥ ९४ ॥
supto'pi na suṣuptau ca svapne'pi śayito na ca
jāgare'pi na jāgarti dhīrastṛptaḥ pade pade

18.94Not asleep even in deep sleep, not lying down even in dream, and not awake even in waking, is the wise man contented in every condition.

न धावति जनाकीर्णं नारण्यमुपशान्तधीः ।
यथातथा यत्रतत्र सम एवावतिष्ठते ॥ १०० ॥
na dhāvati janākīrṇaṃ nāraṇyamupaśāntadhīḥ
yathātathā yatratatra sama evāvatiṣṭhate

18.100The man whose mind is at peace runs neither to a crowded place nor to the forest. He remains the same in whatever condition and in whatever place.

The eightieth verse withdraws the chapter's own subject. Jīvanmukti, liberation while living, has been described for eighty verses and is now denied along with heaven and hell, because it too is a state and states belong to somebody. The ninety-fourth takes the three states of waking, dream and deep sleep, which the Upaniṣads use as the standard analysis of a person, and empties all three at once. The chapter ends not on an achievement but on an absence of preference: the crowd and the forest are the two places a man might go to become something, and he goes to neither.

Chapters 19 and 20: the last word is Janaka's

तत्त्वविज्ञानसन्दंशमादाय हृदयोदरात् ।
नानाविधपरामर्शशल्योद्धारः कृतो मया ॥ १ ॥
tattvavijñānasandaṃśamādāya hṛdayodarāt
nānāvidhaparāmarśaśalyoddhāraḥ kṛto mayā

19.1With the pincers of the knowledge of truth I have extracted from the inmost recess of my heart the thorn of one opinion after another.

क्व धर्मः क्व च वा कामः क्व चार्थः क्व विवेकिता ।
क्व द्वैतं क्व च वाऽद्वैतं स्वमहिम्नि स्थितस्य मे ॥ २ ॥
kva dharmaḥ kva ca vā kāmaḥ kva cārthaḥ kva vivekitā
kva dvaitaṃ kva ca vā'dvaitaṃ svamahimni sthitasya me

19.2For me, abiding in my own glory, where is duty, where is pleasure, where is gain, where is discrimination, where is duality, where even is non-duality?

क्व स्वप्नः क्व सुषुप्तिर्वा क्व च जागरणं तथा ।
क्व तुरीयं भयं वापि स्वमहिम्नि स्थितस्य मे ॥ ५ ॥
kva svapnaḥ kva suṣuptirvā kva ca jāgaraṇaṃ tathā
kva turīyaṃ bhayaṃ vāpi svamahimni sthitasya me

19.5For me, abiding in my own glory, where is dream, where deep sleep, where waking, where the fourth state, and where even fear?

अलं त्रिवर्गकथया योगस्य कथयाप्यलम् ।
अलं विज्ञानकथया विश्रान्तस्य ममात्मनि ॥ ८ ॥
alaṃ trivargakathayā yogasya kathayāpyalam
alaṃ vijñānakathayā viśrāntasya mamātmani

19.8For me, reposing in the self, there is no need to speak of the three ends of life, no need to speak of yoga, no need to speak of knowledge.

The nineteenth chapter is eight verses of a single surgical image. The knowledge of the truth is a pair of pincers, and what it draws out of the heart is a thorn, one opinion after another; doubts and arguments are treated as a foreign body and not as steps towards anything. Verse 5 disposes in half a line of the analysis the Upaniṣads use for a person. Waking, dream and deep sleep go, and turīya, the fourth, the name the tradition gives to what is left when the three are seen through, goes with them.

क्व भूतानि क्व देहो वा क्वेन्द्रियाणि क्व वा मनः ।
क्व शून्यं क्व च नैराश्यं मत्स्वरूपे निरञ्जने ॥ १ ॥
kva bhūtāni kva deho vā kvendriyāṇi kva vā manaḥ
kva śūnyaṃ kva ca nairāśyaṃ matsvarūpe nirañjane

20.1Where in me, who am taintless, are the elements, the body, the senses, the mind, the void, or despair?

क्व शास्त्रं क्वात्मविज्ञानं क्व वा निर्विषयं मनः ।
क्व तृप्तिः क्व वितृष्णात्वं गतद्वन्द्वस्य मे सदा ॥ २ ॥
kva śāstraṃ kvātmavijñānaṃ kva vā nirviṣayaṃ manaḥ
kva tṛptiḥ kva vitṛṣṇātvaṃ gatadvandvasya me sadā

20.2For me, who am ever without the sense of duality, where is scripture, where knowledge of the self, where a mind emptied of objects, where contentment, where the absence of desire?

क्व विद्या क्व च वाविद्या क्वाहं क्वेदं मम क्व वा ।
क्व बन्ध क्व च वा मोक्षः स्वरूपस्य क्व रूपिता ॥ ३ ॥
kva vidyā kva ca vāvidyā kvāhaṃ kvedaṃ mama kva vā
kva bandha kva ca vā mokṣaḥ svarūpasya kva rūpitā

20.3Where is knowledge or ignorance, where I or this or mine, where bondage or liberation, where any mark of what the self is?

क्व प्रारब्धानि कर्माणि जीवन्मुक्तिरपि क्व वा ।
क्व तद् विदेहकैवल्यं निर्विशेषस्य सर्वदा ॥ ४ ॥
kva prārabdhāni karmāṇi jīvanmuktirapi kva vā
kva tad videhakaivalyaṃ nirviśeṣasya sarvadā

20.4For the ever undivided, what are the actions already begun, what is liberation while living, and what is that liberation at death?

क्व कर्ता क्व च वा भोक्ता निष्क्रियं स्फुरणं क्व वा ।
क्वापरोक्षं फलं वा क्व निःस्वभावस्य मे सदा ॥ ५ ॥
kva kartā kva ca vā bhoktā niṣkriyaṃ sphuraṇaṃ kva vā
kvāparokṣaṃ phalaṃ vā kva niḥsvabhāvasya me sadā

20.5For me, who am ever impersonal, where is a doer or an enjoyer, the ceasing or the rising of thought, immediate knowledge or its fruit?

क्व लोकं क्व मुमुक्षुर्वा क्व योगी ज्ञानवान् क्व वा ।
क्व बद्धः क्व च वा मुक्तः स्वस्वरूपेऽहमद्वये ॥ ६ ॥
kva lokaṃ kva mumukṣurvā kva yogī jñānavān kva vā
kva baddhaḥ kva ca vā muktaḥ svasvarūpe'hamadvaye

20.6For me, whose nature is undivided, where is the world, where the seeker of liberation, where the contemplative, where the knower, where the bound man, where the free?

क्व सृष्टिः क्व च संहारः क्व साध्यं क्व च साधनम् ।
क्व साधकः क्व सिद्धिर्वा स्वस्वरूपेऽहमद्वये ॥ ७ ॥
kva sṛṣṭiḥ kva ca saṃhāraḥ kva sādhyaṃ kva ca sādhanam
kva sādhakaḥ kva siddhirvā svasvarūpe'hamadvaye

20.7For me, resting in my undivided nature, where is projection or withdrawal, where end or means, where seeker or success?

क्व प्रमाता प्रमाणं वा क्व प्रमेयं क्व च प्रमा ।
क्व किञ्चित् क्व न किञ्चिद् वा सर्वदा विमलस्य मे ॥ ८ ॥
kva pramātā pramāṇaṃ vā kva prameyaṃ kva ca pramā
kva kiñcit kva na kiñcid vā sarvadā vimalasya me

20.8For me, who am ever pure, where is a knower, a means of knowing, a thing known, or knowledge; where is anything and where is nothing?

The last two chapters are a single figure of speech repeated forty times: kva, where. Byrom notes that the Sanskrit asks where rather than what, which makes the questions locative rather than definitional — not what is bondage, but where would you put it. The list is exhaustive on purpose. The three ends of life go, then the four states, then the elements and the senses, then scripture and self-knowledge, then bondage and liberation, then liberation while living and liberation at death, then knower and known and knowing. Nityaswarupananda's note on verse 20.4 is the sharpest thing in his commentary: the author is refuting the very idea of liberation while living, which everywhere else in Vedānta is the goal, because it presupposes both life and bondage.

क्व विक्षेपः क्व चैकाग्र्यं क्व निर्बोधः क्व मूढता ।
क्व हर्षः क्व विषादो वा सर्वदा निष्क्रियस्य मे ॥ ९ ॥
kva vikṣepaḥ kva caikāgryaṃ kva nirbodhaḥ kva mūḍhatā
kva harṣaḥ kva viṣādo vā sarvadā niṣkriyasya me

20.9For me, who am ever actionless, where is distraction or concentration, dullness or delusion, joy or sorrow?

क्व चैष व्यवहारो वा क्व च सा परमार्थता ।
क्व सुखं क्व च वा दुखं निर्विमर्शस्य मे सदा ॥ १० ॥
kva caiṣa vyavahāro vā kva ca sā paramārthatā
kva sukhaṃ kva ca vā dukhaṃ nirvimarśasya me sadā

20.10For me, who am ever free of mental activity, where is the relative or what transcends it, where happiness or misery?

क्व माया क्व च संसारः क्व प्रीतिर्विरतिः क्व वा ।
क्व जीवः क्व च तद्ब्रह्म सर्वदा विमलस्य मे ॥ ११ ॥
kva māyā kva ca saṃsāraḥ kva prītirviratiḥ kva vā
kva jīvaḥ kva ca tadbrahma sarvadā vimalasya me

20.11For me, who am ever pure, where is illusion or the world, attachment or detachment, the separate creature or brahman?

The last verses take away the terms in which the removing has been described. Distraction and concentration go together in verse 9, and dullness with its opposite; verse 10 removes the pair that the whole of Vedānta is written in, the relative and what transcends it. Verse 11 is the one that costs most. Jīva and brahman, the separate creature and the absolute, are the two ends of the identity the tradition exists to teach, and both are asked where they are.

क्व प्रवृत्तिर्निर्वृत्तिर्वा क्व मुक्तिः क्व च बन्धनम् ।
कूटस्थनिर्विभागस्य स्वस्थस्य मम सर्वदा ॥ १२ ॥
kva pravṛttirnirvṛttirvā kva muktiḥ kva ca bandhanam
kūṭasthanirvibhāgasya svasthasya mama sarvadā

20.12For me, who am ever immutable and undivided and established in the self, where is activity or inactivity, liberation or bondage?

क्वोपदेशः क्व वा शास्त्रं क्व शिष्यः क्व च वा गुरुः ।
क्व चास्ति पुरुषार्थो वा निरुपाधेः शिवस्य मे ॥ १३ ॥
kvopadeśaḥ kva vā śāstraṃ kva śiṣyaḥ kva ca vā guruḥ
kva cāsti puruṣārtho vā nirupādheḥ śivasya me

20.13For me, who am free of limitation and am the absolute good, where is instruction or scripture, where a disciple or a teacher, where the goal of life?

क्व चास्ति क्व च वा नास्ति क्वास्ति चैकं क्व च द्वयम् ।
बहुनात्र किमुक्तेन किञ्चिन्नोत्तिष्ठते मम ॥ १४ ॥
kva cāsti kva ca vā nāsti kvāsti caikaṃ kva ca dvayam
bahunātra kimuktena kiñcinnottiṣṭhate mama

20.14Where is being and where is not-being, where is one and where is two? What more is there to say? Nothing whatever arises from me.

The thirteenth verse dissolves the two people who have been speaking. Once teacher and pupil have gone, the text has removed its own occasion, and the fourteenth stops. Byrom takes the final compound of the chapter, śivasya me, which can be read adjectivally as of me who am blessed, and renders it as a declaration: I am Shiva. He gives his reason — the poet was almost certainly a Śaiva, and at this point had nothing left to compromise.

Since about the eighth century

Nobody knows when the Aṣṭāvakra Gītā was written or by whom, and the estimates span more than a thousand years. Indian editors have generally placed it beside the Bhagavad Gītā or just before it, in the fourth or fifth century BCE, on the strength of the shared title, the shared metre and the shared setting; Nityaswarupananda takes that line, calls the work older than Gauḍapāda's Kārikās, and finds in it the germs of the later extreme theories. Brockington, writing in 1990, rejects the dating outright. The Advaita of the text has undergone a long development and must at the least be later than Śaṅkara; doctrinally it sits close to Sadānanda's Vedāntasāra and Vidyāraṇya's Jīvanmuktiviveka of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and in the way it borrows an epic setting to carry a monistic argument it resembles the Yogavāsiṣṭha, itself a twelfth- or thirteenth-century recasting of the Rāmāyaṇa. Byrom, who translated it, split the difference and guessed at either the eighth century, under a follower of Śaṅkara, or the fourteenth, during the revival of his teaching. The text gives no purchase for anything better. Sanskrit after Pāṇini changed too little to be dated by its language, and the poet took care to leave no trace of himself; the one thing that can be said with confidence about the man who wrote under the name Aṣṭāvakra is that he was not Aṣṭāvakra.

What the text did have was an afterlife. Manuscripts are scattered through European libraries — nine in the Bodleian alone, two of them older by their dates than any Giussani or Hauschild used — and a collation of them shows almost no variation in the verses, and some in the titles given to the chapters. Carlo Giussani published the first translation into a European language at Florence in 1868, from three manuscripts at Tübingen and St Petersburg; Richard Hauschild's German edition followed in 1967. The modern Indian life of the book begins in Ramakrishna's room at Dakshineswar. Nityaswarupananda's introduction records that Ramakrishna kept a copy there and would ask the young Narendranath to read it aloud; after a verse or two Narendra would refuse, saying it was a sin to call man God, and Ramakrishna would answer that he was not asking him to believe it, only to read it. The man who refused became Vivekananda, and the argument of the book — that the self and brahman are not two, and that this is not a doctrine to be believed but a fact to be seen — became the argument he took abroad. Brockington notes the tradition independently and treats it as the measure of the text's standing among modern Vedāntins. Sri Ramanasramam has kept it in print, in an edition whose Sanskrit verses are reproduced in Ramana Maharshi's own handwriting. Byrom received his copy from his own teacher, and reports that Neem Karoli Baba called it "the purest of scriptures."

Nityaswarupananda's translation, serialised month by month in Prabuddha Bharata from 1929 to 1931 and then revised for Advaita Ashrama's book edition, is still the one to start from: every verse in Sanskrit, then word by word, then in English, then a note that says what a reader of Advaita would need to be told. Byrom's version of 1990 goes the other way. He removed the dialogue entirely on the ground that there is no real drama in it and only one voice is ever heard, broke the ślokas into short free-verse lines, and produced something that reads as lyric rather than instruction; Brockington, who emended it throughout, called the result the first English version to catch the freshness and directness of the original, and wrote in the same foreword that the text has with good reason been called "the outpourings of a realized individual." Read together the two translations bracket the problem the book presents. Taken as instruction it is close to unusable, since every instruction it gives is withdrawn within a few verses; taken as report it is unanswerable, because a report is not obliged to tell anyone how to get there. What has survived best is the definition in chapter 8 — bondage is the mind wanting and refusing, liberation is the mind doing neither — which does not depend on the metaphysics around it, and the observation of 16.3 that people are unhappy because they exert themselves and do not know it. What has aged is the certainty that the world is nothing, which the text asserts and never argues, and the portrait of the sage in chapter 18, which is drawn with such completeness that it leaves a reader no way to tell the free man from the merely idle one. The poet saw that objection coming and answered it twice, once by saying that only those like him can know his state, and once, at verse 75, by warning that whoever drops his practice without having arrived will be taken straight back by his desires.

Ashtavakra says announcing I am brahman proves you are not finished

Ashtavakra 18.16 argues that if you can say “I am brahman”, the work is not done, because the sentence still needs two things: the person saying it and the thing he has seen.

Becauseit is the sharpest piece of reasoning in the text and it is reasoning, not evidence; nothing is offered to replace the sentence, because the state it points at has nobody left in it to say anything
LimitsThe argument proves too much: by it, every sentence in the text is evidence that its author had not arrived. Hofstadter takes the opposite view, that the self-referential utterance is the phenomenon rather than the obstacle.
PracticeDistrust your own reports of having understood something; the fluent account is produced by the part that did not.
After AIA model will produce the sentence on request, with the same fluency and none of the seeing. That is the cleanest demonstration the verse could have asked for: the utterance is available without anything behind it.

The Ashtavakra Gita describes the liberated man so fully that nothing in the description separates him from someone who is simply idle. Both look the same from the outside.

Becausethe text concedes the objection twice and answers it neither time, which is what makes the claim contested rather than settled
LimitsThe Bhagavad Gītā keeps a test in conduct, and Kegan keeps one in what a person can hold as object, so the objection is answerable in principle — just not from inside this text. The portrait is a report of a state, and a report is not obliged to be a diagnostic.

The Ashtavakra Gita defines bondage as the mind wanting or refusing

The Ashtavakra Gita says bondage is simply the mind wanting something or pushing something away, and freedom is the mind doing neither. Neither one is a condition of the world or a rank you reach after years of effort.

Becauseit is a definition the text asserts and never argues, and duration does not enter it, because the mind either is moving that way now or is not
LimitsA definition cannot be false, only useless, and this one gives no way to tell the free man from the merely indifferent. The text itself concedes at 18.75 that whoever drops his practice without having arrived is taken straight back by his desires.

After AIThe definition survives the change of object. Wanting the model's answer to be right, and refusing to find that it is wrong, are the two directions of the same movement, and they are what makes a review fail.

The Ashtavakra Gita says effort causes unhappiness and nobody notices

The Ashtavakra Gita says effort is what makes people unhappy, not what cures it, and that nobody notices, because working harder is exactly what people do when they are unhappy.

Becausethe verse states it flatly — all are unhappy because all exert themselves, and nobody knows it — with no argument beneath it; Byrom's gloss names the target as a strenuous indolence, the habit of substituting effort for understanding
LimitsSelf-determination theory finds competence and autonomy needs that effort is how people meet, and the job-crafting literature finds the same, so the claim cannot be general. It is a corrective to effort that has stopped being informative, not an account of effort.
PracticeWhen the response to a bad week is to work harder at the same thing, treat the extra effort as a symptom to be examined before it is spent.
After AIThe strenuous indolence the verse names is now cheap to buy: a machine will produce the appearance of exertion on demand. The question the verse asks — whether the effort is doing anything — has become the only useful one to ask about a day's output.

The Ashtavakra Gita says practice is another chain because you never acted

The Ashtavakra Gita says there is nothing to do, because you never acted in the first place. Trying to meditate your way to freedom is itself one more chain.

The Ashtavakra Gita says that understanding does not stop a person acting. It removes the sense that someone is doing the acting, so the person watches his own body work as if it belonged to somebody else.

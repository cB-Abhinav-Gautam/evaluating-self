57सतिपट्ठानसुत्त · Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta

Majjhima Nikāya 10, Pali canon · translations by Bhikkhu Sujato (2018), Ñāṇamoli and Bodhi (1995) and Thanissaro Bhikkhu (2008)

Linked to 19 chapters: supports 8 · contradicts 8 · mechanism 5 · same claim 6 · pairing 2

The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta is a list, and the list is the argument. The Buddha is at a market town of the Kuru people, and he opens by saying that there is one way to the purification of beings and that it is the four establishings of mindfulness: observing body as body, feelings as feelings, mind as mind, dhammas as dhammas. He then works through fourteen exercises on the body, one on feelings, one on the mind and five on dhammas, and after every single one of them repeats the same five-clause refrain without changing a word. The exercises are not alike — a man attending to the length of his breath, a man noticing which way his limbs are pointing, a man reciting the thirty-one parts of his own body, a man looking at a corpse in each of nine stages of decay — and the refrain is what makes them one practice rather than twenty-one. The discourse ends with a guarantee: seven years of this, or seven months, or seven days, and one of two results follows. It is the most widely taught meditation text in the world, and what it actually asks for is narrower and harder than the practice that now carries its name.

At Kammāsadhamma

एवं मे सुतं—
एकं समयं भगवा कुरूसु विहरति कम्मासधम्मं नाम कुरूनं निगमो।
तत्र खो भगवा भिक्खू आमन्तेसि:
“भिक्खवो”ति।
“भदन्ते”ति ते भिक्खू भगवतो पच्चस्सोसुं।
भगवा एतदवोच:
Evaṁ me sutaṁ—
ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā kurūsu viharati kammāsadhammaṁ nāma kurūnaṁ nigamo.
Tatra kho bhagavā bhikkhū āmantesi:
“bhikkhavo”ti.
“Bhadante”ti te bhikkhū bhagavato paccassosuṁ.
Bhagavā etadavoca:

1So I have heard. At one time the Blessed One was staying among the Kurus, at a market town of theirs named Kammāsadhamma. There he addressed the monks: “Monks.” “Venerable sir,” they replied. The Blessed One said this.

The Kuru country lay north-west of the Ganges plain, around what is now Delhi and Haryana, and it is where the canon puts several of its longest and most technical discourses: this one, its expanded twin in the Dīgha collection, and the long discourse on dependent origination. Buddhaghosa's commentary explains the concentration by saying that the climate there suited body and mind and that the Kurus were fit to receive deep teaching, an explanation that says more about the commentary's habits than about the Kurus. Nothing else in the frame is personal. There is no named questioner, no incident that prompts the talk, no individual addressed. The discourse is delivered to the assembly as a whole and it never returns to the setting.

“एकायनो अयं, भिक्खवे, मग्गो सत्तानं विसुद्धिया, सोकपरिदेवानं समतिक्कमाय, दुक्खदोमनस्सानं अत्थङ्गमाय, ञायस्स अधिगमाय, निब्बानस्स सच्छिकिरियाय, यदिदं चत्तारो सतिपट्ठाना।
“Ekāyano ayaṁ, bhikkhave, maggo sattānaṁ visuddhiyā, sokaparidevānaṁ samatikkamāya, dukkhadomanassānaṁ atthaṅgamāya, ñāyassa adhigamāya, nibbānassa sacchikiriyāya, yadidaṁ cattāro satipaṭṭhānā.

2Monks, this is the one-going path for the purification of beings, for the surmounting of sorrow and lamentation, for the disappearance of pain and dejection, for the attainment of the method, for the realising of nibbāna — namely, the four establishings of mindfulness.

Everything contested about the discourse is already in the first sentence. Ekāyano maggo is a compound of eka, one, and ayana, a going or a way, and it can be read as the only path, the path that goes to one destination, the path travelled alone, or the path that converges. Soma Thera's translation of 1941 made it the only way, and the exclusive reading is the one that travelled: for most of the twentieth century the sentence was quoted as a claim that no other method reaches the goal. Ñāṇamoli and Bodhi print the direct path, and so does Thanissaro Bhikkhu; Sujato prints the path to convergence. The philological objection to the exclusive reading is that the same compound appears elsewhere in the same collection describing a track that leads down into a pit, where only a destination is at issue and no claim of uniqueness could be meant. Anālayo, whose 2003 study is the standard book-length treatment, takes the direct path as the best fit and points out that the commentary itself lists several readings rather than settling on one.

The second word is no easier. Satipaṭṭhāna resolves either as sati plus upaṭṭhāna, the establishing or setting-up of mindfulness, or as sati plus paṭṭhāna, a foundation of mindfulness. The Sanskrit of the northern parallels reads smṛtyupasthāna, which settles the grammar in favour of the first: the text is about establishing an attention, not about four objects that serve as its foundations. And sati itself is the Pali of Sanskrit smṛti, whose ordinary sense is memory. Thomas William Rhys Davids chose mindfulness for it in 1881, and every English version since has kept the word; the sense of holding something in mind, of not letting it drop, is closer to the Pali than the sense of attending closely to what is in front of you.

कतमे चत्तारो?
इध, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति आतापी सम्पजानो सतिमा, विनेय्य लोके अभिज्झादोमनस्सं;
वेदनासु वेदनानुपस्सी विहरति आतापी सम्पजानो सतिमा, विनेय्य लोके अभिज्झादोमनस्सं;
चित्ते चित्तानुपस्सी विहरति आतापी सम्पजानो सतिमा, विनेय्य लोके अभिज्झादोमनस्सं;
धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति आतापी सम्पजानो सतिमा, विनेय्य लोके अभिज्झादोमनस्सं।
उद्देसो निट्ठितो।
Katame cattāro?
Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu kāye kāyānupassī viharati ātāpī sampajāno satimā, vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ;
vedanāsu vedanānupassī viharati ātāpī sampajāno satimā, vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ;
citte cittānupassī viharati ātāpī sampajāno satimā, vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ;
dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati ātāpī sampajāno satimā, vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ.
Uddeso niṭṭhito.

3What four? Here a monk dwells observing body as body — ardent, clearly comprehending, mindful, having removed longing and aversion towards the world. He dwells observing feelings as feelings — ardent, clearly comprehending, mindful, having removed longing and aversion towards the world. He dwells observing mind as mind, with the same three qualities and the same removal. He dwells observing dhammas as dhammas, with the same three qualities and the same removal. Here ends the summary.

The doubled locative — kāye kāyānupassī, observing body in the body — is strange in Pali as it is in English, and the strangeness is doing work. The instruction is not to think about the body but to observe the body under the description of body, without letting it become someone's body, or an attractive body, or my body. The same restriction applies four times over. Three qualities are named and none of them is neutral watching. Ātāpī is from ātāpa, heat, and means ardent in the sense of burning at something; sampajāno is clear comprehension, a discriminating knowledge of what one is doing; satimā is the remembering already described. Then a participle that is usually skipped in summaries: vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ, having removed longing and aversion towards the world. Covetousness and displeasure are to be put away before the observing starts, or as part of it. This is not an exercise in accepting whatever arises.

Observing the body

कथञ्च, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति?
इध, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु अरञ्ञगतो वा रुक्खमूलगतो वा सुञ्ञागारगतो वा निसीदति, पल्लङ्कं आभुजित्वा, उजुं कायं पणिधाय, परिमुखं सतिं उपट्ठपेत्वा।
Kathañca, bhikkhave, bhikkhu kāye kāyānupassī viharati?
Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu araññagato vā rukkhamūlagato vā suññāgāragato vā nisīdati, pallaṅkaṁ ābhujitvā, ujuṁ kāyaṁ paṇidhāya, parimukhaṁ satiṁ upaṭṭhapetvā.

4And how does a monk dwell observing body as body? Here a monk, gone to a forest or to the root of a tree or to an empty hut, sits down, folds his legs crosswise, holds his body upright, and establishes mindfulness in front of him.

सो सतोव अस्ससति, सतोव पस्ससति।
दीघं वा अस्ससन्तो ‘दीघं अस्ससामी’ति पजानाति, दीघं वा पस्ससन्तो ‘दीघं पस्ससामी’ति पजानाति,
रस्सं वा अस्ससन्तो ‘रस्सं अस्ससामी’ति पजानाति, रस्सं वा पस्ससन्तो ‘रस्सं पस्ससामी’ति पजानाति।
So satova assasati, satova passasati.
Dīghaṁ vā assasanto ‘dīghaṁ assasāmī’ti pajānāti, dīghaṁ vā passasanto ‘dīghaṁ passasāmī’ti pajānāti,
rassaṁ vā assasanto ‘rassaṁ assasāmī’ti pajānāti, rassaṁ vā passasanto ‘rassaṁ passasāmī’ti pajānāti.

5Just mindful he breathes in, just mindful he breathes out. Breathing in long he knows “I am breathing in long”; breathing out long he knows “I am breathing out long.” Breathing in short he knows “I am breathing in short”; breathing out short he knows “I am breathing out short.”

‘सब्बकायपटिसंवेदी अस्ससिस्सामी’ति सिक्खति, ‘सब्बकायपटिसंवेदी पस्ससिस्सामी’ति सिक्खति।
‘पस्सम्भयं कायसङ्खारं अस्ससिस्सामी’ति सिक्खति, ‘पस्सम्भयं कायसङ्खारं पस्ससिस्सामी’ति सिक्खति।
‘Sabbakāyapaṭisaṁvedī assasissāmī’ti sikkhati, ‘sabbakāyapaṭisaṁvedī passasissāmī’ti sikkhati.
‘Passambhayaṁ kāyasaṅkhāraṁ assasissāmī’ti sikkhati, ‘passambhayaṁ kāyasaṅkhāraṁ passasissāmī’ti sikkhati.

6He trains: “Experiencing the whole body I shall breathe in”; he trains: “Experiencing the whole body I shall breathe out.” He trains: “Calming the bodily formation I shall breathe in”; he trains: “Calming the bodily formation I shall breathe out.”

The verb changes between the second step and the third, and the change is the whole design of the exercise. In the first two steps the verb is pajānāti, he knows: long breath is registered as long, short as short, and nothing is done about either. In the last two the verb is sikkhati, he trains, and the object is a future tense — I shall breathe in experiencing the whole body. The first half reports, the second half intends. A practice described entirely as bare registration would have kept one verb throughout, and this one does not. What sabbakāya, the whole body, refers to is disputed: the commentary takes it as the whole of the breath, its beginning, middle and end, while Ñāṇamoli and Bodhi and most modern teachers take it as the physical body entire, so that awareness widens from the nostrils to the whole frame.

सेय्यथापि, भिक्खवे, दक्खो भमकारो वा भमकारन्तेवासी वा दीघं वा अञ्छन्तो ‘दीघं अञ्छामी’ति पजानाति, रस्सं वा अञ्छन्तो ‘रस्सं अञ्छामी’ति पजानाति;
Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, dakkho bhamakāro vā bhamakārantevāsī vā dīghaṁ vā añchanto ‘dīghaṁ añchāmī’ti pajānāti, rassaṁ vā añchanto ‘rassaṁ añchāmī’ti pajānāti;

7Just as a skilled turner or a turner's apprentice, making a long pull, knows “I am making a long pull,” and making a short pull knows “I am making a short pull.” The Pali then repeats all four steps word for word with the monk in the turner's place.

The simile is about a craftsman at work, not about a watcher at rest. A lathe-turner pulling a cord knows the length of his own stroke while making it, and the knowing is part of the skill rather than a commentary on it. Every simile among the body exercises is drawn from a trade — a turner, a man sorting grain, a butcher — and in each the knowing belongs to someone who is doing something. The discourse could have compared the meditator to a spectator and does not.

इति अज्झत्तं वा काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति, बहिद्धा वा काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति, अज्झत्तबहिद्धा वा काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति;
समुदयधम्मानुपस्सी वा कायस्मिं विहरति, वयधम्मानुपस्सी वा कायस्मिं विहरति, समुदयवयधम्मानुपस्सी वा कायस्मिं विहरति।
‘अत्थि कायो’ति वा पनस्स सति पच्चुपट्ठिता होति। यावदेव ञाणमत्ताय पटिस्सतिमत्ताय अनिस्सितो च विहरति, न च किञ्चि लोके उपादियति।
एवम्पि खो, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति।
आनापानपब्बं निट्ठितं।
Iti ajjhattaṁ vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati, bahiddhā vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati, ajjhattabahiddhā vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati;
samudayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati, vayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati, samudayavayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati.
‘Atthi kāyo’ti vā panassa sati paccupaṭṭhitā hoti. Yāvadeva ñāṇamattāya paṭissatimattāya anissito ca viharati, na ca kiñci loke upādiyati.
Evampi kho, bhikkhave, bhikkhu kāye kāyānupassī viharati.
Ānāpānapabbaṁ niṭṭhitaṁ.

8So he dwells observing body as body internally, or he dwells observing body as body externally, or he dwells observing body as body both internally and externally. He dwells observing the nature of arising in the body, or the nature of vanishing in the body, or the nature of both arising and vanishing in the body. Or else mindfulness that “there is a body” is established in him just to the extent needed for bare knowing and bare remembering; and he dwells independent, clinging to nothing in the world. That too is how a monk dwells observing body as body. Here ends the section on breathing.

This paragraph is the discourse's engine and it is the part most often dropped. It follows every exercise, with only the object changing, and it asks for five things that the exercise itself has not asked for. Observe the object internally, then in another, then both ways: the commentary reads bahiddhā as another person's body, which turns a private exercise into an exercise in generalisation, while some modern readers take it as the outward aspect of one's own. Observe the object arising, then vanishing, then both: whatever was being attended to is now to be watched coming into existence and going out of it, which converts a steady object into a process. Then the third clause, which limits rather than extends. Mindfulness that there is a body is to be present yāvadeva, just so far as, and no further — enough for knowing and for remembering, and not enough to build anything on. And the last clause, anissito ca viharati, na ca kiñci loke upādiyati, he dwells independent and clings to nothing in the world, is the goal of the whole discourse stated in eight words after every one of its exercises.

पुन चपरं, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु गच्छन्तो वा ‘गच्छामी’ति पजानाति, ठितो वा ‘ठितोम्ही’ति पजानाति, निसिन्नो वा ‘निसिन्नोम्ही’ति पजानाति, सयानो वा ‘सयानोम्ही’ति पजानाति।
यथा यथा वा पनस्स कायो पणिहितो होति तथा तथा नं पजानाति।
Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu gacchanto vā ‘gacchāmī’ti pajānāti, ṭhito vā ‘ṭhitomhī’ti pajānāti, nisinno vā ‘nisinnomhī’ti pajānāti, sayāno vā ‘sayānomhī’ti pajānāti.
Yathā yathā vā panassa kāyo paṇihito hoti tathā tathā naṁ pajānāti.

9Further, when walking a monk knows “I am walking”; when standing he knows “I am standing”; when sitting he knows “I am sitting”; when lying down he knows “I am lying down.” However his body is disposed, so he knows it.

पुन चपरं, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु अभिक्कन्ते पटिक्कन्ते सम्पजानकारी होति, आलोकिते विलोकिते सम्पजानकारी होति, समिञ्जिते पसारिते सम्पजानकारी होति, सङ्घाटिपत्तचीवरधारणे सम्पजानकारी होति, असिते पीते खायिते सायिते सम्पजानकारी होति, उच्चारपस्सावकम्मे सम्पजानकारी होति, गते ठिते निसिन्ने सुत्ते जागरिते भासिते तुण्हीभावे सम्पजानकारी होति।
Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu abhikkante paṭikkante sampajānakārī hoti, ālokite vilokite sampajānakārī hoti, samiñjite pasārite sampajānakārī hoti, saṅghāṭipattacīvaradhāraṇe sampajānakārī hoti, asite pīte khāyite sāyite sampajānakārī hoti, uccārapassāvakamme sampajānakārī hoti, gate ṭhite nisinne sutte jāgarite bhāsite tuṇhībhāve sampajānakārī hoti.

10Further, a monk acts with clear comprehension when going forward and going back; when looking ahead and looking away; when bending and stretching his limbs; when carrying his outer robe, his bowl and his robes; when eating, drinking, chewing and tasting; when defecating and urinating; when walking, standing, sitting, falling asleep, waking, speaking and keeping silent.

इति अज्झत्तं वा काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति …पे…
एवम्पि खो, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति।
सम्पजानपब्बं निट्ठितं।
Iti ajjhattaṁ vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati …pe…
evampi kho, bhikkhave, bhikkhu kāye kāyānupassī viharati.
Sampajānapabbaṁ niṭṭhitaṁ.

11So he dwells observing body as body internally — and here the Pali writes pe, the canon's mark of elision, so that the whole refrain just given is to be said again. That too is how a monk dwells observing body as body. Here ends the section on clear comprehension.

The second and third exercises take the practice out of the sitting posture and into the day, and they are the reason this discourse rather than any other became the basis of a full-time training. The list of occasions in the third is deliberately unglamorous and ends with defecating, sleeping and keeping silent; nothing is exempted and nothing is ranked. What is asked is sampajāna, clear comprehension, and the commentarial tradition analyses it into four questions applied to each act: what is its purpose, is it suitable, does it fit the meditation, is there any delusion in it. That fourfold analysis is not in the discourse. What the discourse gives is the bare instruction that the act be done with clear comprehension, and the list.

पुन चपरं, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु इममेव कायं उद्धं पादतला, अधो केसमत्थका, तचपरियन्तं पूरं नानप्पकारस्स असुचिनो पच्चवेक्खति:
‘अत्थि इमस्मिं काये केसा लोमा नखा दन्ता तचो मंसं न्हारु अट्ठि अट्ठिमिञ्जं वक्कं हदयं यकनं किलोमकं पिहकं पप्फासं अन्तं अन्तगुणं उदरियं करीसं पित्तं सेम्हं पुब्बो लोहितं सेदो मेदो अस्सु वसा खेळो सिङ्घाणिका लसिका मुत्तन्’ति।
Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu imameva kāyaṁ uddhaṁ pādatalā, adho kesamatthakā, tacapariyantaṁ pūraṁ nānappakārassa asucino paccavekkhati:
‘atthi imasmiṁ kāye kesā lomā nakhā dantā taco maṁsaṁ nhāru aṭṭhi aṭṭhimiñjaṁ vakkaṁ hadayaṁ yakanaṁ kilomakaṁ pihakaṁ papphāsaṁ antaṁ antaguṇaṁ udariyaṁ karīsaṁ pittaṁ semhaṁ pubbo lohitaṁ sedo medo assu vasā kheḷo siṅghāṇikā lasikā muttan’ti.

12Further, a monk reviews this very body, upward from the soles of the feet and downward from the tips of the hair, bounded by skin and full of impurities of many kinds: “In this body there are head hairs, body hairs, nails, teeth, skin, flesh, sinews, bones, bone marrow, kidneys, heart, liver, membrane, spleen, lungs, large intestine, small intestine, stomach contents, excrement, bile, phlegm, pus, blood, sweat, fat, tears, grease, spittle, snot, joint fluid, urine.”

सेय्यथापि, भिक्खवे, उभतोमुखा पुतोळि पूरा नानाविहितस्स धञ्ञस्स, सेय्यथिदं—सालीनं वीहीनं मुग्गानं मासानं तिलानं तण्डुलानं। तमेनं चक्खुमा पुरिसो मुञ्चित्वा पच्चवेक्खेय्य: ‘इमे साली इमे वीही इमे मुग्गा इमे मासा इमे तिला इमे तण्डुला’ति।
Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, ubhatomukhā putoḷi pūrā nānāvihitassa dhaññassa, seyyathidaṁ—sālīnaṁ vīhīnaṁ muggānaṁ māsānaṁ tilānaṁ taṇḍulānaṁ. Tamenaṁ cakkhumā puriso muñcitvā paccavekkheyya: ‘ime sālī ime vīhī ime muggā ime māsā ime tilā ime taṇḍulā’ti.

13Just as if there were a sack open at both ends, filled with grains of various kinds — hill rice, red rice, mung beans, kidney beans, sesame, husked rice — and a man with good eyes were to open it and review the contents: “These are hill rice, these are red rice, these are mung beans, these are kidney beans, these are sesame, these are husked rice.”

The exercise is what it appears to be, and softening it makes it unintelligible. The body is to be gone through from the soles up and from the hair down, item by item, thirty-one items, and the text says outright that it is full of impurity of various kinds. Later tradition names the exercise attention to the repulsive. The list is not squeamish about order or category: hair and nails sit beside pus and urine, and the organs are named as organs. What is being attacked is the composite image — the body as a single attractive or shameful thing — and what replaces it is an inventory. The simile does not carry disgust at all, which Anālayo takes to be a point about the exercise: a man with good eyes opening a grain sack is discriminating, not revolted, and the operation asked for is sorting rather than loathing. The Chinese Madhyama-āgama parallel includes the same contemplation, so it is not a Theravāda addition.

पुन चपरं, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु इममेव कायं यथाठितं यथापणिहितं धातुसो पच्चवेक्खति:
‘अत्थि इमस्मिं काये पथवीधातु आपोधातु तेजोधातु वायोधातू’ति।
Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu imameva kāyaṁ yathāṭhitaṁ yathāpaṇihitaṁ dhātuso paccavekkhati:
‘atthi imasmiṁ kāye pathavīdhātu āpodhātu tejodhātu vāyodhātū’ti.

14Further, a monk reviews this very body, however it is placed or disposed, in terms of elements: “In this body there is the earth element, the water element, the fire element, the air element.”

सेय्यथापि, भिक्खवे, दक्खो गोघातको वा गोघातकन्तेवासी वा गाविं वधित्वा चतुमहापथे बिलसो विभजित्वा निसिन्नो अस्स।
Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, dakkho goghātako vā goghātakantevāsī vā gāviṁ vadhitvā catumahāpathe bilaso vibhajitvā nisinno assa.

15Just as a skilled butcher or a butcher's apprentice, having killed a cow, might sit at a crossroads with it divided into portions.

The four elements are not chemistry. Earth is whatever is solid and resistant, water whatever is cohesive and flowing, fire whatever is hot or maintains temperature, air whatever moves or holds pressure; the same body is being described under four functional headings rather than divided into four substances. The butcher simile is the sharpest image in the discourse and the commentary's reading of it is the one that stuck: while the animal is alive and whole, the perception "cow" applies; once it has been cut into portions and laid out for sale, what is there is meat, and the perception "cow" has nowhere to attach. The perception "being" is supposed to go the same way. The butcher is neither cruel nor kind in the simile, and no distaste is asked for; the change is in what can be truthfully said about what is on the table.

पुन चपरं, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु सेय्यथापि पस्सेय्य सरीरं सिवथिकाय छड्डितं एकाहमतं वा द्वीहमतं वा तीहमतं वा उद्धुमातकं विनीलकं विपुब्बकजातं।
सो इममेव कायं उपसंहरति:
‘अयम्पि खो कायो एवंधम्मो एवंभावी एवंअनतीतो’ति।
Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu seyyathāpi passeyya sarīraṁ sivathikāya chaḍḍitaṁ ekāhamataṁ vā dvīhamataṁ vā tīhamataṁ vā uddhumātakaṁ vinīlakaṁ vipubbakajātaṁ.
So imameva kāyaṁ upasaṁharati:
‘ayampi kho kāyo evaṁdhammo evaṁbhāvī evaṁanatīto’ti.

16Further, as though a monk were to see a body thrown aside in a charnel ground, one day dead or two days dead or three days dead, bloated, discoloured, festering. He compares this very body with it: “This body too is of that nature, will become like that, is not exempt from that.”

पुन चपरं, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु सेय्यथापि पस्सेय्य सरीरं सिवथिकाय छड्डितं काकेहि वा खज्जमानं कुललेहि वा खज्जमानं गिज्झेहि वा खज्जमानं कङ्केहि वा खज्जमानं सुनखेहि वा खज्जमानं ब्यग्घेहि वा खज्जमानं दीपीहि वा खज्जमानं सिङ्गालेहि वा खज्जमानं विविधेहि वा पाणकजातेहि खज्जमानं।
Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu seyyathāpi passeyya sarīraṁ sivathikāya chaḍḍitaṁ kākehi vā khajjamānaṁ kulalehi vā khajjamānaṁ gijjhehi vā khajjamānaṁ kaṅkehi vā khajjamānaṁ sunakhehi vā khajjamānaṁ byagghehi vā khajjamānaṁ dīpīhi vā khajjamānaṁ siṅgālehi vā khajjamānaṁ vividhehi vā pāṇakajātehi khajjamānaṁ.

17Further, as though he were to see a body thrown aside in a charnel ground, being eaten by crows, by hawks, by vultures, by herons, by dogs, by tigers, by leopards, by jackals, by various kinds of small creatures. He makes the same comparison with his own body.

पुन चपरं, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु सेय्यथापि पस्सेय्य सरीरं सिवथिकाय छड्डितं अट्ठिकसङ्खलिकं समंसलोहितं न्हारुसम्बन्धं …पे…
अट्ठिकसङ्खलिकं निमंसलोहितमक्खितं न्हारुसम्बन्धं …पे…
अट्ठिकसङ्खलिकं अपगतमंसलोहितं न्हारुसम्बन्धं …पे…
Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu seyyathāpi passeyya sarīraṁ sivathikāya chaḍḍitaṁ aṭṭhikasaṅkhalikaṁ samaṁsalohitaṁ nhārusambandhaṁ …pe…
Aṭṭhikasaṅkhalikaṁ nimaṁsalohitamakkhitaṁ nhārusambandhaṁ …pe…
Aṭṭhikasaṅkhalikaṁ apagatamaṁsalohitaṁ nhārusambandhaṁ …pe…

18Further, as though he were to see a skeleton with flesh and blood, held together by sinews; then a skeleton without flesh but smeared with blood, held together by sinews; then a skeleton with flesh and blood gone, held together by sinews. At each stage the Pali writes pe for the comparison and the refrain, which are to be repeated as before.

The nine stages are a timed sequence and they are meant to be looked at. Cremation was not universal, charnel grounds were real places outside the town where bodies were put, and the discourse assumes its hearers can walk to one. The operative verb is upasaṁharati, he brings together or compares: the corpse is not the object of the exercise, the comparison is. Nothing in the passage asks the meditator to feel anything about the corpse. What it asks is that he place his own body on the same line and grant that it has the same future, and it repeats the finding three times in the same words at every stage — of that nature, going that way, not exempt.

अट्ठिकानि अपगतसम्बन्धानि दिसा विदिसा विक्खित्तानि, अञ्ञेन हत्थट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन पादट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन गोप्फकट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन जङ्घट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन ऊरुट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन कटिट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन फासुकट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन पिट्ठिट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन खन्धट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन गीवट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन हनुकट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन दन्तट्ठिकं अञ्ञेन सीसकटाहं।
Aṭṭhikāni apagatasambandhāni disā vidisā vikkhittāni, aññena hatthaṭṭhikaṁ aññena pādaṭṭhikaṁ aññena gopphakaṭṭhikaṁ aññena jaṅghaṭṭhikaṁ aññena ūruṭṭhikaṁ aññena kaṭiṭṭhikaṁ aññena phāsukaṭṭhikaṁ aññena piṭṭhiṭṭhikaṁ aññena khandhaṭṭhikaṁ aññena gīvaṭṭhikaṁ aññena hanukaṭṭhikaṁ aññena dantaṭṭhikaṁ aññena sīsakaṭāhaṁ.

19Bones no longer held together, scattered in all directions: here a hand bone, there a foot bone, here an ankle bone, there a shin bone, here a thigh bone, there a hip bone, here a rib, there a backbone, here a shoulder bone, there a neck bone, here a jaw bone, there a tooth, here the skull.

अट्ठिकानि सेतानि सङ्खवण्णपटिभागानि …पे…
अट्ठिकानि पुञ्जकितानि तेरोवस्सिकानि …पे…
अट्ठिकानि पूतीनि चुण्णकजातानि।
सो इममेव कायं उपसंहरति:
‘अयम्पि खो कायो एवंधम्मो एवंभावी एवंअनतीतो’ति।
Aṭṭhikāni setāni saṅkhavaṇṇapaṭibhāgāni …pe…
Aṭṭhikāni puñjakitāni terovassikāni …pe…
Aṭṭhikāni pūtīni cuṇṇakajātāni.
So imameva kāyaṁ upasaṁharati:
‘ayampi kho kāyo evaṁdhammo evaṁbhāvī evaṁanatīto’ti.

20Bones bleached white, the colour of shells; bones heaped up, more than a year old; bones rotted away, crumbled to dust. He compares this very body with them: “This body too is of that nature, will become like that, is not exempt from that.”

इति अज्झत्तं वा काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति, बहिद्धा वा काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति, अज्झत्तबहिद्धा वा काये कायानुपस्सी विहरति;
समुदयधम्मानुपस्सी वा कायस्मिं विहरति, वयधम्मानुपस्सी वा कायस्मिं विहरति, समुदयवयधम्मानुपस्सी वा कायस्मिं विहरति।
‘अत्थि कायो’ति वा पनस्स सति पच्चुपट्ठिता होति। यावदेव ञाणमत्ताय पटिस्सतिमत्ताय अनिस्सितो च विहरति, न च किञ्चि लोके उपादियति।
नवसिवथिकपब्बं निट्ठितं। चुद्दसकायानुपस्सना निट्ठिता।
Iti ajjhattaṁ vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati, bahiddhā vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati, ajjhattabahiddhā vā kāye kāyānupassī viharati;
samudayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati, vayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati, samudayavayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṁ viharati.
‘Atthi kāyo’ti vā panassa sati paccupaṭṭhitā hoti. Yāvadeva ñāṇamattāya paṭissatimattāya anissito ca viharati, na ca kiñci loke upādiyati.
Navasivathikapabbaṁ niṭṭhitaṁ. Cuddasakāyānupassanā niṭṭhitā.

21So he dwells observing body as body internally, externally, and both; observing the nature of arising, the nature of vanishing, and both; or else mindfulness that “there is a body” is established in him just to the extent needed for bare knowing and bare remembering, and he dwells independent, clinging to nothing in the world. Here ends the section on the nine charnel grounds. Here end the fourteen contemplations of the body.

The Pali counts for us. Breathing, postures, clear comprehension, the parts, the elements and the nine stages of the corpse make fourteen, and the last line of the section says so. Anālayo's comparison of the three surviving versions — this one, the Madhyama-āgama discourse translated into Chinese from a Sarvāstivāda line, and the Ekottarika-āgama version — finds that the parts of the body, the elements and the charnel grounds appear in all of them, while breathing, the postures and clear comprehension do not. On that evidence the contemplations that modern courses begin with are the ones least securely attested, and the ones they usually leave out are the oldest layer.

Observing feelings

कथञ्च, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु वेदनासु वेदनानुपस्सी विहरति?
इध, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु सुखं वा वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘सुखं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
दुक्खं वा वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘दुक्खं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
अदुक्खमसुखं वा वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘अदुक्खमसुखं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
Kathañca, bhikkhave, bhikkhu vedanāsu vedanānupassī viharati?
Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu sukhaṁ vā vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘sukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.
Dukkhaṁ vā vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘dukkhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.
Adukkhamasukhaṁ vā vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘adukkhamasukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.

22And how does a monk dwell observing feelings as feelings? Here, feeling a pleasant feeling, a monk knows “I feel a pleasant feeling”; feeling a painful feeling, he knows “I feel a painful feeling”; feeling a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, he knows “I feel a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling.”

सामिसं वा सुखं वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘सामिसं सुखं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
निरामिसं वा सुखं वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘निरामिसं सुखं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
सामिसं वा दुक्खं वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘सामिसं दुक्खं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
निरामिसं वा दुक्खं वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘निरामिसं दुक्खं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
सामिसं वा अदुक्खमसुखं वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘सामिसं अदुक्खमसुखं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
निरामिसं वा अदुक्खमसुखं वेदनं वेदयमानो ‘निरामिसं अदुक्खमसुखं वेदनं वेदयामी’ति पजानाति।
Sāmisaṁ vā sukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘sāmisaṁ sukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.
Nirāmisaṁ vā sukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘nirāmisaṁ sukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.
Sāmisaṁ vā dukkhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘sāmisaṁ dukkhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.
Nirāmisaṁ vā dukkhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘nirāmisaṁ dukkhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.
Sāmisaṁ vā adukkhamasukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘sāmisaṁ adukkhamasukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.
Nirāmisaṁ vā adukkhamasukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayamāno ‘nirāmisaṁ adukkhamasukhaṁ vedanaṁ vedayāmī’ti pajānāti.

23Feeling a pleasant feeling of the flesh, he knows it as such; feeling a pleasant feeling not of the flesh, he knows it as such; and so for a painful feeling of the flesh and not of the flesh, and for a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling of the flesh and not of the flesh.

Vedanā is feeling in a narrow technical sense: the tone of an experience, pleasant or painful or neither, and not emotion, which in this scheme belongs to the mind section. The exercise has two moves. The first sorts every experience into one of three tones, including the third, which is the one that normally goes unnoticed and which the discourse insists be named. The second crosses that division with another: sāmisa means with bait or with flesh, nirāmisa without it, and the pair separates a pleasure that depends on the senses and their objects from a pleasure that does not — the gladness of meditative absorption, for instance, or the joy that comes of seeing something clearly. Six categories result, and the only instruction is to know which one is present.

इति अज्झत्तं वा वेदनासु वेदनानुपस्सी विहरति, बहिद्धा वा वेदनासु वेदनानुपस्सी विहरति, अज्झत्तबहिद्धा वा वेदनासु वेदनानुपस्सी विहरति;
समुदयधम्मानुपस्सी वा वेदनासु विहरति, वयधम्मानुपस्सी वा वेदनासु विहरति, समुदयवयधम्मानुपस्सी वा वेदनासु विहरति।
‘अत्थि वेदना’ति वा पनस्स सति पच्चुपट्ठिता होति। यावदेव ञाणमत्ताय पटिस्सतिमत्ताय अनिस्सितो च विहरति, न च किञ्चि लोके उपादियति।
वेदनानुपस्सना निट्ठिता।
Iti ajjhattaṁ vā vedanāsu vedanānupassī viharati, bahiddhā vā vedanāsu vedanānupassī viharati, ajjhattabahiddhā vā vedanāsu vedanānupassī viharati;
samudayadhammānupassī vā vedanāsu viharati, vayadhammānupassī vā vedanāsu viharati, samudayavayadhammānupassī vā vedanāsu viharati.
‘Atthi vedanā’ti vā panassa sati paccupaṭṭhitā hoti. Yāvadeva ñāṇamattāya paṭissatimattāya anissito ca viharati, na ca kiñci loke upādiyati.
Vedanānupassanā niṭṭhitā.

24So he dwells observing feelings as feelings internally, externally, and both; observing the nature of arising in feelings, the nature of vanishing, and both; or else mindfulness that “there are feelings” is established in him just to the extent needed for bare knowing and bare remembering, and he dwells independent, clinging to nothing in the world. Here ends the contemplation of feelings.

Observing mind

कथञ्च, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु चित्ते चित्तानुपस्सी विहरति?
इध, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु सरागं वा चित्तं ‘सरागं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
वीतरागं वा चित्तं ‘वीतरागं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
सदोसं वा चित्तं ‘सदोसं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति। वीतदोसं वा चित्तं ‘वीतदोसं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
समोहं वा चित्तं ‘समोहं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति। वीतमोहं वा चित्तं ‘वीतमोहं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
Kathañca, bhikkhave, bhikkhu citte cittānupassī viharati?
Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu sarāgaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘sarāgaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.
Vītarāgaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘vītarāgaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.
Sadosaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘sadosaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti. Vītadosaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘vītadosaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.
Samohaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘samohaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti. Vītamohaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘vītamohaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.

25And how does a monk dwell observing mind as mind? Here a monk knows a mind with lust as a mind with lust, and a mind without lust as a mind without lust. He knows a mind with hate as a mind with hate, and a mind without hate as a mind without hate. He knows a mind with delusion as a mind with delusion, and a mind without delusion as a mind without delusion.

सङ्खित्तं वा चित्तं ‘सङ्खित्तं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति। विक्खित्तं वा चित्तं ‘विक्खित्तं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
महग्गतं वा चित्तं ‘महग्गतं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति। अमहग्गतं वा चित्तं ‘अमहग्गतं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
सौत्तरं वा चित्तं ‘सौत्तरं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति। अनुत्तरं वा चित्तं ‘अनुत्तरं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
Saṅkhittaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘saṅkhittaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti. Vikkhittaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘vikkhittaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.
Mahaggataṁ vā cittaṁ ‘mahaggataṁ cittan’ti pajānāti. Amahaggataṁ vā cittaṁ ‘amahaggataṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.
Sauttaraṁ vā cittaṁ ‘sauttaraṁ cittan’ti pajānāti. Anuttaraṁ vā cittaṁ ‘anuttaraṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.

26He knows a contracted mind as contracted and a distracted mind as distracted. He knows an exalted mind as exalted and an unexalted mind as unexalted. He knows a surpassable mind as surpassable and an unsurpassable mind as unsurpassable.

समाहितं वा चित्तं ‘समाहितं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति। असमाहितं वा चित्तं ‘असमाहितं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
विमुत्तं वा चित्तं ‘विमुत्तं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति। अविमुत्तं वा चित्तं ‘अविमुत्तं चित्तन्’ति पजानाति।
Samāhitaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘samāhitaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti. Asamāhitaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘asamāhitaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.
Vimuttaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘vimuttaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti. Avimuttaṁ vā cittaṁ ‘avimuttaṁ cittan’ti pajānāti.

27He knows a concentrated mind as concentrated and an unconcentrated mind as unconcentrated. He knows a freed mind as freed and an unfreed mind as unfreed.

Sixteen terms in eight pairs, and the pairs are not of one kind. The first three are the standard roots of unwholesome action — lust, hate, delusion — and their absence. The next three are conditions of attention: contracted or distracted, exalted or not, surpassable or not, where the middle two describe states reached in meditative absorption. The last two are the highest: concentrated or not, freed or not. Nothing is asked for except the knowing, and the last pair makes the point sharpest. A mind that is not free is to be known as not free, and the discourse offers no instruction about what to do next. The exercise is diagnosis without treatment, and the treatment is the next foundation.

इति अज्झत्तं वा चित्ते चित्तानुपस्सी विहरति, बहिद्धा वा चित्ते चित्तानुपस्सी विहरति, अज्झत्तबहिद्धा वा चित्ते चित्तानुपस्सी विहरति;
समुदयधम्मानुपस्सी वा चित्तस्मिं विहरति, वयधम्मानुपस्सी वा चित्तस्मिं विहरति, समुदयवयधम्मानुपस्सी वा चित्तस्मिं विहरति।
‘अत्थि चित्तन्’ति वा पनस्स सति पच्चुपट्ठिता होति। यावदेव ञाणमत्ताय पटिस्सतिमत्ताय अनिस्सितो च विहरति, न च किञ्चि लोके उपादियति।
चित्तानुपस्सना निट्ठिता।
Iti ajjhattaṁ vā citte cittānupassī viharati, bahiddhā vā citte cittānupassī viharati, ajjhattabahiddhā vā citte cittānupassī viharati;
samudayadhammānupassī vā cittasmiṁ viharati, vayadhammānupassī vā cittasmiṁ viharati, samudayavayadhammānupassī vā cittasmiṁ viharati.
‘Atthi cittan’ti vā panassa sati paccupaṭṭhitā hoti. Yāvadeva ñāṇamattāya paṭissatimattāya anissito ca viharati, na ca kiñci loke upādiyati.
Cittānupassanā niṭṭhitā.

28So he dwells observing mind as mind internally, externally, and both; observing the nature of arising in the mind, the nature of vanishing, and both; or else mindfulness that “there is a mind” is established in him just to the extent needed for bare knowing and bare remembering, and he dwells independent, clinging to nothing in the world. Here ends the contemplation of mind.

Observing dhammas

कथञ्च, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति?
इध, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति पञ्चसु नीवरणेसु।
कथञ्च पन, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति पञ्चसु नीवरणेसु?
Kathañca, bhikkhave, bhikkhu dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati?
Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati pañcasu nīvaraṇesu.
Kathañca pana, bhikkhave, bhikkhu dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati pañcasu nīvaraṇesu?

29And how does a monk dwell observing dhammas as dhammas? Here a monk dwells observing dhammas as dhammas with respect to the five hindrances. And how does a monk dwell observing dhammas as dhammas with respect to the five hindrances?

इध, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु सन्तं वा अज्झत्तं कामच्छन्दं ‘अत्थि मे अज्झत्तं कामच्छन्दो’ति पजानाति, असन्तं वा अज्झत्तं कामच्छन्दं ‘नत्थि मे अज्झत्तं कामच्छन्दो’ति पजानाति; यथा च अनुप्पन्नस्स कामच्छन्दस्स उप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च उप्पन्नस्स कामच्छन्दस्स पहानं होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च पहीनस्स कामच्छन्दस्स आयतिं अनुप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति।
Idha, bhikkhave, bhikkhu santaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ kāmacchandaṁ ‘atthi me ajjhattaṁ kāmacchando’ti pajānāti, asantaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ kāmacchandaṁ ‘natthi me ajjhattaṁ kāmacchando’ti pajānāti; yathā ca anuppannassa kāmacchandassa uppādo hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca uppannassa kāmacchandassa pahānaṁ hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca pahīnassa kāmacchandassa āyatiṁ anuppādo hoti tañca pajānāti.

30Here, when sensual desire is present in him, a monk knows “there is sensual desire in me”; when it is absent, he knows “there is no sensual desire in me.” He knows how sensual desire that has not arisen comes to arise; he knows how sensual desire that has arisen comes to be abandoned; and he knows how sensual desire that has been abandoned comes not to arise again in the future.

The word left untranslated in the fourth foundation is the one that has to be. Dhammā runs from a thing simply present in experience to a teaching to a category in the analysis of mind, and the five sets the discourse lists under it are not one kind of thing: one is a set of obstructions, one is an inventory of a person, one is an account of how contact produces bondage, one is a set of qualities to be developed, and the last is a doctrine. Ñāṇamoli and Bodhi print mind-objects, Thanissaro mental qualities, Sujato principles, and none of the three works for all five sets. What they have in common is that each is a scheme from the teaching being used to read what is present, which is why observing dhammas as dhammas is not a further sense-channel but a further way of looking at the same material.

Five moves are asked for, and only the first two are observation of a state. The third, fourth and fifth are observation of a mechanism: how this arises, how it is abandoned, how it is prevented from returning. The verb throughout is pajānāti, he knows, and never pajahati, he abandons. What the meditator is told to see is the causal structure of his own hindrance, not to remove it; the removal, on the text's account, is what knowing the structure produces.

सन्तं वा अज्झत्तं ब्यापादं ‘अत्थि मे अज्झत्तं ब्यापादो’ति पजानाति, असन्तं वा अज्झत्तं ब्यापादं ‘नत्थि मे अज्झत्तं ब्यापादो’ति पजानाति; यथा च अनुप्पन्नस्स ब्यापादस्स उप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च उप्पन्नस्स ब्यापादस्स पहानं होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च पहीनस्स ब्यापादस्स आयतिं अनुप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति।
सन्तं वा अज्झत्तं थिनमिद्धं ‘अत्थि मे अज्झत्तं थिनमिद्धन्’ति पजानाति, असन्तं वा अज्झत्तं थिनमिद्धं ‘नत्थि मे अज्झत्तं थिनमिद्धन्’ति पजानाति, यथा च अनुप्पन्नस्स थिनमिद्धस्स उप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च उप्पन्नस्स थिनमिद्धस्स पहानं होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च पहीनस्स थिनमिद्धस्स आयतिं अनुप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति।
Santaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ byāpādaṁ ‘atthi me ajjhattaṁ byāpādo’ti pajānāti, asantaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ byāpādaṁ ‘natthi me ajjhattaṁ byāpādo’ti pajānāti; yathā ca anuppannassa byāpādassa uppādo hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca uppannassa byāpādassa pahānaṁ hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca pahīnassa byāpādassa āyatiṁ anuppādo hoti tañca pajānāti.
Santaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ thinamiddhaṁ ‘atthi me ajjhattaṁ thinamiddhan’ti pajānāti, asantaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ thinamiddhaṁ ‘natthi me ajjhattaṁ thinamiddhan’ti pajānāti, yathā ca anuppannassa thinamiddhassa uppādo hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca uppannassa thinamiddhassa pahānaṁ hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca pahīnassa thinamiddhassa āyatiṁ anuppādo hoti tañca pajānāti.

31When ill will is present in him he knows it, and when it is absent he knows that; and he knows how the unarisen arises, how the arisen is abandoned, how the abandoned does not arise again. The same five clauses are given for dullness and drowsiness.

सन्तं वा अज्झत्तं उद्धच्चकुक्कुच्चं ‘अत्थि मे अज्झत्तं उद्धच्चकुक्कुच्चन्’ति पजानाति, असन्तं वा अज्झत्तं उद्धच्चकुक्कुच्चं ‘नत्थि मे अज्झत्तं उद्धच्चकुक्कुच्चन्’ति पजानाति; यथा च अनुप्पन्नस्स उद्धच्चकुक्कुच्चस्स उप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च उप्पन्नस्स उद्धच्चकुक्कुच्चस्स पहानं होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च पहीनस्स उद्धच्चकुक्कुच्चस्स आयतिं अनुप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति।
सन्तं वा अज्झत्तं विचिकिच्छं ‘अत्थि मे अज्झत्तं विचिकिच्छा’ति पजानाति, असन्तं वा अज्झत्तं विचिकिच्छं ‘नत्थि मे अज्झत्तं विचिकिच्छा’ति पजानाति; यथा च अनुप्पन्नाय विचिकिच्छाय उप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च उप्पन्नाय विचिकिच्छाय पहानं होति तञ्च पजानाति, यथा च पहीनाय विचिकिच्छाय आयतिं अनुप्पादो होति तञ्च पजानाति।
Santaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ uddhaccakukkuccaṁ ‘atthi me ajjhattaṁ uddhaccakukkuccan’ti pajānāti, asantaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ uddhaccakukkuccaṁ ‘natthi me ajjhattaṁ uddhaccakukkuccan’ti pajānāti; yathā ca anuppannassa uddhaccakukkuccassa uppādo hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca uppannassa uddhaccakukkuccassa pahānaṁ hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca pahīnassa uddhaccakukkuccassa āyatiṁ anuppādo hoti tañca pajānāti.
Santaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ vicikicchaṁ ‘atthi me ajjhattaṁ vicikicchā’ti pajānāti, asantaṁ vā ajjhattaṁ vicikicchaṁ ‘natthi me ajjhattaṁ vicikicchā’ti pajānāti; yathā ca anuppannāya vicikicchāya uppādo hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca uppannāya vicikicchāya pahānaṁ hoti tañca pajānāti, yathā ca pahīnāya vicikicchāya āyatiṁ anuppādo hoti tañca pajānāti.

32The same five clauses are given for restlessness and remorse, and then for doubt: when doubt is present he knows it, when it is absent he knows that, and he knows how the unarisen arises, how the arisen is abandoned, and how the abandoned does not arise again in the future.

इति अज्झत्तं वा धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति, बहिद्धा वा धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति, अज्झत्तबहिद्धा वा धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति;
समुदयधम्मानुपस्सी वा धम्मेसु विहरति, वयधम्मानुपस्सी वा धम्मेसु विहरति, समुदयवयधम्मानुपस्सी वा धम्मेसु विहरति।
‘अत्थि धम्मा’ति वा पनस्स सति पच्चुपट्ठिता होति। यावदेव ञाणमत्ताय पटिस्सतिमत्ताय अनिस्सितो च विहरति, न च किञ्चि लोके उपादियति।
नीवरणपब्बं निट्ठितं।
Iti ajjhattaṁ vā dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati, bahiddhā vā dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati, ajjhattabahiddhā vā dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati;
samudayadhammānupassī vā dhammesu viharati, vayadhammānupassī vā dhammesu viharati, samudayavayadhammānupassī vā dhammesu viharati.
‘Atthi dhammā’ti vā panassa sati paccupaṭṭhitā hoti. Yāvadeva ñāṇamattāya paṭissatimattāya anissito ca viharati, na ca kiñci loke upādiyati.
Nīvaraṇapabbaṁ niṭṭhitaṁ.

33So he dwells observing dhammas as dhammas internally, externally, and both; observing the nature of arising among dhammas, the nature of vanishing, and both; or else mindfulness that “there are dhammas” is established in him just to the extent needed for bare knowing and bare remembering, and he dwells independent, clinging to nothing in the world. Here ends the section on the hindrances.

The five hindrances are sensual desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt, and elsewhere in the canon they are the standard obstacle to meditative absorption. Here they are not obstacles to the exercise; they are the exercise. A mind too restless to settle has, on this instruction, something to observe rather than something to fix, and the state that would elsewhere be a failure of practice becomes its object. That inversion is why the discourse can be given to beginners at all.

पुन चपरं, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति पञ्चसु उपादानक्खन्धेसु।
कथञ्च पन, भिक्खवे, भिक्खु धम्मेसु धम्मानुपस्सी विहरति पञ्चसु उपादानक्खन्धेसु?
Puna caparaṁ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati pañcasu upādānakkhandhesu.
Kathañca pana, bhikkhave, bhikkhu dhammesu dhammānupassī viharati pañcasu upādānakkhandhesu?

34Further, a monk dwells observing dhammas as dhammas with respect to the five constituents that are clung to. And how does he do so?

‘इति रूपं, इति रूपस्स समुदयो, इति रूपस्स अत्थङ्गमो;
इति वेदना, इति वेदनाय समुदयो, इति वेदनाय अत्थङ्गमो;
इति सञ्ञा, इति सञ्ञाय समुदयो, इति सञ्ञाय अत्थङ्गमो;
इति सङ्खारा, इति सङ्खारानं समुदयो, इति सङ्खारानं अत्थङ्गमो;
इति विञ्ञाणं, इति विञ्ञाणस्स समुदयो, इति विञ्ञाणस्स अत्थङ्गमो’ति।
‘Iti rūpaṁ, iti rūpassa samudayo, iti rūpassa atthaṅgamo;
iti vedanā, iti vedanāya samudayo, iti vedanāya atthaṅgamo;
iti saññā, iti saññāya samudayo, iti saññāya atthaṅgamo;
iti saṅkhārā, iti saṅkhārānaṁ samudayo, iti saṅkhārānaṁ atthaṅgamo;
iti viññāṇaṁ, iti viññāṇassa samudayo, iti viññāṇassa atthaṅgamo’ti.

35“Such is form, such is the origin of form, such is the passing away of form. Such is feeling, such is its origin, such is its passing away. Such is perception, such is its origin, such is its passing away. Such are volitions, such is their origin, such is their passing away. Such is consciousness, such is its origin, such is its passing away.”

The second set is the five constituents — form, feeling, perception, volitions, consciousness — the same inventory taken apart in the discourse on the mark of not-self, and here it is handled in three words each. What is asked is not the argument of that discourse but a smaller thing: to see each constituent, to see it start, to see it stop. The qualifier upādāna, clung to, is doing work, since the objects are not the five in general but the five as the objects of clinging. This is the shortest of the five sets and the one that most obviously assumes prior doctrine; a hearer who did not already know what saṅkhārā covers would get nothing from the sentence.

37He knows the ear and sounds and the fetter arising dependent on both; the nose and smells; the tongue and tastes; the body and touches; and the mind and its objects, together with the fetter that arises dependent on each pair, and with the same three clauses about its arising, its abandoning and its non-return.

Three items are named where two would do. There is the faculty, there is what meets it, and there is the fetter that arises in dependence on the two together — and the fetter is placed in neither of them. It is not in the eye, because the eye of a freed person meets the same sights; it is not in the sight, because the same sight binds one person and not another. The mind counts as the sixth faculty on the same footing as the other five, with its own objects and its own fetter, which is why the discourse never divides experience into a physical outside and a mental inside.

40When the awakening factor of equanimity is present in him he knows it; when it is absent he knows that; and he knows how the unarisen factor comes to arise, and how the arisen factor comes to fulfilment by development.

The seven factors are mindfulness, investigation of dhammas, energy, rapture, tranquillity, concentration and equanimity, and they are the positive counterpart of the hindrances. The formula applied to them is the same as the one applied to the hindrances with one term changed. The hindrance formula ends in non-recurrence: how the abandoned does not arise again. The awakening-factor formula ends in fulfilment: how the arisen comes to completion through development. Everything else about the two is symmetrical, including the verb. Even here, where the object is something to be grown rather than got rid of, the instruction is to know how it grows and not to grow it, which is the strongest evidence in the discourse that observation and cultivation are not being distinguished as they are in most modern accounts.

41Further, a monk dwells observing dhammas as dhammas with respect to the four noble truths. Here he knows as it really is “this is suffering”; he knows as it really is “this is the origin of suffering”; he knows as it really is “this is the cessation of suffering”; he knows as it really is “this is the way leading to the cessation of suffering.”

The last set takes four lines, and the brevity is itself evidence. In the Dīgha collection the same discourse appears at three times the length under the title of the Great Discourse on the Establishings of Mindfulness, and the whole of the difference falls here: in that version the four truths are analysed at length, with a definition of suffering, an account of craving, and the eightfold path spelled out limb by limb. Everything else in the two versions matches. The ladder descends by single years, then by single months, then by halves: seven years down to one year, seven months down to one month, then half a month, then seven days. Sixteen rungs in all. The promise is stated in the same words at every rung, and it is a conditional about a method rather than a claim about any particular person: whoever should develop them evaṁ, in this way. What is promised is not a guaranteed enlightenment but one of two outcomes, the second of which is a stage short of the first.

48This is what the Blessed One said. Satisfied, those monks delighted in his words. Here ends the tenth discourse, on the establishings of mindfulness.

The opening sentence returns as the closing sentence, and the clause between them — so it was said, and it was said with reference to this — tells the hearer what the discourse has been. The claim made at the start was not self-evident and was not defended by argument; it was defended by exhibition. Everything between the two statements is the content of the word this. The discourse never says why observing the body should purify a being, or why knowing that a hindrance is present should cause it to go. It says what to do, for how long, and what follows.

Since the fifth century BC

The discourse survives three times in Pali. This version in the Majjhima collection is the tenth discourse of the first book; the Dīgha version, longer by its analysis of the four truths, is the twenty-second of that collection; and the Saṁyutta collection devotes a whole division of a hundred and four short discourses to the subject. Two more versions survive in Chinese, one in the Madhyama-āgama translated at the end of the fourth century from a Sarvāstivāda line of transmission, one in the Ekottarika-āgama. Anālayo's Perspectives on Satipaṭṭhāna of 2013 sets the three side by side and finds that the exercises common to all of them are the anatomical parts, the elements, the charnel grounds, the feelings, the states of mind, the hindrances and the awakening factors; breathing, the postures, clear comprehension, the constituents, the sense bases and the four truths appear in some and not others. Buddhaghosa's fifth-century commentary, which knows none of this, reads the Pali order as designed, each set placed where it is for a reason, and Soma Thera's translation of that commentary in 1941 put the reading into English as The Way of Mindfulness.

For most of the intervening centuries the text was chanted rather than practised. Theravāda monasticism treated serious meditation as the work of a small number of forest monks, and the systematic treatment of the discourse's exercises lived in Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga as one subject among forty. The change came from Burma in the late nineteenth century, and it was a change of audience before it was a change of method. Ledi Sayadaw, who died in 1923, argued that lay people could and should study the higher doctrine and meditate, and wrote manuals in Burmese for people who had no Pali. Mingun Jetavana Sayadaw and then Mahāsi Sayadaw built a method out of the discourse that could be taught to a beginner in an afternoon: observe the rise and fall of the abdomen, and label whatever interrupts it — hearing, thinking, aching — with a single word. The colonial and post-colonial Burmese state backed the revival; the Sixth Buddhist Council held in Rangoon between 1954 and 1956 edited the canon and made mass lay meditation a national project. U Ba Khin, an accountant general, taught a parallel method built on sweeping attention through the body, and his student S. N. Goenka carried it to India and then everywhere as the ten-day course.

Nyanaponika Thera, a German-born monk in Ceylon, gave the method its English vocabulary. His The Heart of Buddhist Meditation, published in German in 1950 and in English from 1954, glossed sati as bare attention: attending to what appears at each moment of perception, and to nothing beyond it. The phrase is a reading of one clause of the refrain, the clause that limits mindfulness to what is needed for bare knowing, and it was made to carry the whole. Jon Kabat-Zinn opened a stress reduction clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical School in 1979 and took that vocabulary with him; and the eight-week course he designed there became mindfulness-based stress reduction, and the cognitive therapy built on it followed in 2002, and between them they put the discourse's first exercise, stripped of its frame, into hospitals, schools and companies in every country. The correction from the scholars came later and is textual rather than clinical. Rupert Gethin showed that sati in the canon keeps its sense of remembering and calls a scheme to mind rather than emptying the mind of schemes; Bhikkhu Bodhi argued in 2011 that no early text defines mindfulness as non-judgemental awareness and that the discourse's fourth foundation is explicitly evaluative, since the hindrances are to be abandoned and the awakening factors fulfilled; Robert Sharf traced the Burmese revival's emphasis on unmediated experience to a modern argument rather than an ancient one. What none of them disputes is that the practices work as taught. The dispute is over what the text was asking for, and on that the text is not ambiguous: it asks for ardour, for the prior removal of longing and aversion, for observation extended to another person's body and another person's mind, and for a series of exercises on decomposition that no clinical course includes.

Evan Thompson's argument that mindfulness is not the inward inspection of a private mental theatre cuts against the modern teaching and not against this text, which places another person's body and another person's mind among the objects of the practice from the first exercise onward and never treats the meditator as sealed in. And the discourse on the mark of not-self, delivered to five men in a deer park, supplies what this one leaves out: the reason why watching a body arise and vanish should end in clinging to nothing at all. Read alone, the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta is a method without an argument. Read after the other, it is the procedure by which the argument is made available to someone who has only his own experience to work from.

LimitsA promise repeated sixteen times is rhetoric, and the absence of a mechanism is the thing Anattalakkhana Sutta supplies from outside. Fleming Know Thyself is the contrast: two weeks of trained feedback produced small measured gains, which is the form of evidence this text does not offer.
PracticeSitting practice — take the conditional seriously as a conditional: a bounded promise about a method you can run, not a claim you are asked to believe first.
After AIThe honest modern position is the same shape: a method with measured short-term effects, a contested account of why it works, and a promise that outruns both.

The Satipatthana instruction includes putting aside wanting and resisting

The sutta's opening formula tells the practitioner to put aside longing and aversion toward the world as part of the observing itself. This is therefore not an exercise in accepting whatever arises.

Becausethe opening formula says vineyya loke abhijjhādomanassaṁ, alongside ātāpī, ardent, from a root meaning heat, and sampajāno, discriminating knowledge of what one is doing
LimitsWhether vineyya is a precondition or a result of the practice is argued, and the translation decides the claim. Hayes ACT Get Out of Your Mind takes the opposite view deliberately and has trials behind it.
PracticeSitting practice — the text asks for ardour and for the prior removal of wanting and not wanting; a practice that asks only for acceptance is doing something else, which may be fine but should not be called this.
After AIIt is the sharpest correction available to the version of mindfulness sold as neutral stress reduction, which is the version that reaches most people, including through tools that now offer it.
opinionObserving the body — the refrain

The Satipatthana refrain adds five instructions the exercises never give

The refrain repeated after every exercise asks for five things none of the exercises asks for: observe inside, outside and both; observe arising, vanishing and both; hold mindfulness only as far as knowing needs; depend on nothing; cling to nothing.

Becausethe reading rests on two Pali terms: yāvadeva, just so far as, which limits rather than extends, and anissito ca viharati, na ca kiñci loke upādiyati, which states the goal of the whole discourse in eight words after every exercise
LimitsTwenty-one exercises and one paragraph repeated after each; whether the repetition carries the weight this puts on it is an interpretive choice, and the refrain is one of the clauses whose attestation across versions Anālayo shows to be uneven.
PracticeWitness practice in review — run the refrain rather than the exercise: watch the reaction arise and pass, watch it in the other person too, and stop when knowing is reached.
After AIThe internal-and-external clause is what keeps this from being introspection, and it is the clause a solitary practice with a chatbot as interlocutor has no way to supply.

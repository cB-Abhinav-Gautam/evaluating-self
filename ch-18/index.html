18To Trust or to Think

Zana Buçinca, Maja Barbara Malaya and Krzysztof Z. Gajos, Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction 5, CSCW1, article 188, 2021

People who are given a machine's recommendation tend to follow it even when it is wrong and they would have done better alone. Adding an explanation of how the machine reached its answer was meant to fix this, by letting the person see when the reasoning was faulty. It has not. Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos start from that failure and offer a reason for it. Reading an explanation carefully is effortful, and people do not do effortful things for every decision; they form a general impression of whether the machine is competent and act on that. An explanation, on this account, works as a signal of competence rather than as something examined, and can raise reliance rather than lower it. The remedy they borrow from medicine is the cognitive forcing function, an intervention at the moment of decision that interrupts the fast route and makes the person think. Three such designs, in a nutrition task with a simulated assistant that was right three times in four, cut the rate at which people followed wrong suggestions compared with two ordinary explanation designs, though they did not remove it. The cost was that people liked the designs that worked least, and the benefit went mostly to the people who already enjoy thinking.

The problem

The expectation behind decision support was that a person and a model together would beat either alone. What studies have found instead is that the pair usually beats the person and usually loses to the model, in medical treatment selection, deceptive review detection, loan default prediction and other tasks. The authors' reading is that the shortfall comes from overreliance: when the model is wrong, the person follows it anyway. Explanations were supposed to make wrong suggestions detectable, and the evidence to 2021 was that they did not; teams with explanations still did worse than the model alone, and one study found that the mere presence of an explanation raised trust and reliance whatever its content.

The paper's account of why uses the two-system vocabulary. Most decisions run on fast heuristic thinking, which serves well and is prone to predictable errors; analytical thinking is slow and costly and is invoked rarely. The authors are careful not to call the fast route bad, since pattern recognition built on experience is what expertise is, but experts too fall prey to bias, and the design of explainable systems assumes a person who engages analytically with every explanation. Since people are averse to that much effort, the assumption is likely false. So the task, they argue, is not only to make better explanations but to raise the person's motivation to engage with them at all.

Cognitive forcing functions

The idea comes from clinical medicine, where diagnostic errors are more often failures of information synthesis than of knowledge. Two kinds of remedy have been tried: educational strategies, which teach clinicians about pitfalls in advance, and cognitive forcing functions, which act at the moment of decision. Checklists, diagnostic time-outs and deliberate slowing are the standard examples, and the evidence is that the interventions applied at decision time work better than the ones taught beforehand. The authors list three analogues already tried in machine-assisted settings: making the person decide before seeing the recommendation, which counters anchoring; delaying the recommendation, since a slower algorithm has been shown to improve people's judgment of its accuracy; and letting the person choose whether and when to see it, since unsolicited advice that contradicts a first idea provokes resistance. They predict a price. Across visualisation and education research, people prefer easier designs and learn and perform better with harder ones, so an intervention that forces thought will probably be liked less.

The experiment

Experts are hard to recruit, so the task was designed for laypeople. Participants saw a photograph of a meal and had to make it low in carbohydrate by picking the ingredient highest in carbohydrate from a list of about ten and choosing a replacement, from another list of ten, that cut carbohydrate while keeping the flavour. Each meal had one ingredient with far more carbohydrate than the rest. The assistant was simulated rather than trained, so that its errors could be controlled: it recognised the right ingredient three times in four, and when it failed it recommended replacing the second highest ingredient, which on these plates was a low-carbohydrate one. Its explanation showed the top four substitutes with an estimated carbohydrate reduction and a flavour similarity computed from a database of flavour molecules, in the style of feature-based explanations.

Six designs were compared. With no assistant, participants saw the photograph and the two menus. Two simple explainable designs showed the suggestion and explanation at once, one of them adding a confidence statement when the assistant was unsure. Three forcing designs withheld it in different ways. On demand hid the suggestion behind a button. Update required an unassisted decision first, then showed the suggestion and allowed a revision, on the hypothesis that a disagreement would make the person curious about the explanation. Wait showed a processing message for thirty seconds before the suggestion appeared, on the hypothesis that the person would form their own answer in the meantime and then check the explanation against it. Each of 199 participants recruited online, after exclusions, saw two designs in two blocks of thirteen questions, with three wrong suggestions in each block, and rated preference, trust, mental demand and complexity after each. After the first block they answered four items from the Need for Cognition scale.

What happened

On all questions taken together, any assistance beat none, and the forcing designs did no better than the simple ones. The difference appeared where the assistant was wrong. On those questions people with no assistant did best of all, people with simple explanations did worst, and people under cognitive forcing were in between: they identified the carbohydrate source correctly 27 per cent of the time against 8 per cent with simple explanations and 49 per cent unassisted. For the full decision, source and replacement together, the figures were 9, 3 and 18 per cent. Where people under simple explanation went wrong they overwhelmingly followed the assistant; forcing reduced that following, from 64 to 48 per cent on source detection, and raised correct answers, without changing the rate of the person's own unassisted errors. The three forcing designs did not differ from one another, and neither did the two simple ones.

The subjective ratings ran the other way. Participants trusted the simple designs a little more, preferred any assistance to none, found the unassisted task most demanding, and rated the forcing designs as more complex than the simple ones. Trust and preference correlated negatively with performance on wrong suggestions; mental demand correlated positively with it. People did best in the conditions they preferred least, trusted least and found hardest, which is the trade-off the authors had predicted. They note one further implication. Studies that evaluate explanations with proxy tasks, asking people to predict what the model will say, force attention on the explanation and so behave like a strong forcing function, which is why such studies report better results than studies of real decisions.

Who it helped

The authors then audit their own intervention for what they call intervention-generated inequality, a benefit that flows mostly to a group already advantaged. The relevant trait here, they argue, is need for cognition, a stable disposition to enjoy effortful thinking, which earlier work had linked to the uptake of complex interface features. Splitting participants at the median, they found that people high in the trait did better on the task overall and that the gains from forcing fell mostly to them: for the full decision, forcing improved high scorers significantly and low scorers not at all, while both groups improved on source detection. High scorers also trusted and preferred the forcing designs less than the simple ones; low scorers rated the two the same. The result cuts both ways. The interventions widened a gap, and since low scorers did not mind them, stricter interventions might be aimed at the people who need them most without being rejected.

The paper closes on its limits. One non-critical task, one population, a simulated assistant; the authors expect the results to travel because experts are known to fall into heuristic thinking too, but they have not shown it. And an intervention people dislike may not be adopted, which leads them to propose adaptive designs that deploy the strongest forcing only on the instances where a wrong suggestion is likely and a person is likely to follow it.

Since 2021

The paper named a category, and cognitive forcing functions became standard vocabulary in the study of assisted decisions. Its central negative finding, that explanations raise reliance without improving its calibration, was confirmed in the same year by a larger study from Bansal and colleagues and in later meta-analyses, which find that explanations lift accuracy slightly and appropriate reliance hardly at all. Its positive finding was refined rather than overturned. Vasconcelos and colleagues showed in 2023 that explanations can reduce overreliance when the task is hard enough and the explanation easy enough that checking is cheaper than deferring, which turns the paper's motivation story into a cost-benefit one. Gajos and Mamykina found in 2022 that people who receive a recommendation first also learn less from the task, adding a second cost to the fast route. The adaptive designs proposed in the closing pages were built by the same group, which trained a policy to decide when to force and when to leave the person alone. The trade-off has not gone away. Every intervention that reduces overreliance so far makes the interface slower or harder, people still rate the easy designs higher, and the systems in wide use have kept the easy designs.

Claims drawn from this chapter · 8

evidenceWhat happened

All three ways of withholding the AI's answer worked equally well

Bucinca and colleagues tested three ways of making people think before they saw the AI's suggestion: hiding it behind a button, asking for the person's own decision first, and a thirty-second delay. None worked better than the others.

Becauseno significant difference between on-demand, update and wait, and none between the two simple designs
LimitsA null result on a sample of 199 across six conditions is weak evidence of equivalence rather than evidence of no difference, and the three designs differ in cost — a thirty-second delay is not interchangeable with a button in practice.
PracticePick whichever forcing design fits your work. What matters is that the suggestion is withheld until you have something of your own, not how it is withheld.
After AIThat the mechanism is insensitive to the implementation is the useful part: writing your own answer first works as well as any interface feature, and it is the one version available without waiting for a vendor.
evidenceWho it helped

Bucinca found the think-first design helped mainly people who enjoy thinking

In Bucinca's study the designs that made people think before seeing the AI's answer helped mainly those who already scored high on enjoying mental effort (need for cognition). Low scorers did not improve, so the gap between them widened.

Becausea median split on a four-item scale: on the whole decision, high scorers improved significantly and low scorers not at all, while both improved on source detection
LimitsA median split on four items, in a sample of 199, on an individual difference the study did not manipulate. The authors note the result cuts both ways: low scorers did not mind the forcing designs, so stricter ones might be aimed at them.
PracticeDo not assume a review practice that works for you will work for the team. Check who it is helping before making it policy.
After AIAn intervention that widens an existing gap is the standing risk for every remedy in this area, and this is the only paper in the set that audited its own for it.
contestedSince 2021

Bucinca's finding was assumed, not shown, to hold for experts

The study tested one low-stakes nutrition task, with people recruited online and a made-up AI assistant. That the finding also holds for experts doing real work is an assumption the authors make, not something they measured.

Becausethe authors state the limit themselves and rest the transfer on the claim that experts fall into heuristic thinking too
LimitsKlein's tradition would predict the opposite: an expert who notices a case is anomalous and switches to deliberate search is doing exactly what the forcing function is meant to induce. Whether that protection survives a confident suggestion is untested.
PracticeBefore applying a crowd-worker result to your own domain, ask whether the study's participants had any of the recognition the task rewards. Usually they had none.
After AIThe crowd-worker studies measure the floor rather than the decay, which is the one thing that separates them from Bainbridge's operator. What happens to an expert whose recognition is no longer exercised is the question none of them asks.

opinionThe problem

People read an explanation as proof of competence instead of checking it

Buçinca and colleagues argue that people treat an explanation from a machine as a sign that it knows what it is doing, rather than reading the explanation itself. That is why a better explanation does not produce a better decision.

Becauseit is the paper's proposed mechanism, argued from effort aversion and the two-system account rather than measured directly
LimitsThe mechanism is inferred from the pattern of results, not observed; Vasconcelos and colleagues showed in 2023 that explanations do reduce over-reliance where checking is cheaper than deferring, which recasts it as a cost problem rather than a motivation one.
PracticeStop investing in better explanations and start investing in whether the explanation gets read at all. The second is the binding constraint.
After AIWritten for feature-based explanations of a classifier, the claim now covers chains of reasoning that are longer, more fluent and more persuasive than the answers they justify — a stronger competence signal and a more expensive thing to examine.
evidenceWhat happened

People were most accurate in the setup they liked and trusted least

In Bucinca's study, people judged the AI's wrong suggestions most accurately in the conditions they liked least, trusted least and rated as the most mental effort.

Becausetrust and preference correlated negatively with accuracy on wrong suggestions and mental demand correlated positively, the trade-off the authors had predicted in advance
LimitsCorrelations across conditions in a within-subjects design, with the ratings taken immediately after each block; they do not show that discomfort causes accuracy, only that the two travelled together here. Cognitive load theory predicts the opposite in learning settings.
PracticeTreat your own preference between two tools as evidence about their comfort, not their value, and check the uncomfortable one on the cases where it matters.
After AIThe preference is what determines which designs get shipped, so the finding predicts its own fate: the interfaces in wide use are the ones people rated highest and performed worst under.
opinionWhat happened

Studies that ask people to predict the model make them think harder

Studies that test AI explanations by asking people to guess what the model will say force them to read closely. That is why such studies report better results than studies of people making real decisions.

Becauseit is a methodological inference the authors draw from their own design rather than something they tested
LimitsAn inference, not a test; nobody has compared the same explanation under a proxy task and a real decision to confirm the gap is caused by the forcing rather than by the difference in stakes.
PracticeDiscount any result about explanations that was measured by asking people to predict the model's output. The measurement changed the behaviour it measured.
After AIThe point generalises past explanations: any evaluation that makes people attend closely to model output measures a condition that ordinary use does not create.

Where the assistant was wrong, its explanation made people worse than nothing

On the questions where the assistant gave the wrong answer, people who were shown a short explanation of that answer did worse than people who worked with no assistant at all.

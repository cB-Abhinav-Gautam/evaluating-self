54माण्डूक्योपनिषत् गौडपादकारिका · The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad and Gauḍapāda's Kārikā

Gauḍapāda, c. 600 · with Śaṅkara's commentary · Swami Nikhilananda, translator, Sri Ramakrishna Ashrama, 1936; Swami Gambhirananda, translator, Sri Ramakrishna Math, 1987

Linked to 20 chapters: supports 6 · contradicts 8 · mechanism 8 · same claim 12 · pairing 5

The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad is twelve sentences of prose. It says that the syllable OM is everything; that the self has four quarters; that three of them are waking, dream and dreamless sleep; and that the fourth is what the other three are made of, describable only by saying what it is not. It is the shortest of the principal Upaniṣads, and one late text says that if a man can study only one Upaniṣad, this is the one that will do. What made it the centre of Indian philosophy rather than a curiosity is the commentary attached to it. Gauḍapāda, who lived some time before the eighth century and whom tradition makes the teacher of Śaṅkara's teacher, wrote two hundred and fifteen verses on those twelve sentences, in four treatises. The first stays close to the text. The other three drop scripture almost entirely and argue. By the end of the fourth, Gauḍapāda has claimed that nothing has ever been born, that causation cannot be stated without contradiction, that waking and dreaming stand or fall together, and that the appearance of a world is to consciousness what a circle of fire is to a whirling stick: an appearance that comes from nowhere, goes nowhere, and never belonged to anything.

The claim is not offered as a mood. It is offered as the conclusion of arguments that can be checked, and that is why the text has held its place. Gauḍapāda is the earliest Advaitin whose work survives whole, and the first Indian philosopher to make non-duality stand on reasoning rather than on the authority of the Veda. Śaṅkara, three or four generations later, wrote a commentary on both the Upaniṣad and the verses, and Nikhilananda takes the mere fact of it as a measure of the work's standing: Śaṅkara commented on the Upaniṣads, the Gītā and the Brahma Sūtras, and on this. His bhāṣya does two things at once: it tightens Gauḍapāda's arguments, and it repeatedly stages an objector, sometimes a realist, sometimes a Sāṃkhya, sometimes a Buddhist idealist, to be answered in the objector's own terms.

Almost nothing is known about Gauḍapāda himself. His name may mean no more than "the teacher from Gauḍa", the region of Bengal. Tradition makes him the teacher of Govinda, who was the teacher of Śaṅkara; Ānandagiri, writing a subcommentary on Śaṅkara, says he did austerities at Badarī in the Himalayas and had the doctrine revealed to him there. Nikhilananda, whose 1936 translation is the fullest English presentation of the whole — Upaniṣad, verses, bhāṣya and notes after Ānandagiri — says flatly that the date cannot be fixed, because Śaṅkara's own date is not fixed. Sixth or seventh century is the ordinary estimate. Nikhilananda's is the translation to read with the renderings below, which are made from the Sanskrit and are meant to be plain; Gambhirananda's later version gives the verses with a spare English rendering and no commentary. The Upaniṣad's twelve sentences are numbered singly in what follows; Gauḍapāda's verses carry two numbers, the treatise and the verse.

Āgama: what the scripture says

Śaṅkara opens his commentary with a simile that sets the shape of the whole. A man with a disease returns to his normal state when the cause of the disease is removed; the self, labouring under a misapprehension and identifying itself with misery, returns to its own state when the illusion of duality ceases. Nothing is gained. Something stops. The treatise therefore cannot produce liberation and does not claim to: scripture, in Śaṅkara's account, works indirectly, by pointing at the illusory character of duality. He also refuses to call the work a śāstra. It is a prakaraṇa, a short manual that confines itself to the essential topics of a system, and its subject is the meaning of OM.

ओमित्येतदक्षरमिदं सर्वं तस्योपव्याख्यानं
भूतं भवद् भविष्यदिति सर्वमोङ्कार एव
यच्चान्यत् त्रिकालातीतं तदप्योङ्कार एव ॥ १ ॥
omityetadakṣaramidaṃ sarvaṃ tasyopavyākhyānaṃ
bhūtaṃ bhavad bhaviṣyaditi sarvamoṅkāra eva
yaccānyat trikālātītaṃ tadapyoṅkāra eva

1OM: this syllable is all this. Its unfolding is this — what was, what is, what will be, all of it is OM; and whatever else there is beyond the three times, that too is OM.

सर्वं ह्येतद् ब्रह्मायमात्मा ब्रह्म सोऽयमात्मा चतुष्पात् ॥ २ ॥
sarvaṃ hyetad brahmāyamātmā brahma so'yamātmā catuṣpāt

2All this is Brahman. This self is Brahman. This self has four quarters.

Pāda, quarter, is literally a foot, and Śaṅkara is careful about which kind. These are not four feet like a cow's, unrelated to each other and all present at once; they are the four quarters of a coin, where a quarter goes into a half and a half into a whole. Viśva goes into Taijasa, Taijasa into Prājña, Prājña into the fourth. He adds a second distinction that decides how the rest is read: for the first three, quarter means instrument, a means of getting to the next; for the fourth it means what is got to. His reason for beginning with a syllable at all is that a name and the thing named are not two things, and OM is the name of the self.

जागरितस्थानो बहिष्प्रज्ञः सप्ताङ्ग एकोनविंशतिमुखः
स्थूलभुग्वैश्वानरः प्रथमः पादः ॥ ३ ॥
jāgaritasthāno bahiṣprajñaḥ saptāṅga ekonaviṃśatimukhaḥ
sthūlabhugvaiśvānaraḥ prathamaḥ pādaḥ

3The first quarter is Vaiśvānara: its place is waking, its knowing faces outward, seven-limbed, nineteen-mouthed, eating what is gross.

स्वप्नस्थानोऽन्तःप्रज्ञः सप्ताङ्ग एकोनविंशतिमुखः
प्रविविक्तभुक्तैजसो द्वितीयः पादः ॥ ४ ॥
svapnasthāno'ntaḥprajñaḥ saptāṅga ekonaviṃśatimukhaḥ
praviviktabhuktaijaso dvitīyaḥ pādaḥ

4The second is Taijasa: its place is dream, its knowing faces inward, seven-limbed, nineteen-mouthed, eating what is fine.

यत्र सुप्तो न कञ्चन कामं कामयते न कञ्चन स्वप्नं
पश्यति तत् सुषुप्तम् । सुषुप्तस्थान एकीभूतः प्रज्ञानघन
एवानन्दमयो ह्यानन्दभुक् चेतोमुखः प्राज्ञस्तृतीयः पादः ॥ ५ ॥
yatra supto na kañcana kāmaṃ kāmayate na kañcana svapnaṃ
paśyati tat suṣuptam | suṣuptasthāna ekībhūtaḥ prajñānaghana
evānandamayo hyānandabhuk cetomukhaḥ prājñastṛtīyaḥ pādaḥ

5Where one asleep desires no desire and sees no dream, that is deep sleep. The third is Prājña: its place is deep sleep, become one, a single mass of knowing, made of bliss, eating bliss, its mouth thought.

एष सर्वेश्वरः एष सर्वज्ञ एषोऽन्तर्याम्येष योनिः सर्वस्य
प्रभवाप्ययौ हि भूतानाम् ॥ ६ ॥
eṣa sarveśvaraḥ eṣa sarvajña eṣo'ntaryāmyeṣa yoniḥ sarvasya
prabhavāpyayau hi bhūtānām

6This is the lord of all, this the knower of all, this the inner ruler. This is the womb of all, the coming forth and the going back of beings.

The nineteen mouths are the instruments through which a person meets a world: five senses, five organs of action, five breaths, and mind, intellect, ego and memory. The seven limbs Śaṅkara takes from the Chāndogya's description of the cosmic person — heaven for a head, the sun for an eye, air for breath, space for the trunk, water below, earth for feet, and the sacrificial fire for a mouth — and the point of importing them is that the waking self is being described as the whole physical world seen from inside one body. Deny that, he says, and the self shrinks to the size of its own body, and Advaita turns into Sāṃkhya.

The equipment is the same in waking and in dream, which is the first hint of what the second treatise will do with the pair. What changes is only where the food comes from. Dream runs on the stock of impressions laid down in waking: Śaṅkara's image is a piece of canvas with the pictures already painted on it. Deep sleep is described differently again, not as a state but as a being — lord, knower, inner ruler, the womb of all — because nothing is left in it to divide knowing from itself. He is precise about the bliss: the sleeper is free of the effort of holding a subject apart from an object, and so has an abundance of bliss, but this is not bliss itself. And the lord of the sixth sentence is not a maker standing outside what he makes. That, Śaṅkara says, is what others hold; here the same thing is both what the world is made of and what makes it.

बहिष्प्रज्ञो विभुर्विश्वो ह्यन्तःप्रज्ञस्तु तैजसः ।
घनप्रज्ञस्तथा प्राज्ञ एक एव त्रिधा स्मृतः ॥ १ ॥
bahiṣprajño vibhurviśvo hyantaḥprajñastu taijasaḥ
ghanaprajñastathā prājña eka eva tridhā smṛtaḥ

1.1Viśva knows what is outside and is spread everywhere; Taijasa knows what is inside; Prājña is knowing made dense. One only, remembered as three.

दक्षिणाक्षिमुखे विश्वो मनस्यन्तस्तु तैजसः ।
आकाशे च हृदि प्राज्ञस्त्रिधा देहे व्यवस्थितः ॥ २ ॥
dakṣiṇākṣimukhe viśvo manasyantastu taijasaḥ
ākāśe ca hṛdi prājñastridhā dehe vyavasthitaḥ

1.2Viśva is in the right eye, Taijasa within the mind, Prājña in the space of the heart. Three standings in one body.

Śaṅkara reads the three seats as one thing a man can check in an afternoon. The person in the right eye is called Indha, the burning one, in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, and Śaṅkara takes him to be the same perceiver as the one in the sun; the eye is named for the rest of the senses rather than against them. Close it, and the forms just seen come back inside the mind as impressions, which is the second seat and the same perceiver working on a stock instead of on the world. Let the recollecting stop as well, and nothing is left to divide the seer from himself, and he is in the space of the heart. All three, he insists, are met with in waking. Nobody has to fall asleep to find them.

विश्वो हि स्थूलभुङ्नित्यं तैजसः प्रविविक्तभुक् ।
आनन्दभुक् तथा प्राज्ञस्त्रिधा भोगं निबोधत ॥ ३ ॥
viśvo hi sthūlabhuṅnityaṃ taijasaḥ praviviktabhuk
ānandabhuk tathā prājñastridhā bhogaṃ nibodhata

1.3Viśva always eats the gross, Taijasa what is fine, Prājña eats bliss. Know enjoyment to be threefold.

स्थूलं तर्पयते विश्वं प्रविविक्तं तु तैजसम् ।
आनन्दश्च तथा प्राज्ञं त्रिधा तृप्तिं निबोधत ॥ ४ ॥
sthūlaṃ tarpayate viśvaṃ praviviktaṃ tu taijasam
ānandaśca tathā prājñaṃ tridhā tṛptiṃ nibodhata

1.4The gross satisfies Viśva, the fine Taijasa, bliss Prājña. Know satisfaction to be threefold.

त्रिषु धामसु यद्भोज्यं भोक्ता यश्च प्रकीर्तितः ।
वेदैतदुभयं यस्तु स भुञ्जानो न लिप्यते ॥ ५ ॥
triṣu dhāmasu yadbhojyaṃ bhoktā yaśca prakīrtitaḥ
vedaitadubhayaṃ yastu sa bhuñjāno na lipyate

1.5Whoever knows both — what is enjoyed in the three places, and the enjoyer who has been named — is not stained though he enjoys.

The seats matter less than the arithmetic. Three positions, one occupant, and a food apiece: the solid world, the impressions it has left, and the rest that comes of holding neither apart from the other. Verse 5 then quietly changes the subject: a man who knows both the eaten and the eater is not touched by the eating, which is the first appearance in the text of a knower who is not a participant.

प्रभवः सर्वभावानां सतामिति विनिश्चयः ।
सर्वं जनयति प्राणश्चेतोंऽशून्पुरुषः पृथक् ॥ ६ ॥
prabhavaḥ sarvabhāvānāṃ satāmiti viniścayaḥ
sarvaṃ janayati prāṇaścetoṃ'śūnpuruṣaḥ pṛthak

1.6Existing things have an origin: so it is settled by some. Prāṇa brings everything forth, and the puruṣa sends out the rays of thought, each apart.

विभूतिं प्रसवं त्वन्ये मन्यन्ते सृष्टिचिन्तकाः ।
स्वप्नमायासरूपेति सृष्टिरन्यैर्विकल्पिता ॥ ७ ॥
vibhūtiṃ prasavaṃ tvanye manyante sṛṣṭicintakāḥ
svapnamāyāsarūpeti sṛṣṭiranyairvikalpitā

1.7Others who think about creation hold that it is the display of his power. By others creation is imagined to be like a dream or a conjuring.

इच्छामात्रं प्रभोः सृष्टिरिति सृष्टौ विनिश्चिताः ।
कालात्प्रसूतिं भूतानां मन्यन्ते कालचिन्तकाः ॥ ८ ॥
icchāmātraṃ prabhoḥ sṛṣṭiriti sṛṣṭau viniścitāḥ
kālātprasūtiṃ bhūtānāṃ manyante kālacintakāḥ

1.8Those who are certain about creation say it is the Lord's will and nothing more. Those who think in terms of time hold that beings are born out of time.

भोगार्थं सृष्टिरित्यन्ये क्रीडार्थमिति चापरे ।
देवस्यैष स्वभावोऽयमाप्तकामस्य का स्पृहा ॥ ९ ॥
bhogārthaṃ sṛṣṭirityanye krīḍārthamiti cāpare
devasyaiṣa svabhāvo'yamāptakāmasya kā spṛhā

1.9Some say creation is for enjoyment, others that it is for play. But this is the nature of the shining one — what is there to want for one who already has everything?

These four verses are a catalogue of the answers available in the seventh century to the question why there is a world: the world is the display of God's power, the world is a dream or a conjuring trick, the world is God's will and nothing more, the world is what time brings forth, the world is for God's enjoyment, the world is God's play. Śaṅkara calls the people who give them sṛṣṭi-cintakāḥ, speculators about creation. Gauḍapāda's answer is not another item on the list. It is that the question has no purchase: creating implies wanting, wanting implies lack, and the shining one lacks nothing. What looks like creation is his nature, the way heat is the nature of fire. Nikhilananda, writing in 1936, compares asking for a cause of the manifold to diagnosing the headache of a headless man.

Śaṅkara's gloss on the dream-and-conjuring theory contains the image the fourth treatise will make an argument out of. A juggler throws a rope into the sky, climbs it with weapons in his hand, disappears, is cut to pieces in a fight above, and falls back down; the spectators watch a man dismembered and take no interest in whether it happened, because they can see the real juggler standing on the ground the whole time, unmoved and unseen. The three states are the rope; Viśva, Taijasa and Prājña are the one who seems to have climbed it; the fourth is the man who never left the ground.

नान्तःप्रज्ञं न बहिष्प्रज्ञं नोभयतःप्रज्ञं न प्रज्ञानघनं
न प्रज्ञं नाप्रज्ञम् । अदृष्टमव्यवहार्यमग्राह्यमलक्षणं
अचिन्त्यमव्यपदेश्यमेकात्मप्रत्ययसारं प्रपञ्चोपशमं
शान्तं शिवमद्वैतं चतुर्थं मन्यन्ते स आत्मा स विज्ञेयः ॥ ७ ॥
nāntaḥprajñaṃ na bahiṣprajñaṃ nobhayataḥprajñaṃ na prajñānaghanaṃ
na prajñaṃ nāprajñam | adṛṣṭamavyavahāryamagrāhyamalakṣaṇaṃ
acintyamavyapadeśyamekātmapratyayasāraṃ prapañcopaśamaṃ
śāntaṃ śivamadvaitaṃ caturthaṃ manyante sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ

7Not knowing what is within, not knowing what is without, not knowing both, not a mass of knowing, neither knowing nor unknowing; unseen, beyond dealing, ungraspable, without a mark, unthinkable, not to be pointed at, its essence the certainty of the one self, in which the spread-out world comes to rest, at peace, benign, without a second: that is what they hold the fourth to be. That is the self. That is what is to be known.

The seventh sentence is the centre of the Upaniṣad and it is written almost entirely in denials. Six of them close off the map the first six sentences have just drawn: not the outward knowing of waking, not the inward knowing of dream, not both together, not the undivided mass of deep sleep, not knowing, not its absence. Six more take away every handle by which anything is ordinarily held: sight, transaction, grasp, mark, thought, reference. Only then do three positive words arrive, and they are not descriptions but what is left: śānta, śiva, advaita.

Śaṅkara stages the obvious objection before the sentence is even quoted, and gives it to the objector in five words: "Then it becomes mere void." His answer is a principle he will use for the rest of the book: an imagination cannot exist without something to be imagined on. Silver is seen on mother-of-pearl, a snake on a rope, a man on a tree stump, water on sand; there is always a real thing carrying the false one. Denying the attributes removes what was superimposed and leaves the carrier. A second objector then says that a thing which cannot be indicated at all is like the horns of a hare, and pursuing it is a waste of a life. Śaṅkara's answer is that knowing the fourth to be one's own self ends the hunt for what one is not, as knowing the shell ends the desire for the silver. The last clause of the sentence does the same work: sa ātmā sa vijñeyaḥ, that is the self, that is what is to be known. Turīya means only "the fourth", and Śaṅkara warns that it is not a fourth item beside the other three. The negations establish it as the very self that was present in all three states all along.

निवृत्तेः सर्वदुःखानामीशानः प्रभुरव्ययः ।
अद्वैतः सर्वभावानां देवस्तुर्यो विभुः स्मृतः ॥ १० ॥
nivṛtteḥ sarvaduḥkhānāmīśānaḥ prabhuravyayaḥ
advaitaḥ sarvabhāvānāṃ devasturyo vibhuḥ smṛtaḥ

1.10Turīya is held to be the lord, the ruler who does not waste, who ends all sorrow, without a second, the shining one spread through all beings.

कार्यकारणबद्धौ ताविष्येते विश्वतैजसौ ।
प्राज्ञः कारणबद्धस्तु द्वौ तौ तुर्ये न सिध्यतः ॥ ११ ॥
kāryakāraṇabaddhau tāviṣyete viśvataijasau
prājñaḥ kāraṇabaddhastu dvau tau turye na sidhyataḥ

1.11Viśva and Taijasa are bound by cause and effect; Prājña is bound by cause alone. Neither holds in the fourth.

नाऽऽत्मानं न परांश्चैव न सत्यं नापि चानृतम् ।
प्राज्ञः किञ्चन संवेत्ति तुर्यं तत्सर्वदृक्सदा ॥ १२ ॥
nā''tmānaṃ na parāṃścaiva na satyaṃ nāpi cānṛtam
prājñaḥ kiñcana saṃvetti turyaṃ tatsarvadṛksadā

1.12Prājña knows nothing whatever — not himself, not another, not the true, not the false. The fourth sees everything, always.

द्वैतस्याग्रहणं तुल्यमुभयोः प्राज्ञतुर्ययोः ।
बीजनिद्रायुतः प्राज्ञः सा च तुर्ये न विद्यते ॥ १३ ॥
dvaitasyāgrahaṇaṃ tulyamubhayoḥ prājñaturyayoḥ
bījanidrāyutaḥ prājñaḥ sā ca turye na vidyate

1.13Not grasping duality is common to Prājña and to turīya. But Prājña keeps the sleep that holds a seed, and that sleep is not in the fourth.

स्वप्ननिद्रायुतावाद्यौ प्राज्ञस्त्वस्वप्ननिद्रया ।
न निद्रां नैव च स्वप्नं तुर्ये पश्यन्ति निश्चिताः ॥ १४ ॥
svapnanidrāyutāvādyau prājñastvasvapnanidrayā
na nidrāṃ naiva ca svapnaṃ turye paśyanti niścitāḥ

1.14The first two are joined to sleep with dreaming, Prājña to sleep without dreaming. Those who are settled see neither sleep nor dream in the fourth.

अन्यथा गृह्णतः स्वप्नो निद्रा तत्त्वमजानतः ।
विपर्यासे तयोः क्षीणे तुरीयं पदमश्नुते ॥ १५ ॥
anyathā gṛhṇataḥ svapno nidrā tattvamajānataḥ
viparyāse tayoḥ kṣīṇe turīyaṃ padamaśnute

1.15Dream belongs to one who takes a thing for what it is not; sleep to one who does not know what is. When both errors are worn away, the fourth is reached.

अनादिमायया सुप्तो यदा जीवः प्रबुध्यते ।
अजमनिद्रमस्वप्नमद्वैतं बुध्यते तदा ॥ १६ ॥
anādimāyayā supto yadā jīvaḥ prabudhyate
ajamanidramasvapnamadvaitaṃ budhyate tadā

1.16When the jīva, asleep under a beginningless illusion, is woken, he wakes to what is unborn, sleepless, dreamless, without a second.

Verses 11 to 16 are the sharpest thing in the first treatise, because they refuse the identification that the sixth sentence invites. Deep sleep is not the goal. Prājña and turīya agree in that neither grasps duality, and that agreement is what makes deep sleep tempting as a picture of liberation; but Prājña is asleep, and sleep holds a seed. The word is bīja-nidrā, seed-sleep: the condition out of which waking and dream grow again the moment it stirs. Turīya has nothing to grow from, which is also Śaṅkara's reason for saying that the man who knows it does not come back: what he has known is not a cause.

Verse 15 redefines its two terms so that they cover more than they seem to. Dream is mis-taking, seeing one thing as another, and in Śaṅkara's reading it covers waking as well, since waking also takes a rope for a snake. Sleep is not knowing at all, and it covers all three states, since none of them knows what is. Waking and dream thus fall into one class and deep sleep into another, effect and cause. Verse 16 then says what waking up from the second kind of sleep means, and Śaṅkara fills in the content of the sleep in the first person: this is my father, this is my son, this is my property, these are my animals, I am happy, I am miserable, I have lost by this and gained by that. The waking is not something the sleeper does. It comes from a teacher who has seen it himself, and what the teacher says is that you are not this thing made of cause and effect.

प्रपञ्चो यदि विद्येत निवर्तेत न संशयः ।
मायामात्रमिदं द्वैतमद्वैतं परमार्थतः ॥ १७ ॥
prapañco yadi vidyeta nivarteta na saṃśayaḥ
māyāmātramidaṃ dvaitamadvaitaṃ paramārthataḥ

1.17If the spread-out world existed, it would surely come to an end. This duality is nothing but illusion; without a second, in the highest sense.

विकल्पो विनिवर्तेत कल्पितो यदि केनचित् ।
उपदेशादयं वादो ज्ञाते द्वैतं न विद्यते ॥ १८ ॥
vikalpo vinivarteta kalpito yadi kenacit
upadeśādayaṃ vādo jñāte dvaitaṃ na vidyate

1.18The many would cease if anyone had imagined them. This talk is for teaching; once it is known, there is no duality.

Verse 17 is the hinge of the book, and it is a conditional that runs the other way from the one a reader expects. If the world were real, its ending would be a real event and liberation would be a change; because there is no such change, the world was never there to end. Verse 18 then says what the whole treatise has been doing. Talk of quarters, states, sounds and seats is upadeśa, teaching, a ladder; in the known thing there is no duality and therefore nothing that the ladder described.

सोऽयमात्माध्यक्षरमोङ्कारोऽधिमात्रं पादा मात्रा मात्राश्च पादा
अकार उकारो मकार इति ॥ ८ ॥
so'yamātmādhyakṣaramoṅkāro'dhimātraṃ pādā mātrā mātrāśca pādā
akāra ukāro makāra iti

8This self, as the syllable, is OM; as the parts, the quarters are the sounds and the sounds are the quarters: a, u and m.

जागरितस्थानो वैश्वानरोऽकारः प्रथमा मात्राऽऽप्तेरादिमत्त्वाद्
वाऽऽप्नोति ह वै सर्वान् कामानादिश्च भवति य एवं वेद ॥ ९ ॥
jāgaritasthāno vaiśvānaro'kāraḥ prathamā mātrā''pterādimattvād
vā''pnoti ha vai sarvān kāmānādiśca bhavati ya evaṃ veda

9Waking, Vaiśvānara, is a, the first part, from reaching or from being first. One who knows this reaches all he wants, and comes first.

स्वप्नस्थानस्तैजस उकारो द्वितीया मात्रोत्कर्षात्
उभयत्वाद्वोत्कर्षति ह वै ज्ञानसन्ततिं समानश्च भवति
नास्याब्रह्मवित्कुले भवति य एवं वेद ॥ १० ॥
svapnasthānastaijasa ukāro dvitīyā mātrotkarṣāt
ubhayatvādvotkarṣati ha vai jñānasantatiṃ samānaśca bhavati
nāsyābrahmavitkule bhavati ya evaṃ veda

10Dream, Taijasa, is u, the second part, from being higher or from being between. One who knows this raises the run of his knowing, becomes even, and has no one in his line who does not know Brahman.

सुषुप्तस्थानः प्राज्ञो मकारस्तृतीया मात्रा मितेरपीतेर्वा
मिनोति ह वा इदं सर्वमपीतिश्च भवति य एवं वेद ॥ ११ ॥
suṣuptasthānaḥ prājño makārastṛtīyā mātrā miterapītervā
minoti ha vā idaṃ sarvamapītiśca bhavati ya evaṃ veda

11Deep sleep, Prājña, is m, the third part, from measuring or from taking in. One who knows this measures all this, and takes it in.

The second half of the Upaniṣad walks the same ground in sound. Each identification is carried by a pun on the name of the sound, and the puns are not ornament: a is the first letter and waking is the first experience; u is what lies between; m closes the mouth and ends the utterance, as sleep takes the world in. The fourth is the one that is not a sound.

विश्वस्यात्वविवक्षायामादिसामान्यमुत्कटम् ।
मात्रासम्प्रतिपत्तौ स्यादाप्तिसामान्यमेव च ॥ १९ ॥
viśvasyātvavivakṣāyāmādisāmānyamutkaṭam
mātrāsampratipattau syādāptisāmānyameva ca

1.19When Viśva is to be spoken of as a, what stands out in common is coming first; and in fitting sound to quarter, reaching.

तैजसस्योत्वविज्ञान उत्कर्षो दृश्यते स्फुटम् ।
मात्रासम्प्रतिपत्तौ स्यादुभयत्वं तथाविधम् ॥ २० ॥
taijasasyotvavijñāna utkarṣo dṛśyate sphuṭam
mātrāsampratipattau syādubhayatvaṃ tathāvidham

1.20In knowing Taijasa as u, being higher is plainly seen; and in fitting sound to quarter, being between, of the same kind.

मकारभावे प्राज्ञस्य मानसामान्यमुत्कटम् ।
मात्रासम्प्रतिपत्तौ तु लयसामान्यमेव च ॥ २१ ॥
makārabhāve prājñasya mānasāmānyamutkaṭam
mātrāsampratipattau tu layasāmānyameva ca

1.21In taking Prājña as m, what stands out in common is measure; and in fitting sound to quarter, absorption.

त्रिषु धामसु यत्तुल्यं सामान्यं वेत्ति निश्चितः ।
स पूज्यः सर्वभूतानां वन्द्यश्चैव महामुनिः ॥ २२ ॥
triṣu dhāmasu yattulyaṃ sāmānyaṃ vetti niścitaḥ
sa pūjyaḥ sarvabhūtānāṃ vandyaścaiva mahāmuniḥ

1.22Whoever knows for certain what is common in the three places is worthy of the worship of all beings, a great sage.

अकारो नयते विश्वमुकारश्चापि तैजसम् ।
मकारश्च पुनः प्राज्ञं नामात्रे विद्यते गतिः ॥ २३ ॥
akāro nayate viśvamukāraścāpi taijasam
makāraśca punaḥ prājñaṃ nāmātre vidyate gatiḥ

1.23The sound a leads to Viśva, u to Taijasa, m to Prājña. In what has no part there is no going.

अमात्रश्चतुर्थोऽव्यवहार्यः प्रपञ्चोपशमः शिवोऽद्वैत
एवमोङ्कार आत्मैव संविशत्यात्मनाऽऽत्मानं य एवं वेद ॥ १२ ॥
amātraścaturtho'vyavahāryaḥ prapañcopaśamaḥ śivo'dvaita
evamoṅkāra ātmaiva saṃviśatyātmanā''tmānaṃ ya evaṃ veda

12The fourth is without parts, beyond dealing, the spread-out world at rest, benign, without a second. So OM is the self. One who knows this enters the self by the self.

ओंकारं पादशो विद्यात्पादा मात्रा न संशयः ।
ओंकारं पादशो ज्ञात्वा न किञ्चिदपि चिन्तयेत् ॥ २४ ॥
oṃkāraṃ pādaśo vidyātpādā mātrā na saṃśayaḥ
oṃkāraṃ pādaśo jñātvā na kiñcidapi cintayet

1.24Know OM quarter by quarter; the quarters are the sounds, there is no doubt of it. Knowing OM quarter by quarter, one should think of nothing at all.

युञ्जीत प्रणवे चेतः प्रणवो ब्रह्म निर्भयम् ।
प्रणवे नित्ययुक्तस्य न भयं विद्यते क्वचित् ॥ २५ ॥
yuñjīta praṇave cetaḥ praṇavo brahma nirbhayam
praṇave nityayuktasya na bhayaṃ vidyate kvacit

1.25Yoke the mind to the praṇava. The praṇava is Brahman, in which there is no fear. For one always joined to it there is no fear anywhere.

प्रणवो ह्यपरं ब्रह्म प्रणवश्च परः स्मृतः ।
अपूर्वोऽनन्तरोऽबाह्योऽनपरः प्रणवोऽव्ययः ॥ २६ ॥
praṇavo hyaparaṃ brahma praṇavaśca paraḥ smṛtaḥ
apūrvo'nantaro'bāhyo'naparaḥ praṇavo'vyayaḥ

1.26The praṇava is the lower Brahman, and it is held to be the higher: nothing before it, nothing inside, nothing outside, nothing after, and it does not waste.

सर्वस्य प्रणवो ह्यादिर्मध्यमन्तस्तथैव च ।
एवं हि प्रणवं ज्ञात्वा व्यश्नुते तदनन्तरम् ॥ २७ ॥
sarvasya praṇavo hyādirmadhyamantastathaiva ca
evaṃ hi praṇavaṃ jñātvā vyaśnute tadanantaram

1.27The praṇava is the beginning of everything, and the middle, and the end. Knowing it so, one reaches that, at once.

प्रणवं हीश्वरं विद्यात्सर्वस्य हृदि संस्थितम् ।
सर्वव्यापिनमोङ्कारं मत्वा धीरो न शोचति ॥ २८ ॥
praṇavaṃ hīśvaraṃ vidyātsarvasya hṛdi saṃsthitam
sarvavyāpinamoṅkāraṃ matvā dhīro na śocati

1.28Know the praṇava to be the lord, seated in the heart of all. Knowing OM to pervade everything, the steady man does not grieve.

अमात्रोऽनन्तमात्रश्च द्वैतस्योपशमः शिवः ।
ओंकारो विदितो येन स मुनिर्नेतरो जनः ॥ २९ ॥
amātro'nantamātraśca dvaitasyopaśamaḥ śivaḥ
oṃkāro vidito yena sa munirnetaro janaḥ

1.29Whoever has known OM — without parts, of endless parts, the ending of duality, benign — he is the sage, and no one else.

Verse 23 is the practical point of the whole sound analysis, and it is a negative one. Each sound delivers its meditator to its own quarter: a to the waking cosmos, u to the cosmic mind, m to the causal condition. In the partless fourth there is no arriving, because arriving needs a cause and the fourth is not one. Verse 24 tells the student to know OM quarter by quarter and then to think of nothing at all, which is the first statement of the discipline the third treatise will name. Verse 26 puts the whole of the tradition's two Brahmans into one line — the syllable is the lower Brahman and it is also the higher — and Nikhilananda's note observes drily that the first half is for ordinary students and the second for the sharpest. The chapter closes on the man who has known the soundless OM. He is the sage, says verse 29, and no one else; and the note adds what the verse implies, that learning without the thing itself is worth nothing.

Vaitathya: waking is worth no more than dream

The second treatise drops scripture and argues. Śaṅkara says so at the opening: what the first chapter established on the authority of the Vedas can equally be settled by reasoning, and that is what the second chapter is for. Vaitathya is the noun from vitatha, not-so; Nikhilananda's chapter heading is Illusion. What is declared not-so is every object, inner or outer. The argument begins where nobody disputes it, in dream, and then removes the difference between dream and waking one reason at a time.

वैतथ्यं सर्वभावानां स्वप्न आहुर्मनीषिणः ।
अन्तःस्थानात्तु भावानां संवृतत्वेन हेतुना ॥ १ ॥
vaitathyaṃ sarvabhāvānāṃ svapna āhurmanīṣiṇaḥ
antaḥsthānāttu bhāvānāṃ saṃvṛtatvena hetunā

2.1The wise call all things seen in dream unreal, because they are seen inside the body, and because the space there is shut in.

अदीर्घत्वाच्च कालस्य गत्वा देशान्न पश्यति ।
प्रतिबुद्धश्च वै सर्वस्तस्मिन्देशे न विद्यते ॥ २ ॥
adīrghatvācca kālasya gatvā deśānna paśyati
pratibuddhaśca vai sarvastasmindeśe na vidyate

2.2Nor can the time be long enough for him to have gone to those places and seen them; and when he wakes, no one is in that place.

अभावश्च रथादीनां श्रूयते न्यायपूर्वकम् ।
वैतथ्यं तेन वै प्राप्तं स्वप्न आहुः प्रकाशितम् ॥ ३ ॥
abhāvaśca rathādīnāṃ śrūyate nyāyapūrvakam
vaitathyaṃ tena vai prāptaṃ svapna āhuḥ prakāśitam

2.3That there are no chariots there is heard in scripture, arrived at by reasoning. So the unreality that reasoning reaches is what scripture has declared.

Three arguments, all from ordinary experience. A dreamed mountain or elephant is seen inside a body, and there is no room for a mountain in the channels of a body. A man asleep in the east dreams himself in the north, but the journey there and back would take a month and he has been asleep for an hour; and when he wakes he is where he lay down, not where he went. Śaṅkara adds a third that is entirely social: the people he met in daylight in his dream never say afterwards that they met him. Verse 3 then cites the Bṛhadāraṇyaka's line that in dream there are no chariots and no roads, and is careful about the order: reason got there first, and scripture is reporting the same finding for its own purpose, which is to show that in dream the self shines by its own light.

अन्तःस्थानात्तु भेदानां तस्माज्जागरिते स्मृतम् ।
यथा तत्र तथा स्वप्ने संवृतत्वेन भिद्यते ॥ ४ ॥
antaḥsthānāttu bhedānāṃ tasmājjāgarite smṛtam
yathā tatra tathā svapne saṃvṛtatvena bhidyate

2.4Because they are seen inside, the differences of the waking state are called unreal too. As it is there, so it is in dream; the difference lies in the shutting in.

स्वप्नजागरितस्थाने ह्येकमाहुर्मनीषिणः ।
भेदानां हि समत्वेन प्रसिद्धेनैव हेतुना ॥ ५ ॥
svapnajāgaritasthāne hyekamāhurmanīṣiṇaḥ
bhedānāṃ hi samatvena prasiddhenaiva hetunā

2.5The wise say the dream state and the waking state are one, because the differences are alike, on the ground already given.

This is the move the whole treatise turns on, and Śaṅkara sets it out as a formal inference: what is to be proved is that waking objects are unreal; the reason is that they are perceived; the example is dream; and the reason holds of the waking case as it held of the dream case. The one difference anyone can point to, that dream space is cramped and waking space is not, is noticed only from waking. Inside the dream nothing seems cramped at all.

आदावन्ते च यन्नास्ति वर्तमानेऽपि तत्तथा ॥
वितथैः सदृशाः सन्तोऽवितथा इव लक्षिताः ॥ ६ ॥
ādāvante ca yannāsti vartamāne'pi tattathā
vitathaiḥ sadṛśāḥ santo'vitathā iva lakṣitāḥ

2.6What is not there at the beginning and not at the end is not there in the middle either. They are like what is false, and yet they are taken to be otherwise.

सप्रयोजनता तेषां स्वप्ने विप्रतिपद्यते ।
तस्मादाद्यन्तवत्त्वेन मिथ्यैव खलु ते स्मृताः ॥ ७ ॥
saprayojanatā teṣāṃ svapne vipratipadyate
tasmādādyantavattvena mithyaiva khalu te smṛtāḥ

2.7Their serving a purpose is contradicted in dream. Therefore, having a beginning and an end, they are held to be false.

Verse 6 is the criterion, and it is the sentence of the text most often quoted on its own: what is absent at the beginning and absent at the end is absent in the middle. Śaṅkara applies it to everything perceived, on the ground that whatever changes was not there before its change and will not be there after it. Verse 7 answers the standing objection to that criterion. A realist says the test of a real thing is that it works: food stops hunger, a cart carries you, and dream food does not. Gauḍapāda's answer is that the objection cancels itself. A man eats his fill in waking, falls asleep and dreams himself starving; a man feasts in dream and wakes up hungry. Dream means serve dream ends exactly as waking means serve waking ends, and each state stultifies the other's currency.

अपूर्वं स्थानिधर्मो हि यथा स्वर्गनिवासिनाम् ।
तान्यं प्रेक्षते गत्वा यथैवेह सुशिक्षितः ॥ ८ ॥
apūrvaṃ sthānidharmo hi yathā svarganivāsinām
tānyaṃ prekṣate gatvā yathaiveha suśikṣitaḥ

2.8What is not there before is a property of the place, as with those who live in heaven. He goes and sees it there, as a well-taught man sees things here.

स्वप्नवृत्तावपि त्वन्तश्चेतसा कल्पितं त्वसत् ।
बहिश्चेतोगृहीतं सद्दृष्टं वैतथ्यमेतयोः ॥ ९ ॥
svapnavṛttāvapi tvantaścetasā kalpitaṃ tvasat
bahiścetogṛhītaṃ saddṛṣṭaṃ vaitathyametayoḥ

2.9Even within a dream, what the mind imagines inside is unreal, and what the mind takes hold of outside is real. Both are known to be untrue.

जाग्रद्वृत्तावपि त्वन्तश्चेतसा कल्पितं त्वसत् ।
बहिश्चेतोगृहीतं सद्युक्तं वैतथ्यमेतयोः ॥ १० ॥
jāgradvṛttāvapi tvantaścetasā kalpitaṃ tvasat
bahiścetogṛhītaṃ sadyuktaṃ vaitathyametayoḥ

2.10Even within waking, what the mind imagines inside is unreal, and what the mind takes hold of outside is real. Both, by the same reason, are untrue.

The objection behind verse 8 is that dreams show things waking never shows — the dreamer with eight arms, the elephant with four tusks — so they are a species of their own and not a good model for anything. Gauḍapāda's answer is that strange objects belong to the state, as a thousand eyes belong to Indra in heaven and as the sights of a road belong to that road for the traveller who knows it. Verses 9 and 10 are the pair that does the real damage. Inside a dream a dreamer distinguishes what he merely imagines from what he actually sees; he has a working distinction between the real and the unreal, and it is entirely internal to the dream. Waking has the same distinction, drawn in the same way, and there is no reason yet given why it should be worth more.

उभयोरपि वैतथ्यं भेदानां स्थानयोर्यदि ।
क एतान्बुध्यते भेदान् को वै तेषां विकल्पकः ॥ ११ ॥
ubhayorapi vaitathyaṃ bhedānāṃ sthānayoryadi
ka etānbudhyate bhedān ko vai teṣāṃ vikalpakaḥ

2.11If the differences in both places are untrue, who is it that knows these differences, and who imagines them?

कल्पयत्यात्मनाऽऽत्मानमात्मा देवः स्वमायया
स एव बुध्यते भेदानिति वेदान्तनिश्चयः ॥ १२ ॥
kalpayatyātmanā''tmānamātmā devaḥ svamāyayā
sa eva budhyate bhedāniti vedāntaniścayaḥ

2.12The shining self imagines himself by himself, through his own illusion. He alone knows the differences. This is the settled view of Vedānta.

विकरोत्यपरान्भावानन्तश्चित्ते व्यवस्थितान् ।
नियतांश्च बहिश्चित्त एवं कल्पयते प्रभुः ॥ १३ ॥
vikarotyaparānbhāvānantaścitte vyavasthitān
niyatāṃśca bahiścitta evaṃ kalpayate prabhuḥ

2.13He brings forth the various things that stand inside the mind, and the fixed things that stand outside. So the lord imagines.

चित्तकाला हि येऽन्तस्तु द्वयकालाश्च ये बहिः ॥
कल्पिता एव ते सर्वे विशेषो नान्यहेतुकः ॥ १४ ॥
cittakālā hi ye'ntastu dvayakālāśca ye bahiḥ
kalpitā eva te sarve viśeṣo nānyahetukaḥ

2.14Things that last as long as the thought, and things that answer to two times, are all imagined. There is no other ground for telling them apart.

अव्यक्ता एव येऽन्तस्तु स्फुटा एव च ये बहिः ।
कल्पिता एव ते सर्वे विशेषस्त्विन्द्रियान्तरे ॥ १५ ॥
avyaktā eva ye'ntastu sphuṭā eva ca ye bahiḥ
kalpitā eva te sarve viśeṣastvindriyāntare

2.15Things unmanifest within and things plain without are all imagined. The difference lies only in the organ.

Verse 11 is the objector's question and the treatise takes it seriously. If everything perceived is false, what holds knowledge and memory up? Deny an answer, Śaṅkara says, and you have nihilism. The answer is that the self imagines, by its own power, in itself, and knows what it has imagined, as a rope is imagined to be a snake; there is no second thing doing the imagining, and no maker standing outside the world like a potter outside his pot. Verse 14 takes the strongest realist objection, that outer things are not mind-dependent because they answer to two times — they persist, and two observers can date them against each other. Śaṅkara's reply is that the two-timedness is nothing but the objects measuring each other. What is thought to anchor the world is one appearance holding another in place.

जीवं कल्पयते पूर्वं ततो भावान्पृथग्विधान् ।
बाह्यानाध्यात्मिकांश्चैव यथाविद्यस्तथास्मृतिः ॥ १६ ॥
jīvaṃ kalpayate pūrvaṃ tato bhāvānpṛthagvidhān
bāhyānādhyātmikāṃścaiva yathāvidyastathāsmṛtiḥ

2.16First the jīva is imagined, and then the various things, outer and inner. As is one's knowledge, so is one's memory.

अनिश्चिता यथा रज्जुरन्धकारे विकल्पिता ।
सर्पधारादिभिर्भावैस्तद्वदात्मा विकल्पितः ॥ १७ ॥
aniścitā yathā rajjurandhakāre vikalpitā
sarpadhārādibhirbhāvaistadvadātmā vikalpitaḥ

2.17As a rope, not made out in the dark, is imagined to be a snake, a line of water, a stick, so is the self imagined.

निश्चितायां यथा रज्ज्वां विकल्पो विनिवर्तते ।
रज्जुरेवेति चाद्वैतं तद्वदात्मविनिश्चयः ॥ १८ ॥
niścitāyāṃ yathā rajjvāṃ vikalpo vinivartate
rajjureveti cādvaitaṃ tadvadātmaviniścayaḥ

2.18When the rope is made out, the imaginings stop and it is the rope alone and nothing else. So is the settling of the self.

Verse 16 fixes an order that matters: the individual is not first in the world and then deceived about it; the individual is the first imagination, and everything else is imagined for him to know. The engine that keeps it going is a loop between knowing and remembering, each making the other, which is why the world is stable without being real. Then comes the illustration the tradition has never put down. A rope in bad light is taken for a snake, or a trickle of water, or a stick; the mistakes differ and the rope does not; and when the rope is seen the snake does not go anywhere, because it was never there to go.

Śaṅkara adds one condition that is easy to miss. The mistake is possible only because the rope was never properly seen. Nobody mistakes his own fingers for something else, because they are known. Ignorance is not an extra thing added to the situation; it is the whole of what makes the situation possible.

प्राणादिभिरनन्तैश्च भावैरेतैर्विकल्पितः ।
मायैषा तस्य देवस्य यया सम्मोहितः स्वयम् ॥ १९ ॥
prāṇādibhiranantaiśca bhāvairetairvikalpitaḥ
māyaiṣā tasya devasya yayā sammohitaḥ svayam

2.19He is imagined as the breath and as endless other things. This is the illusion of that shining one, by which he is himself deluded.

प्राण इति प्राणविदो भूतानीति च तद्विदः ।
गुणा इति गुणविदस्तत्त्वानीति च तद्विदः ॥ २० ॥
prāṇa iti prāṇavido bhūtānīti ca tadvidaḥ
guṇā iti guṇavidastattvānīti ca tadvidaḥ

2.20Those who know the breath call it the breath; those who know the elements, the elements; those who know the strands, the strands; those who know the categories, the categories.

पादा इति पादविदो विषया इति तद्विदः ।
लोका इति लोकविदो देवा इति च तद्विदः ॥ २१ ॥
pādā iti pādavido viṣayā iti tadvidaḥ
lokā iti lokavido devā iti ca tadvidaḥ

2.21Those who know the quarters call it the quarters; those who know objects, objects; those who know worlds, worlds; those who know gods, the gods.

वेदा इति वेदविदो यज्ञा इति च तद्विदः ।
भोक्तेति च भोक्तृविदो भोज्यमिति च तद्विदः ॥ २२ ॥
vedā iti vedavido yajñā iti ca tadvidaḥ
bhokteti ca bhoktṛvido bhojyamiti ca tadvidaḥ

2.22Those who know the Vedas call it the Vedas; those who know sacrifices, sacrifices; those who know the enjoyer, the enjoyer; and those who know the enjoyed, the enjoyed.

सूक्ष्म इति सूक्ष्मविदः स्थूल इति च तद्विदः ।
मूर्त इति मूर्तविदोऽमूर्त इति च तद्विदः ॥ २३ ॥
sūkṣma iti sūkṣmavidaḥ sthūla iti ca tadvidaḥ
mūrta iti mūrtavido'mūrta iti ca tadvidaḥ

2.23Those who know the subtle call it subtle; those who know the gross, gross; those who know what has form, form; those who know the formless, the formless.

काल इति कालविदो दिश इति च तद्विदः ।
वादा इति वादविदो भुवनानीति तद्विदः ॥ २४ ॥
kāla iti kālavido diśa iti ca tadvidaḥ
vādā iti vādavido bhuvanānīti tadvidaḥ

2.24Those who know time call it time; those who know the directions, the directions; the debaters call it the matter in dispute; those who know the worlds, the worlds.

मन इति मनोविदो बुद्धिरिति च तद्विदः ।
चित्तमिति चित्तविदो धर्माधर्मौ च तद्विदः ॥ २५ ॥
mana iti manovido buddhiriti ca tadvidaḥ
cittamiti cittavido dharmādharmau ca tadvidaḥ

2.25Those who know mind call it mind; those who know intellect, intellect; those who know thought, thought; and the ritualists call it merit and demerit.

पञ्चविंशक इत्येके षड्विंश चापरे ।
एकत्रिंशक इत्याहुरनन्त इति चापरे ॥ २६ ॥
pañcaviṃśaka ityeke ṣaḍviṃśa cāpare
ekatriṃśaka ityāhurananta iti cāpare

2.26Some say the categories are twenty-five, others twenty-six; some say thirty-one, and others say they are endless.

The numbers in verse 26 are not invented for the joke. Twenty-five are the categories of Sāṃkhya: nature, the great one, the I-maker, the five subtle elements, the five organs of sense, the five of action, the five objects, mind, and the person. The followers of Patañjali add the lord and make twenty-six. The Pāśupatas add six more — passion, ignorance, time, portion, illusion and necessity — and make thirty-one. The commentary draws the only conclusion the list will support: schools that cannot agree on the count are not reading the number off the thing itself.

लोकांल्लोकविदः प्राहुराश्रमा इति तद्विदः ।
स्त्रीपुंनपुंसकं लैङ्गाः परापरमथापरे ॥ २७ ॥
lokāṃllokavidaḥ prāhurāśramā iti tadvidaḥ
strīpuṃnapuṃsakaṃ laiṅgāḥ parāparamathāpare

2.27Those who please men call it pleasure; those who know the stages of life, the stages; grammarians call it masculine, feminine and neuter; others call it the higher and the lower.

सृष्टिरिति सृष्टिविदो लय इति च तद्विदः ।
स्थितिरिति स्थितिविदः सर्वे चेह तु सर्वदा ॥ २८ ॥
sṛṣṭiriti sṛṣṭivido laya iti ca tadvidaḥ
sthitiriti sthitividaḥ sarve ceha tu sarvadā

2.28Those who know creation call it creation; those who know dissolution, dissolution; those who know maintenance, maintenance. All of these, here, always.

यं भावं दर्शयेद्यस्य तं भावं स तु पश्यति ।
तं चावति स भूत्वाऽसौ तद्ग्रहः समुपैति तम् ॥ २९ ॥
yaṃ bhāvaṃ darśayedyasya taṃ bhāvaṃ sa tu paśyati
taṃ cāvati sa bhūtvā'sau tadgrahaḥ samupaiti tam

2.29Whatever thing is shown to a man, that is the thing he sees; and becoming it, it protects him; and possessed by it, he arrives at it.

Ten verses of a list that could go on for ten more, and the comedy is deliberate. Every school's ultimate principle is on it: the breath of one school, the elements of the materialists, the three strands of Sāṃkhya, the counted categories of the systems, the merit and demerit of the ritualists, the void of one Buddhist school. Śaṅkara declines to gloss the items one by one, on the ground that it would serve no purpose and the meaning is plain enough. The point is made by the shape of the list rather than by its contents: what each school has found is the self, seen through what that school was looking with.

Verse 29 is the sharpest sentence in the treatise and it cuts towards the reader. Whatever view a man is shown, that is what he sees; it takes him in, protects him from the others, and he arrives at it. Nikhilananda's note is blunt about the cost: a man who takes his own idea for the reality and calls the rest false has not arrived at the highest truth, and this, he says, is the mistake the mystics generally make.

एतैरेषोऽपृथग्भावैः पृथगेवेति लक्षितः ।
एवं यो वेद तत्त्वेन कल्पयेत्सोऽविशङ्कितः ॥ ३० ॥
etaireṣo'pṛthagbhāvaiḥ pṛthageveti lakṣitaḥ
evaṃ yo veda tattvena kalpayetso'viśaṅkitaḥ

2.30This self is not separate from all these, and yet it is seen as separate. Whoever knows this as it is may say what he likes without hesitation.

स्वप्नमाये यथा दृष्टे गन्धर्वनगरं यथा ।
तथा विश्वमिदं दृष्टं वेदान्तेषु विचक्षणैः ॥ ३१ ॥
svapnamāye yathā dṛṣṭe gandharvanagaraṃ yathā
tathā viśvamidaṃ dṛṣṭaṃ vedānteṣu vicakṣaṇaiḥ

2.31As a dream is seen, as a conjuring is seen, as a city of the gandharvas in the sky, so is this world seen by those who are clear-sighted in the Upaniṣads.

न निरोधो न चोत्पत्तिर्न बद्धो न च साधकः ।
न मुमुक्षुर्न वै मुक्त इत्येषा परमार्थता ॥ ३२ ॥
na nirodho na cotpattirna baddho na ca sādhakaḥ
na mumukṣurna vai mukta ityeṣā paramārthatā

2.32There is no dissolving and no arising, none in bondage and none striving, none seeking release and none released. That is the highest truth.

Verse 32 is the verse the whole book exists for, and it is not softened anywhere in the commentary. Not only is there no bound soul; there is no seeker, and no one who has ever got free, because getting free would be an event and events need something that is really there to happen to. Śaṅkara's bhāṣya on this verse is the longest in the chapter and it takes the hardest form of the objection: if the teaching only negates, it establishes nothing, and its conclusion is the void. His answer works the rope again. The snake needs the rope; a mistake needs something to be mistaken; and when the mistake goes, what is left is not another mistake but the thing that was there all along. Pressed further — perhaps the rope is imagined too — he replies that the one who does the imagining must be there before the imagining, and that this is what cannot be argued away. A void has to be somebody's void.

भावैरसद्भिरेवायमद्वयेन च कल्पितः ।
भावा अप्यद्वयेनैव तस्मादद्वयता शिवा ॥ ३३ ॥
bhāvairasadbhirevāyamadvayena ca kalpitaḥ
bhāvā apyadvayenaiva tasmādadvayatā śivā

2.33This is imagined as the unreal things and as the non-duality too; and the things are imagined in the non-duality. Therefore non-duality is what is kind.

नाऽऽत्मभावेन नानेदं न स्वेनापि कथञ्चन ।
न पृथङ्नापृथक्किञ्चिदिति तत्त्वविदो विदुः ॥ ३४ ॥
nā''tmabhāvena nānedaṃ na svenāpi kathañcana
na pṛthaṅnāpṛthakkiñciditi tattvavido viduḥ

2.34This manifold is not the self, nor does it stand by itself. It is neither separate nor not separate: so those who know the truth know.

Verse 34 is where Śaṅkara says why non-duality is called kind. Misery, in his account, is what happens when a man finds one thing standing apart from another; the buffalo is not the horse, the pot is known only against the cloth, and whatever he wants is accordingly somewhere he is not. Let the many be seen as what the one is doing, and nothing stands apart, and there is nothing left for the misery to be made of. He is careful that this is not an identity of the sentient with the insentient. The light does not turn the rope into the snake; it shows that there was only ever the rope.

वीतरागभयक्रोधैर्मुनिभिर्वेदपारगैः ।
निर्विकल्पो ह्ययं दृष्टः प्रपञ्चोपशमोऽद्वयः ॥ ३५ ॥
vītarāgabhayakrodhairmunibhirvedapāragaiḥ
nirvikalpo hyayaṃ dṛṣṭaḥ prapañcopaśamo'dvayaḥ

2.35Sages free of longing, fear and anger, who have gone to the end of the Vedas, have seen it: free of imagining, the spread-out world at rest, without a second.

तस्मादेवं विदित्वैनमद्वैते योजयेत्स्मृतिम् ।
अद्वैतं समनुप्राप्य जडवल्लोकमाचरेत् ॥ ३६ ॥
tasmādevaṃ viditvainamadvaite yojayetsmṛtim
advaitaṃ samanuprāpya jaḍavallokamācaret

2.36Therefore, knowing it so, fix the attention on the non-dual. Having reached the non-dual, behave in the world like a dull thing.

निस्तुतिर्निर्नमस्कारो निःस्वधाकार एव च ।
चलाचलनिकेतश्च यतिर्यादृच्छिको भवेत् ॥ ३७ ॥
nistutirnirnamaskāro niḥsvadhākāra eva ca
calācalaniketaśca yatiryādṛcchiko bhavet

2.37Without praise, without salutation, without the rites for the dead, with the changing and the unchanging for his shelter, let the monk live on what chance brings.

तत्त्वमाध्यात्मिकं दृष्ट्वा तत्त्वं दृष्ट्वा तु बाह्यतः ।
तत्त्वीभूतस्तदारामस्तत्त्वादप्रच्युतो भवेत् ॥ ३८ ॥
tattvamādhyātmikaṃ dṛṣṭvā tattvaṃ dṛṣṭvā tu bāhyataḥ
tattvībhūtastadārāmastattvādapracyuto bhavet

2.38Having seen the truth of what is within and the truth of what is without, become that truth, delighting in it, let him not fall away from it.

Verse 33 carries a qualification that most summaries lose, and Nikhilananda's note makes it explicit: non-duality is itself a word that means something only against duality, so it too belongs to the ladder and not to what the ladder reaches. The last three verses are the only practical instruction in the treatise, and they are severe. Śaṅkara reads them as a description of the wandering renunciate: no hymns, no bowing to deities, not even the rites owed to the dead, since nothing remains that those rites could obtain; his shelter is his body, which changes, and the knowledge of the self, which does not; and he eats whatever is put in front of him. The instruction to behave like a dull thing is not a recommendation of stupidity. Śaṅkara glosses it twice: do not let others see what you have become, and, second, the man does not see others as separate from himself, and so does not take up the part of a knower. Nikhilananda adds that the verse is addressed to the student and the next verse to the one who has arrived, and that the difference between them is that the first still makes an effort.

Advaita: nothing is ever born

The third treatise is named for non-duality and states it without cover. It opens, oddly for a tradition that has produced so much devotion, by looking down on devotion.

उपासानाश्रितो धर्मो जाते ब्रह्मणि वर्तते ।
प्रागुत्पत्तेरजं सर्वं तेनासौ कृपणः स्मृतः ॥ १ ॥
upāsānāśrito dharmo jāte brahmaṇi vartate
prāgutpatterajaṃ sarvaṃ tenāsau kṛpaṇaḥ smṛtaḥ

3.1The worshipper's way stands in a Brahman that has come to be. Before it came to be, all was unborn; and so he is called a poor thing.

अतो वक्ष्याम्यकार्पण्यमजाति समतां गतम् ।
यथा न जायते किञ्चिज्जायमानं समन्ततः ॥ २ ॥
ato vakṣyāmyakārpaṇyamajāti samatāṃ gatam
yathā na jāyate kiñcijjāyamānaṃ samantataḥ

3.2Therefore I shall speak of what is not poor, of what is unborn and has come to sameness, and how nothing whatever is born, though it is born on every side.

To worship is to stand in front of something, and what stands in front of you has come to be. The worshipper's error is not that he worships but that he takes the unborn for something born. Gauḍapāda does not throw the practice away; he places it. The third treatise will come back at verse 16 to say that the stages of life are for three grades of sight and that worship was taught for the lower ones, out of compassion.

आत्मा ह्याकाशवज्जीवैर्घटाकाशैरिवोदितः ।
घटादिवच्च सङ्घातैर्जातावेतन्निदर्शनम् ॥ ३ ॥
ātmā hyākāśavajjīvairghaṭākāśairivoditaḥ
ghaṭādivacca saṅghātairjātāvetannidarśanam

3.3The self is like space, and the jīvas are like the spaces in jars. It is like the jars and their kind with respect to aggregates. This is the illustration for birth.

घटादिषु प्रलीनेषु घटाकाशादयो यथा ।
आकाशे सम्प्रलीयन्ते तद्वज्जीवा इहाऽऽत्मनि ॥ ४ ॥
ghaṭādiṣu pralīneṣu ghaṭākāśādayo yathā
ākāśe sampralīyante tadvajjīvā ihā''tmani

3.4As when jars and the rest are destroyed the jar-spaces merge in space, so do the jīvas here in the self.

यथैकस्मिन्घटाकाशे रजोधूमादिभिर्युते ।
न सर्वे सम्प्रयुज्यन्ते तद्वज्जीवाः सुखादिभिः ॥ ५ ॥
yathaikasminghaṭākāśe rajodhūmādibhiryute
na sarve samprayujyante tadvajjīvāḥ sukhādibhiḥ

3.5As when one jar-space is fouled with dust and smoke the others are not, so it is with the jīvas and their pleasures and pains.

रूपकार्यसमाख्याश्च भिद्यन्ते तत्र तत्र वै ।
आकाशस्य न भेदोऽस्ति तद्वज्जीवेषु निर्णयः ॥ ६ ॥
rūpakāryasamākhyāśca bhidyante tatra tatra vai
ākāśasya na bhedo'sti tadvajjīveṣu nirṇayaḥ

3.6Shape and use and name differ from one to the next; but space has no division, and so it is settled about the jīvas.

Śaṅkara sets the Sāṃkhya against the jars at length. Nature works for the sake of another, the Sāṃkhya says, and the other must be a person; many bodies then call for many persons, and one self spread through all of them will not do. His answer is that bondage and release never touch the person on Sāṃkhya's own account, since they belong to the intellect, and that one person is enough to be the one for whose sake anything is done. The multiplicity was carrying no weight. It was a picture the argument did not need.

नाऽऽकाशस्य घटाकाशो विकारावयवौ यथा ।
नैवाऽऽत्मनः सदा जीवो विकारावयवौ तथा ॥ ७ ॥
nā''kāśasya ghaṭākāśo vikārāvayavau yathā
naivā''tmanaḥ sadā jīvo vikārāvayavau tathā

3.7The jar-space is not a part of space nor a change in it. So the jīva is never a part of the self, nor a change in it.

यथा भवति बालानां गगनं मलिनं मलैः ।
तथा भवत्यबुद्धानामात्माऽपि मलिनो मलैः ॥ ८ ॥
yathā bhavati bālānāṃ gaganaṃ malinaṃ malaiḥ
tathā bhavatyabuddhānāmātmā'pi malino malaiḥ

3.8As the sky seems dirtied by dirt to children, so the self seems stained by stains to the unawakened.

मरणे सम्भवे चैव गत्यागमनयोरपि ।
स्थितौ सर्वशरीरेषु आकाशेनाविलक्षणः ॥ ९ ॥
maraṇe sambhave caiva gatyāgamanayorapi
sthitau sarvaśarīreṣu ākāśenāvilakṣaṇaḥ

3.9In death and in birth, in going and in coming, in standing in every body, it is not unlike space.

The illustration answers a question that any monism has to answer: if there is only one, why does your headache not reach me. The space in a jar is not a piece of space; it is space, taken with a jar. Fill one jar with smoke and the others stay clear, and what one jīva suffers stays where it is, without there being two spaces. Śaṅkara is careful about the status of the picture. Verse 9 says only that the self is not unlike space, and the point of comparison is the being-everywhere, not any substance the two share; verse 8 says that an untrained eye sees dirt in the sky. And verse 15 will say what all such pictures are for.

सङ्घाताः स्वप्नवत्सर्वे आत्ममायाविसर्जिताः ।
आधिक्ये सर्वसाम्ये वा नोपपत्तिर्हि विद्यते ॥ १० ॥
saṅghātāḥ svapnavatsarve ātmamāyāvisarjitāḥ
ādhikye sarvasāmye vā nopapattirhi vidyate

3.10The aggregates are all like a dream, sent out by the self's illusion. Whether one is greater or all are equal, neither is to be had.

रसादयो हि ये कोशा व्याख्यातास्तैत्तिरीयके ।
तेषामात्मा परो जीवः खं यथा सम्प्रकाशितः ॥ ११ ॥
rasādayo hi ye kośā vyākhyātāstaittirīyake
teṣāmātmā paro jīvaḥ khaṃ yathā samprakāśitaḥ

3.11The sheaths that begin with the one made of food, explained in the Taittirīya, have this for their self, the higher one, the jīva, as the sky has been shown.

द्वयोर्द्वयोर्मधुज्ञाने परं ब्रह्म प्रकाशितम् ।
पृथिव्यामुदरे चैव यथाऽऽकाशः प्रकाशितः ॥ १२ ॥
dvayordvayormadhujñāne paraṃ brahma prakāśitam
pṛthivyāmudare caiva yathā''kāśaḥ prakāśitaḥ

3.12In the honey teaching the highest Brahman is shown in each of the two and two; in the earth and in the body too, as space is shown in both.

जीवात्मनोरनन्यत्वमभेदेन प्रशस्यते ।
नानात्वं निन्द्यते यच्च तदेवं हि समञ्जसम् ॥ १३ ॥
jīvātmanorananyatvamabhedena praśasyate
nānātvaṃ nindyate yacca tadevaṃ hi samañjasam

3.13That the jīva and the self are not different is praised through their not being separate, and manifoldness is blamed. That is how it fits.

जीवात्मनोः पृथक्त्वं यत्प्रागुत्पत्तेः प्रकीर्तितम् ।
भविष्यद्वृत्त्या गौणं तन्मुख्यत्वं हि न युज्यते ॥ १४ ॥
jīvātmanoḥ pṛthaktvaṃ yatprāgutpatteḥ prakīrtitam
bhaviṣyadvṛttyā gauṇaṃ tanmukhyatvaṃ hi na yujyate

3.14The separateness of jīva and self that is spoken of before creation is figurative, said in view of what is to come: taken as the main sense, it does not hold.

मृल्लोहविस्फुलिङ्गाद्यै सृष्टिर्या चोदिताऽन्यथा ।
उपायः सोऽवताराय नास्ति भेदः कथञ्चन ॥ १५ ॥
mṛllohavisphuliṅgādyai sṛṣṭiryā coditā'nyathā
upāyaḥ so'vatārāya nāsti bhedaḥ kathañcana

3.15The creation set out with clay, metal and sparks, or otherwise, is a means of leading in. There is no difference at all.

आश्रमास्त्रिविधा हीनमध्यमोत्कृष्टदृष्टयः ।
उपासनोपदिष्टेयं तदर्थमनुकम्पया ॥ १६ ॥
āśramāstrividhā hīnamadhyamotkṛṣṭadṛṣṭayaḥ
upāsanopadiṣṭeyaṃ tadarthamanukampayā

3.16There are three stages of life, of low, middling and high sight. This worship has been taught for their sake, out of compassion.

Verse 15 is Gauḍapāda's account of what the Upaniṣads are doing when they tell stories of a beginning. Know one lump of clay and you know everything made of clay; know iron and you know all ironwork; sparks come from a fire and are of the nature of fire. These, he says, are a way in, not a doctrine. Śaṅkara stages the objection directly — before creation all may have been one, but after it there is plainly a world and there are plainly many people — and answers that such passages exist to establish the identity of the individual and Brahman, and for no other purpose. Verse 16 adds the reason such teaching exists at all: people see differently, and the lower teaching is given to them out of kindness, not out of confusion.

स्वसिद्धान्तव्यवस्थासु द्वैतिनो निश्चिता दृढम् ।
परस्परं विरुध्यन्ते तैरयं न विरुध्यते ॥ १७ ॥
svasiddhāntavyavasthāsu dvaitino niścitā dṛḍham
parasparaṃ virudhyante tairayaṃ na virudhyate

3.17The dualists hold hard to the settling of their own doctrines and contradict each other. With them this doctrine has no quarrel.

अद्वैतं परमार्थो हि द्वैतं तद्भेद उच्यते ।
तेषामुभयथा द्वैतं तेनायं न विरुध्यते ॥ १८ ॥
advaitaṃ paramārtho hi dvaitaṃ tadbheda ucyate
teṣāmubhayathā dvaitaṃ tenāyaṃ na virudhyate

3.18Non-duality is the highest thing; duality is said to be a division of it. For them duality goes both ways, and so this has no quarrel with them.

मायया भिद्यते ह्येतन्नान्यथाऽजं कथञ्चन ।
तत्त्वतो भिद्यमाने हि मर्त्यताममृतं व्रजेत् ॥ १९ ॥
māyayā bhidyate hyetannānyathā'jaṃ kathañcana
tattvato bhidyamāne hi martyatāmamṛtaṃ vrajet

3.19This is divided only by illusion, and by nothing else; for if it were divided in truth, the deathless would go to death.

अजातस्यैव भावस्य जातिमिच्छन्ति वादिनः ।
अजातो ह्यमृतो भावो मर्त्यतां कथमेष्यति ॥ २० ॥
ajātasyaiva bhāvasya jātimicchanti vādinaḥ
ajāto hyamṛto bhāvo martyatāṃ kathameṣyati

3.20The disputants want a birth for what is never born. How shall a thing unborn and deathless go over into death?

न भवत्यमृतं मर्त्यं न मर्त्यममृतं तथा ।
प्रकृतेरन्यथाभावो न कथञ्चिद्भविष्यति ॥ २१ ॥
na bhavatyamṛtaṃ martyaṃ na martyamamṛtaṃ tathā
prakṛteranyathābhāvo na kathañcidbhaviṣyati

3.21The deathless does not become mortal, nor the mortal deathless. A thing does not in any way become other than its nature.

स्वभावेनामृतो यस्य भावो गच्छति मर्त्यताम् ।
कृतकेनामृतस्तस्य कथं स्थास्यति निश्चलः ॥ २२ ॥
svabhāvenāmṛto yasya bhāvo gacchati martyatām
kṛtakenāmṛtastasya kathaṃ sthāsyati niścalaḥ

3.22If a thing deathless by nature goes over to death, how will it stand unmoving when its deathlessness is something made?

Verse 17 sounds like tolerance and is not. Śaṅkara names the schools in his gloss — Kapila, Kaṇāda, the Buddha, the Jina — and says their views are false for the ordinary reason that they lack support, and for an additional one: they are a standing source of attachment and hatred, since each holds its own conclusion to be the only truth and dislikes whoever disagrees. Non-duality has no quarrel with them because it includes them. The verses that follow supply the argument the whole chapter rests on and it is disarmingly simple: nothing changes into what it is not. Fire does not become cold; the deathless does not acquire death. If the deathlessness a thing has is something it was given, it can be taken away, and then it was never deathlessness.

भूततोऽभूततो वाऽपि सृज्यमाने समा श्रुतिः ।
निश्चितं युक्तियुक्तं च यत्तद्भवति नेतरत् ॥ २३ ॥
bhūtato'bhūtato vā'pi sṛjyamāne samā śrutiḥ
niścitaṃ yuktiyuktaṃ ca yattadbhavati netarat

3.23Whether what is made comes from something real or something unreal, scripture speaks alike. What is settled and joined with reason is what stands, and not the other.

नेह नानेति चाऽऽम्नायादिन्द्रो मायाभिरित्यपि ॥
अजायमानो बहुधा मायया जायते तु सः ॥ २४ ॥
neha nāneti cā''mnāyādindro māyābhirityapi
ajāyamāno bahudhā māyayā jāyate tu saḥ

3.24Scripture says there is no manifoldness here, and that Indra takes on forms by illusions; though unborn, he is born in many ways, but by illusion.

सम्भूतेरपवादाच्च सम्भवः प्रतिषिध्यते ।
को न्वेनं जनयेदिति कारणं प्रतिषिध्यते ॥ २५ ॥
sambhūterapavādācca sambhavaḥ pratiṣidhyate
ko nvenaṃ janayediti kāraṇaṃ pratiṣidhyate

3.25By the denial of coming-into-being, birth is denied; and by the question who could have begotten him, the cause is denied.

स एष नेति नेतीति व्याख्यातं निह्नुते यतः ।
सर्वमग्राह्यभावेन हेतुनाऽजं प्रकाशते ॥ २६ ॥
sa eṣa neti netīti vyākhyātaṃ nihnute yataḥ
sarvamagrāhyabhāvena hetunā'jaṃ prakāśate

3.26Because it denies, by saying not this, not this, what has been explained, being ungraspable, the unborn shines out.

Gauḍapāda's use of scripture here is a piece of exegetical policy, and verse 23 states it: the texts say both things, so the texts cannot settle it, and what settles it is which reading survives reasoning. Then he points to the passages he takes to be decisive: there is no manifoldness here; the unborn is born in many ways, by illusion. The last of the four is the neti neti of the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, and its function is what the seventh sentence of the Upaniṣad established: the unborn shows itself precisely by the failure of every attempt to take hold of it.

सतो हि मायया जन्म युज्यते न तु तत्त्वतः ।
तत्त्वतो जायते यस्य जातं तस्य हि जायते ॥ २७ ॥
sato hi māyayā janma yujyate na tu tattvataḥ
tattvato jāyate yasya jātaṃ tasya hi jāyate

3.27The birth of what is, by illusion, holds; in truth it does not. For one who says a thing is born in truth, what is born is born again.

असतो मायया जन्म तत्त्वतो नैव युज्यते ।
वन्ध्यापुत्रो न तत्त्वेन मायया वाऽपि जायते ॥ २८ ॥
asato māyayā janma tattvato naiva yujyate
vandhyāputro na tattvena māyayā vā'pi jāyate

3.28The birth of what is not, in truth, does not hold at all. The son of a barren woman is born neither in truth nor by illusion.

यथा स्वप्ने द्वयाभासं स्पन्दते मायया मनः ।
तथा जाग्रद्द्वयाभासं स्पन्दते मायया मनः ॥ २९ ॥
yathā svapne dvayābhāsaṃ spandate māyayā manaḥ
tathā jāgraddvayābhāsaṃ spandate māyayā manaḥ

3.29As in dream the mind stirs by illusion and seems to be two, so in waking the mind stirs by illusion and seems to be two.

अद्वयं च द्वयाभासं मनः स्वप्ने न संशयः ।
अद्वयं च द्वयाभासं तथा जाग्रन्न संशयः ॥ ३० ॥
advayaṃ ca dvayābhāsaṃ manaḥ svapne na saṃśayaḥ
advayaṃ ca dvayābhāsaṃ tathā jāgranna saṃśayaḥ

3.30There is no doubt that in dream the mind is without a second and appears as two; and no doubt that in waking, likewise, it is without a second and appears as two.

मनोदृश्यमिदं द्वैतं यत्किञ्चित्सचराचरम् ।
मनसो ह्यमनीभावे द्वैतं नैवोपलभ्यते ॥ ३१ ॥
manodṛśyamidaṃ dvaitaṃ yatkiñcitsacarācaram
manaso hyamanībhāve dvaitaṃ naivopalabhyate

3.31All this duality, whatever moves and does not move, is what the mind sees. When the mind is no longer a mind, no duality is found.

आत्मसत्यानुबोधेन न सङ्कल्पयते यदा ।
अमनस्तां तदा याति ग्राह्याभावे तदग्रहम् ॥ ३२ ॥
ātmasatyānubodhena na saṅkalpayate yadā
amanastāṃ tadā yāti grāhyābhāve tadagraham

3.32When, by waking to the truth of the self, it does not imagine, it goes to the state of no-mind; there being nothing to grasp, it does not grasp.

अकल्पकमजं ज्ञानं ज्ञेयाभिन्नं प्रचक्षते ।
ब्रह्मज्ञेयमजं नित्यमजेनाजं विबुध्यते ॥ ३३ ॥
akalpakamajaṃ jñānaṃ jñeyābhinnaṃ pracakṣate
brahmajñeyamajaṃ nityamajenājaṃ vibudhyate

3.33Knowledge that does not imagine and is not born is said to be no different from what is known. Brahman is what is known, unborn, and by the unborn the unborn is known.

The key term of this group is amanībhāva, the state of not being a mind. The mind is not abolished; what stops is the imagining that constitutes it as a mind, and verse 32 gives the condition: with nothing on offer to grasp, there is no grasping. Verse 33 then closes the gap that would otherwise remain between the knower and the known, and closes it in the only way non-duality permits: the knowledge is not born, the thing known is not born, and they are not two things.

निगृहीतस्य मनसो निर्विकल्पस्य धीमतः ।
प्रचारः स तु विज्ञेयः सुषुप्तेऽन्यो न तत्समः ॥ ३४ ॥
nigṛhītasya manaso nirvikalpasya dhīmataḥ
pracāraḥ sa tu vijñeyaḥ suṣupte'nyo na tatsamaḥ

3.34The going of the mind held in and free of imagining, in a man of understanding, is to be known: it is not the same as the other one, in deep sleep.

लीयते हि सुषुप्ते तन्निगृहीतं न लीयते ।
तदेव निर्भयं ब्रह्म ज्ञानालोकं समन्ततः ॥ ३५ ॥
līyate hi suṣupte tannigṛhītaṃ na līyate
tadeva nirbhayaṃ brahma jñānālokaṃ samantataḥ

3.35The mind merges in deep sleep; held in, it does not merge. That is Brahman itself, fearless, the light of knowledge on every side.

अजमनिद्रमस्वप्नमनामकमरूपकम् ।
सकृद्विभातं सर्वज्ञं नोपचारः कथञ्चन ॥ ३६ ॥
ajamanidramasvapnamanāmakamarūpakam
sakṛdvibhātaṃ sarvajñaṃ nopacāraḥ kathañcana

3.36Unborn, sleepless, dreamless, without a name and without a form, shining once for all, knowing all: for it there is no manner of speaking.

सर्वाभिलापविगतः सर्वचिन्तासमुत्थितः ।
सुप्रशान्तः सकृज्ज्योतिः समाधिरचलोऽभयः ॥ ३७ ॥
sarvābhilāpavigataḥ sarvacintāsamutthitaḥ
supraśāntaḥ sakṛjjyotiḥ samādhiracalo'bhayaḥ

3.37Gone beyond all saying, risen above all thinking, altogether at peace, light once for all, still, unshaken, without fear.

ग्रहो न तत्र नोत्सर्ग्रश्चिन्ता यत्र न विद्यते ।
आत्मसंस्थं तदा ज्ञानमजाति समतां गतम् ॥ ३८ ॥
graho na tatra notsargraścintā yatra na vidyate
ātmasaṃsthaṃ tadā jñānamajāti samatāṃ gatam

3.38There is no taking there and no letting go, where there is no thinking at all. Then knowledge stands in the self, unborn, gone to sameness.

अस्पर्शयोगो वै नाम दुर्दर्शः सर्वयोगिभिः ।
योगिनो बिभ्यति ह्यस्मादभये भयदर्शिनः ॥ ३९ ॥
asparśayogo vai nāma durdarśaḥ sarvayogibhiḥ
yogino bibhyati hyasmādabhaye bhayadarśinaḥ

3.39The yoga that touches nothing is hard for all yogins to see. Yogins are afraid of it, for they see fear where there is no fear.

Verses 34 and 35 draw the line that the rest of the book will not let anyone cross. A mind that has stopped is not a mind that has gone to sleep. In deep sleep the mind merges; here it is held and does not merge; the two look alike from outside and are opposites. Verse 39 gives the discipline its name. Asparśa yoga is a contradiction on its face, since yoga means joining and asparśa means not touching anything, and Śaṅkara's gloss makes the paradox the point: this yoga is without relation because there is nothing for it to be in relation with. His account of why yogins fear it is one of the sharpest sentences in the commentary. They are afraid, he says, that it means the annihilation of their self, which is exactly what they should expect if what they take themselves to be is the individual they have been training.

मनसो निग्रहायत्तमभयं सर्वयोगिनाम् ।
दुःखक्षयः प्रबोधश्चाप्यक्षया शान्तिरेव च ॥ ४० ॥
manaso nigrahāyattamabhayaṃ sarvayoginām
duḥkhakṣayaḥ prabodhaścāpyakṣayā śāntireva ca

3.40The yogins depend on the holding-in of the mind for fearlessness, for the ending of sorrow, for waking, and for a peace that does not wear out.

उत्सेक उदधेर्यद्वत्कुशाग्रेणैकबिन्दुना ।
मनसो निग्रहस्तद्वद्भवेदपरिखेदतः ॥ ४१ ॥
utseka udadheryadvatkuśāgreṇaikabindunā
manaso nigrahastadvadbhavedaparikhedataḥ

3.41As one might empty the ocean, drop by drop, with the tip of a blade of grass, so is the holding-in of the mind: only by never tiring.

उपायेन निगृह्णीयाद्विक्षिप्तं कामभोगयोः ।
सुप्रसन्नं लये चैव यथा कामो लयस्तथा ॥ ४२ ॥
upāyena nigṛhṇīyādvikṣiptaṃ kāmabhogayoḥ
suprasannaṃ laye caiva yathā kāmo layastathā

3.42Hold in, by a device, the mind scattered among desires and enjoyments, and the mind well pleased in torpor; for torpor is as much a trouble as desire.

दुःखं सर्वमनुस्मृत्य कामभोगान्निवर्तयेत् ।
अजं सर्वमनुस्मृत्य जातं नैव तु पश्यति ॥ ४३ ॥
duḥkhaṃ sarvamanusmṛtya kāmabhogānnivartayet
ajaṃ sarvamanusmṛtya jātaṃ naiva tu paśyati

3.43Turning away from enjoyments, remembering that all of it is sorrow; remembering that all is unborn, he does not see anything born.

Śaṅkara's account of how the mind is turned back is short, and it is not a technique. Remember what the duality costs: everything in it changes, everything that changes ends, and a mind that has taken that in stops asking for the enjoyments. Then remember what the teachers and the texts say is there instead, that it is unborn, and the born will not be seen. Nikhilananda's note counts four obstacles rather than two — torpor, distraction, the pleasure of a small success, and attachment to whatever stage has been reached — and the last two are the ones a practitioner is least likely to report.

लये सम्बोधयेच्चित्तं विक्षिप्तं शमयेत्पुनः ।
सकषायं विजानीयात्समप्राप्तं न चालयेत् ॥ ४४ ॥
laye sambodhayeccittaṃ vikṣiptaṃ śamayetpunaḥ
sakaṣāyaṃ vijānīyātsamaprāptaṃ na cālayet

3.44In torpor, wake the mind; scattered, quieten it again. Know it when it is stained, and when it has reached evenness, do not stir it.

नाऽऽस्वादयेत्सुखं तत्र निःसङ्गः प्रज्ञया भवेत् ।
निश्चलं निश्चरच्चित्तमेकी कुर्यात्प्रयत्नतः ॥ ४५ ॥
nā''svādayetsukhaṃ tatra niḥsaṅgaḥ prajñayā bhavet
niścalaṃ niścaraccittamekī kuryātprayatnataḥ

3.45Let him not taste the pleasure there; let him be unattached, by discernment. When the mind, grown still, moves out again, let him make it one with effort.

यदा न लीयते चित्तं न च विक्षिप्यते पुनः ।
अनिङ्गनमनाभासं निष्पन्नं ब्रह्म तत्तदा ॥ ४६ ॥
yadā na līyate cittaṃ na ca vikṣipyate punaḥ
aniṅganamanābhāsaṃ niṣpannaṃ brahma tattadā

3.46When the mind neither merges nor is scattered again, unmoving and showing no appearance, then it is Brahman, and the work is done.

स्वस्थं शान्तं सनिर्वाणमकथ्यं सुखमुत्तमम् ।
अजमजेन ज्ञेयेन सर्वज्ञं परिचक्षत ॥ ४७ ॥
svasthaṃ śāntaṃ sanirvāṇamakathyaṃ sukhamuttamam
ajamajena jñeyena sarvajñaṃ paricakṣata

3.47Standing in itself, at peace, with the fire put out, not to be spoken, the highest happiness: unborn, with the unborn as what it knows, knowing everything, they say.

न कश्चिज्जायते जीवः सम्भवोऽस्य न विद्यते ।
एतत्तदुत्तमं सत्यं यत्र किञ्चिन्न जायते ॥ ४८ ॥
na kaścijjāyate jīvaḥ sambhavo'sya na vidyate
etattaduttamaṃ satyaṃ yatra kiñcinna jāyate

3.48No jīva is ever born; there is no coming to be for it. This is the highest truth: that nothing whatever is born.

These nine verses are the most practical passage in the book and the least like what a reader expects from it. Verse 41 concedes the difficulty without flinching: bringing the mind under control is emptying an ocean with the tip of a blade of grass, drop by drop. Verse 42 sets the trap the tradition knew best, that a mind well pleased with its own torpor is in as much trouble as a mind chasing what it wants, and the two are named in the same breath. Verse 44 lists four positions and what to do in each, and the third is the one the practice manuals tend to miss: a mind neither asleep nor scattered but stained, still holding attachments it has not noticed, and looking from the outside exactly like a mind at rest. Verse 45 removes the last support: do not taste the happiness that comes with the stillness, since the seeking of pleasure in absorption is the mark of an enquiry that has given up. Śaṅkara's comment on 44 is explicit that the state to be woken from is the yogic trance, and Nikhilananda's note draws the conclusion the tradition has argued about ever since: what is aimed at is not truth seen with the eyes shut, but truth seen with the eyes open on every object.

Verse 46 states the finish, and it is a double negation with nothing added: neither merged nor scattered, unmoving, and with no appearance in it. Verse 48 then ends the chapter on the line the whole book exists to make available: no one is ever born, and nothing whatever comes to be.

Alātaśānti: the quenching of the firebrand

The fourth treatise is as long as the other three together and it is the one that has caused all the trouble. Śaṅkara says what it is for at the opening: the first three chapters have established non-duality by scripture, by the unreality of objects and by reason, and the fourth examines the rival systems and shows that they cannot be true philosophy, because they contradict each other and because each is held with attachment to its own side. Its method is refutation. Its material is every available theory of causation. And its vocabulary, for two hundred lines at a stretch, is the vocabulary of the Buddhist schools it is refuting.

ज्ञानेनाऽऽकाशकल्पेन धर्मान्यो गगनोपमान् ।
ज्ञेयाभिन्नेन सम्बुद्धस्तं वन्दे द्विपदां वरम् ॥ १ ॥
jñānenā''kāśakalpena dharmānyo gaganopamān
jñeyābhinnena sambuddhastaṃ vande dvipadāṃ varam

4.1To him who woke to the things that are like the sky, by a knowledge like space and not different from what it knows: to that best of two-footed beings I bow.

अस्पर्शयोगो वै नाम सर्वसत्त्वसुखो हितः ।
अविवादोऽविरुद्धश्च देशितस्तं नमाम्यहम् ॥ २ ॥
asparśayogo vai nāma sarvasattvasukho hitaḥ
avivādo'viruddhaśca deśitastaṃ namāmyaham

4.2To the yoga that touches nothing, good for all creatures, their happiness and their welfare, without dispute and without contradiction, as it was taught: to that I bow.

Whom does the first verse salute? Dvipadāṃ varam, best of bipeds, and knowledge compared to space, and dharmas for what is known: the phrasing is that of a Buddhist hymn, and the question has been live for a thousand years. Śaṅkara takes the verse to salute Nārāyaṇa, the promulgator of the doctrine, and Ānandagiri's subcommentary supplies the story that Gauḍapāda received the teaching at Badarī. Nikhilananda, who reproduces Das Gupta's case for the Buddha at length, answers it with the text's own last verse but one. Either way, what is saluted in the second verse is a discipline and not a person, and it is the asparśa yoga of the third treatise, now given its own bow.

भूतस्य जातिमिच्छन्ति वादिनः केचिदेव हि ।
अभूतस्यापरे धीरा विवदन्तः परस्परम् ॥ ३ ॥
bhūtasya jātimicchanti vādinaḥ kecideva hi
abhūtasyāpare dhīrā vivadantaḥ parasparam

4.3Some disputants want a birth for what is; others, steady men, want one for what is not; and they quarrel with each other.

भूतं न जायते किञ्चिदभूतं नैव जायते ।
विवदन्तो द्वया ह्येवमजातिं ख्यापयन्ति ते ॥ ४ ॥
bhūtaṃ na jāyate kiñcidabhūtaṃ naiva jāyate
vivadanto dvayā hyevamajātiṃ khyāpayanti te

4.4What is is not born, and what is not is not born at all. Quarrelling in this way, the two of them proclaim non-origination.

ख्याप्यमानामजातिं तैरनुमोदामहे वयम् ।
विवदामो न तैः सार्धमविवादं निबोधत ॥ ५ ॥
khyāpyamānāmajātiṃ tairanumodāmahe vayam
vivadāmo na taiḥ sārdhamavivādaṃ nibodhata

4.5We approve the non-origination they proclaim. We do not quarrel with them. Hear from us what has no quarrel in it.

This is the move of the chapter in miniature. The Sāṃkhya holds that an effect is already present in its cause; the Vaiśeṣika holds that it is a new thing. Each has an unanswerable objection to the other: nothing that already exists needs to be born, and nothing that does not exist can produce anything. Gauḍapāda accepts both objections and neither position. Between them the disputants have proved his conclusion, and he says so with what looks like courtesy and is in fact the whole argument: we agree with what you have shown, and we take no side in your quarrel.

अजातस्यैव धर्मस्य जातिमिच्छन्ति वादिनः ।
अजातो ह्यमृतो धर्मो मर्त्यतां कथमेष्यति ॥ ६ ॥
ajātasyaiva dharmasya jātimicchanti vādinaḥ
ajāto hyamṛto dharmo martyatāṃ kathameṣyati

4.6The disputants want a birth for a thing that is never born. How will a thing unborn and deathless go over into death?

न भवत्यमृतं मर्त्यं न मर्त्यममृतं तथा ।
प्रकृतेरन्यथाभावो न कथञ्चिद्भविष्यति ॥ ७ ॥
na bhavatyamṛtaṃ martyaṃ na martyamamṛtaṃ tathā
prakṛteranyathābhāvo na kathañcidbhaviṣyati

4.7The deathless does not become mortal, nor the mortal deathless. A thing will not in any way become other than its nature.

जरामरणनिर्मुक्ताः सर्वे धर्माः स्वभावतः ।
जरामरणमिच्छन्तश्च्यवन्ते तन्मनीषया ॥ १० ॥
jarāmaraṇanirmuktāḥ sarve dharmāḥ svabhāvataḥ
jarāmaraṇamicchantaścyavante tanmanīṣayā

4.10All things are by their nature free of age and death. Wanting age and death, they fall away from that by the very thought.

Before the refutations begin, Gauḍapāda settles what a nature is, and Śaṅkara gives it four marks. A nature is what a thing has by full attainment, as the powers a yogin has trained for do not desert him afterwards; it is the character that does not vary with time or place, as heat does not leave fire; it belongs to a thing from its birth, as flight belongs to a bird; and it needs nothing outside the thing, as water runs downhill without being sent. Whatever can be taken away was never one. If the made-up things of ordinary experience hold their natures as firmly as that, he asks, what is to be said of what was never born.

कारणं यस्य वै कार्यं कारणं तस्य जायते ।
जायमानं कथमजं भिन्नं नित्यं कथं च तत् ॥ ११ ॥
kāraṇaṃ yasya vai kāryaṃ kāraṇaṃ tasya jāyate
jāyamānaṃ kathamajaṃ bhinnaṃ nityaṃ kathaṃ ca tat

4.11If the cause of a thing is born as that thing's effect, how is the cause unborn? And how is it fixed and apart, if it is being born?

कारणाद्यद्यनन्यत्वमतः कार्यमजं यदि ।
जायमानाद्धि वै कार्यात्कारणं ते कथं ध्रुवम् ॥ १२ ॥
kāraṇādyadyananyatvamataḥ kāryamajaṃ yadi
jāyamānāddhi vai kāryātkāraṇaṃ te kathaṃ dhruvam

4.12If the effect is no different from the cause, then the effect too is unborn. And how is the cause fixed, if it is no different from an effect that is being born?

हेतोरादिः फलं येषामादिर्हेतुः फलस्य च ।
हेतोः फलस्य चानादिः कथं तैरूपवर्ण्यते ॥ १४ ॥
hetorādiḥ phalaṃ yeṣāmādirhetuḥ phalasya ca
hetoḥ phalasya cānādiḥ kathaṃ tairūpavarṇyate

4.14Those for whom the effect is the beginning of the cause, and the cause the beginning of the effect — how do they call either of them beginningless?

सम्भवे हेतुफलयोरेषितव्यः क्रमस्त्वया ।
युगपत्सम्भवे यस्मादसम्बन्धो विषाणवत् ॥ १६ ॥
sambhave hetuphalayoreṣitavyaḥ kramastvayā
yugapatsambhave yasmādasambandho viṣāṇavat

4.16If cause and effect do come to be, you must find an order for them. If they come at once, there is no relation between them, as between two horns.

यदि हेतोः फलात्सिद्धिः फलसिद्धिश्च हेतुतः ।
कतरत्पूर्वनिष्पन्नं यस्य सिद्धिरपेक्षया ॥ १८ ॥
yadi hetoḥ phalātsiddhiḥ phalasiddhiśca hetutaḥ
kataratpūrvaniṣpannaṃ yasya siddhirapekṣayā

4.18If the cause is settled by the effect and the effect by the cause, which of the two is finished first, that the other should depend on it?

बीजाङ्कुराख्यो दृष्टान्तः सदा साध्यसमो हि सः ।
न हि साध्यसमो हेतुः सिद्धौ साध्यस्य युज्यते ॥ २० ॥
bījāṅkurākhyo dṛṣṭāntaḥ sadā sādhyasamo hi saḥ
na hi sādhyasamo hetuḥ siddhau sādhyasya yujyate

4.20The illustration of the seed and the sprout is always the very thing to be proved. What has itself to be proved does not serve to prove anything.

स्वतो वा परतो वाऽपि न किञ्चिद्वस्तु जायते ।
सदसत्सदसद्वाऽपि न किञ्चिद्वस्तु जायते ॥ २२ ॥
svato vā parato vā'pi na kiñcidvastu jāyate
sadasatsadasadvā'pi na kiñcidvastu jāyate

4.22Nothing whatever is born of itself or of another. Nothing whatever is born, whether it is, or is not, or both.

The argument has three prongs and they close on each other. If cause and effect are the same, the cause is born and so is not a cause; if they are different, nothing connects them; if they make each other, the order cannot be stated, and Gauḍapāda's phrase for that position is a father born from his son. The seed and the sprout, the standard illustration of a beginningless chain, is dismissed on a point of method that any logician would grant: the example offered in proof is itself the disputed case. Śaṅkara works the exchange out over several pages, refusing at each stage the retreat to a series: there is no such thing as the series apart from the seeds and the sprouts, and every seed in it was produced and so had a beginning. And verse 22 closes the last door. Nothing comes from itself, from another, from both; nothing that exists is born, nothing that does not exist is born, nothing that both is and is not is born, because those two marks do not sit together.

प्रज्ञप्तेः सनिमित्तत्वमन्यथा द्वयनाशतः ।
सङ्क्लेशस्योपलब्धेश्च परतन्त्रास्तिता मता ॥ २४ ॥
prajñapteḥ sanimittatvamanyathā dvayanāśataḥ
saṅkleśasyopalabdheśca paratantrāstitā matā

4.24Knowing must have a cause outside it; otherwise both would be lost. And because suffering is met with, they hold that something stands on its own.

प्रज्ञप्तेः सनिमित्तत्वमिष्यते युक्तिदर्शनात् ।
निमित्तस्यानिमित्तत्वमिष्यते भूतदर्शनात् ॥ २५ ॥
prajñapteḥ sanimittatvamiṣyate yuktidarśanāt
nimittasyānimittatvamiṣyate bhūtadarśanāt

4.25That knowing has a cause is allowed, from the showing of reason. That the cause is no cause is allowed, from the showing of what is.

चित्तं न संस्पृशत्यर्थं नार्थाभासं तथैव च ।
अभूतो हि यतश्चार्थो नार्थाभासस्ततः पृथक् ॥ २६ ॥
cittaṃ na saṃspṛśatyarthaṃ nārthābhāsaṃ tathaiva ca
abhūto hi yataścārtho nārthābhāsastataḥ pṛthak

4.26The mind does not touch a thing, nor the appearance of a thing. For the thing is not; and the appearance is not apart from the mind.

Śaṅkara's image for the realist's case is a clear crystal. Knowing by itself has one character, which is that it lights things up, and there is no variety inside it; set something coloured beside the crystal and the colour is in the crystal, and that, the realist says, is what an object does for a knowing. Blue and yellow and the sound of a bell are various, and nothing inside the knowing accounts for it. The reply in verse 25 does not deny the demand. It grants the demand as reason makes it, and then asks what the cause turns out to be when it is looked for in the thing.

निमित्तं न सदा चित्तं संस्पृशत्यध्वसु त्रिषु ।
अनिमित्तो विपर्यासः कथं तस्य भविष्यति ॥ २७ ॥
nimittaṃ na sadā cittaṃ saṃspṛśatyadhvasu triṣu
animitto viparyāsaḥ kathaṃ tasya bhaviṣyati

4.27The mind never touches a cause, in any of the three times. Having no cause, how shall it fall into error?

तस्मान्न जायते चित्तं चित्तदृश्यं न जायते ।
तस्य पश्यन्ति ये जातिं खे वै पश्यन्ति ते पदम् ॥ २८ ॥
tasmānna jāyate cittaṃ cittadṛśyaṃ na jāyate
tasya paśyanti ye jātiṃ khe vai paśyanti te padam

4.28So the mind is not born, and what the mind sees is not born. Those who see a birth for it are seeing the tracks of birds in the sky.

Verse 24 is the objector's, and Śaṅkara marks it as such; the hardest thing in it is the burn, which hurts as no idea does. The answer in verse 25 and after is the idealist's, and Śaṅkara says so plainly: these verses give the position of the Vijñānavādins, and Gauḍapāda approves their arguments. No final object is ever reached — the jar resolves into clay, the cloth into thread, the thread into fibre, and the regress runs out of language before it runs out of objects. Then verse 28 turns the same blade. If nothing that the mind sees is ever born, the mind is not born either, and those who make the mind momentary and real are looking for the footprints of birds in the sky. Śaṅkara ranks the schools for audacity as he goes: the idealists outdo the dualists, and the nihilists outdo the idealists, being men who would compress the sky into their palms. His standing objection to the void is one sentence long and does not change from chapter to chapter: if all this is a void, somebody is left to say so.

आदावन्ते च यन्नास्ति वर्तमानेऽपि तत्तथा ।
वितथैः सदृशाः सन्तोऽवितथा इव लक्षिताः ॥ ३१ ॥
ādāvante ca yannāsti vartamāne'pi tattathā
vitathaiḥ sadṛśāḥ santo'vitathā iva lakṣitāḥ

4.31What is not there at the beginning and not at the end is not there in the middle. Things are like what is false, and are taken as though they were not.

सर्वे धर्मा मृषा स्वप्ने कायस्यान्तर्निदर्शनात् ।
संवृतेऽस्मिन्प्रदेशे वै भूतानां दर्शनं कुतः ॥ ३३ ॥
sarve dharmā mṛṣā svapne kāyasyāntarnidarśanāt
saṃvṛte'sminpradeśe vai bhūtānāṃ darśanaṃ kutaḥ

4.33In dream all things are false, being seen inside the body. In a place so shut in, how should there be a sight of beings?

उपलम्भात्समाचारादस्तिवस्तुत्ववादिनाम् ।
जातिस्तु देशिता बुद्धैः अजातेस्त्रसतां सदा ॥ ४२ ॥
upalambhātsamācārādastivastutvavādinām
jātistu deśitā buddhaiḥ ajātestrasatāṃ sadā

4.42Because things are met with and dealt with, a birth has been taught by the wise for those who always fear non-origination.

उपलम्भात्समाचारान्मायाहस्ती यथोच्यते ।
उपलम्भात्समाचारादस्ति वस्तु तथोच्यते ॥ ४४ ॥
upalambhātsamācārānmāyāhastī yathocyate
upalambhātsamācārādasti vastu tathocyate

4.44As an elephant made by magic is called an elephant because it is met with and dealt with, so a thing is called a thing.

Verse 42 is the most generous sentence in the book and the most double-edged. Causation is taught, Gauḍapāda says, and taught on purpose, to those who cannot do without it: people who are sure that things exist because they can see them and deal with them, and who take the duties of their station seriously, and who are frightened by the claim that nothing is born, hearing in it the annihilation of themselves. Let them hold on to it, says Śaṅkara; the harm is small. Verse 44 then removes the ground under the criterion that made the concession necessary. The magician's elephant is perceived and can be tied up and ridden, and there is no elephant.

जात्याभासं चलाभासं वस्त्वाभासं तथैव च ।
अजाचलमवस्तुत्वं विज्ञानं शान्तमद्वयम् ॥ ४५ ॥
jātyābhāsaṃ calābhāsaṃ vastvābhāsaṃ tathaiva ca
ajācalamavastutvaṃ vijñānaṃ śāntamadvayam

4.45What appears as birth, as motion, as a thing, is knowing itself: unborn, unmoving, no thing at all, at peace, without a second.

ऋजुवक्रादिकाभासमलातस्पन्दितं यथा ।
ग्रहणग्राहकाभासं विज्ञानस्पन्दितं तथा ॥ ४७ ॥
ṛjuvakrādikābhāsamalātaspanditaṃ yathā
grahaṇagrāhakābhāsaṃ vijñānaspanditaṃ tathā

4.47As a firebrand in motion appears straight or bent, so knowing in motion appears as the grasper and the grasped.

अस्पन्दमानमलातमनाभासमजं यथा ।
अस्पन्दमानं विज्ञानमनाभासमजं तथा ॥ ४८ ॥
aspandamānamalātamanābhāsamajaṃ yathā
aspandamānaṃ vijñānamanābhāsamajaṃ tathā

4.48As a firebrand not in motion is without appearances and unborn, so knowing not in motion is without appearances and unborn.

Śaṅkara's gloss on verse 45 fixes what the firebrand is an image of. We say that Devadatta is born, that Devadatta is walking, that Devadatta is tall and fair; the name takes the verbs and the adjectives, and nothing else in the sentence is doing any work. Consciousness stands in that relation to birth, to motion and to being a thing: it appears under all three and is none of them. That is why the next verse can say without qualification that the mind is never born, and that whoever has seen this does not fall into the mistake again.

अलाते स्पन्दमाने वै नाऽऽभासा अन्यतोभुवः ।
न ततोऽन्यत्र निस्पन्दान्नालातं प्रविशन्ति ते ॥ ४९ ॥
alāte spandamāne vai nā''bhāsā anyatobhuvaḥ
na tato'nyatra nispandānnālātaṃ praviśanti te

4.49When the firebrand moves, the appearances do not come from anywhere else. When it is still, they do not go elsewhere, and they do not enter the firebrand.

न निर्गता अलातात्ते द्रव्यत्वाभावयोगतः ।
विज्ञानेऽपि तथैव स्युराभासस्याविशेशतः ॥ ५० ॥
na nirgatā alātātte dravyatvābhāvayogataḥ
vijñāne'pi tathaiva syurābhāsasyāviśeśataḥ

4.50They did not come out of the firebrand, having nothing of substance in them. In knowing it is exactly the same, the appearance being no different.

विज्ञाने स्पन्दमाने वै नाऽऽभासा अन्यतोभुवः ।
न ततोऽन्यत्र निस्पन्दान्न विज्ञानं विशन्ति ते ॥ ५१ ॥
vijñāne spandamāne vai nā''bhāsā anyatobhuvaḥ
na tato'nyatra nispandānna vijñānaṃ viśanti te

4.51When knowing moves, the appearances do not come from anywhere else. When it is still, they do not go elsewhere, and they do not enter it.

न निर्गतास्ते विज्ञानाद्द्रव्यत्वाभावयोगतः ।
कार्यकारणताभावाद्यतोऽचिन्त्याः सदैव ते ॥ ५२ ॥
na nirgatāste vijñānāddravyatvābhāvayogataḥ
kāryakāraṇatābhāvādyato'cintyāḥ sadaiva te

4.52They did not come out of knowing, having nothing of substance in them. There is no cause and effect between them, and so they are for ever beyond thought.

A torch swung in the dark makes a wheel of fire. There is no wheel; there is a point of light and a movement. Gauḍapāda gets four denials out of it in three verses, and they are the four that a theory of causation would have to answer: the figures do not arrive from outside, they do not depart when the swinging stops, they do not sink back into the brand, and they never came out of it in the first place, because a shape is not the kind of thing that can be somewhere. Śaṅkara's note is exact about what the figure is: what actually exists is a point, and the mind, out of ignorance, sees forms in it. Then verses 51 and 52 substitute consciousness for the brand, word for word, and the list of denials survives the substitution intact. Śaṅkara marks the one place where the analogy breaks, and it breaks in the direction that strengthens the claim: the firebrand really does move, and consciousness does not. The motion is the mistake.

यावद्धेतुफलावेशस्तावद्धेतुफलोद्भवः ।
क्षीणे हेतुफलावेशे नास्ति हेतुफलोद्भवः ॥ ५५ ॥
yāvaddhetuphalāveśastāvaddhetuphalodbhavaḥ
kṣīṇe hetuphalāveśe nāsti hetuphalodbhavaḥ

4.55As long as there is holding to cause and effect, cause and effect keep arising. When that holding wears away, there is no arising of cause and effect.

धर्मा य इति जायन्ते जायन्ते ते न तत्त्वतः ।
जन्म मायोपमं तेषां सा च माया न विद्यते ॥ ५८ ॥
dharmā ya iti jāyante jāyante te na tattvataḥ
janma māyopamaṃ teṣāṃ sā ca māyā na vidyate

4.58The things that are said to be born are not born in truth. Their birth is like a thing conjured, and the conjuring is not there either.

न कश्चिज्जायते जीवः सम्भवोऽस्य न विद्यते ।
एतत्तदुत्तमं सत्यं यत्र किञ्चिन्न जायते ॥ ७१ ॥
na kaścijjāyate jīvaḥ sambhavo'sya na vidyate
etattaduttamaṃ satyaṃ yatra kiñcinna jāyate

4.71No jīva is ever born; there is no coming to be for it. This is the highest truth: that nothing whatever is born.

चित्तस्पन्दितमेवेदं ग्राह्यग्राहकवद्द्वयम् ।
चित्तं निर्विषयं नित्यमसङ्गं तेन कीर्तितम् ॥ ७२ ॥
cittaspanditamevedaṃ grāhyagrāhakavaddvayam
cittaṃ nirviṣayaṃ nityamasaṅgaṃ tena kīrtitam

4.72This pair, the grasped and the grasper, is nothing but the stirring of mind. Mind has no object, and so it is called for ever unattached.

Verse 55 is where the metaphysics turns into a description of a life, and Śaṅkara puts the content of the holding into the first person: I am the doer, these good and bad deeds are mine, I shall enjoy their fruits, born in due course as some being or other. That is what causation means to the person who believes it, and that, he says, is what continues without a break. The end of it is not a better position within the chain but the wearing away of the attachment to the chain, which he compares to being freed of a possessing spirit. Verse 58 anticipates the obvious counter: if birth is an illusion, is the illusion real? No. The word names something that is not there, and the thing conjured has no seed except another conjuring.

अजः कल्पितसंवृत्या परमार्थेन नाप्यजः ।
परतन्त्राभिनिष्पत्त्या संवृत्या जायते तु सः ॥ ७४ ॥
ajaḥ kalpitasaṃvṛtyā paramārthena nāpyajaḥ
paratantrābhiniṣpattyā saṃvṛtyā jāyate tu saḥ

4.74By an imagined convention it is unborn; in the highest sense not even unborn. By the convention that turns on another's doctrine, it is born.

अस्ति नास्त्यस्ति नास्तीति नास्ति नास्तीति वा पुनः ।
चलस्थिरोभयाभावैरावृणोत्येव बालिशः ॥ ८३ ॥
asti nāstyasti nāstīti nāsti nāstīti vā punaḥ
calasthirobhayābhāvairāvṛṇotyeva bāliśaḥ

4.83It is; it is not; it is and is not; it neither is nor is not: with the moving and the still, with both and with neither, the childish keep it covered.

कोट्यश्चतस्र एतास्तु ग्रहैर्यासां सदाऽऽवृतः ।
भगवानाभिरस्पृष्टो येन दृष्टः स सर्वदृक् ॥ ८४ ॥
koṭyaścatasra etāstu grahairyāsāṃ sadā''vṛtaḥ
bhagavānābhiraspṛṣṭo yena dṛṣṭaḥ sa sarvadṛk

4.84These are the four corners, and one who holds them stays covered by them. He by whom it is seen untouched by all of them sees everything.

अवस्त्वनुपलम्भं च लोकोत्तरमिति स्मृतम् ।
ज्ञानं ज्ञेयं च विज्ञेयं सदा बुद्धैः प्रकीर्तितम् ॥ ८८ ॥
avastvanupalambhaṃ ca lokottaramiti smṛtam
jñānaṃ jñeyaṃ ca vijñeyaṃ sadā buddhaiḥ prakīrtitam

4.88What is no thing and is not met with is held to be beyond the world. Knowledge, the knowable and what is finally to be known have always been declared by the awakened.

Verse 74 is the point at which the argument turns on itself, and it is the reason the book cannot be accused of substituting one doctrine for another. Asked whether the unbornness of the self is a truth, Gauḍapāda answers that it is a truth in the relative way, said against those who assert a birth, and that in the highest sense the self is not even unborn, since unborn is the correlative of born and both belong to the same vocabulary. Śaṅkara accepts this without softening it. Verse 83's four corners then dispose of the four positions available to metaphysics — it exists, it does not, both, neither — and his gloss assigns each to a school: the Vaiśeṣika, the momentary idealists among the Buddhists, the Jaina, and the nihilist. What holds each of them in place is not the position but the attachment to it, and the word Gauḍapāda uses for all four of them is the word for a child.

प्रकृत्याऽऽकाशवज्ज्ञेयाः सर्वे धर्मा अनादयः ।
विद्यते न हि नानात्वं तेषां क्वचन किञ्चन ॥ ९१ ॥
prakṛtyā''kāśavajjñeyāḥ sarve dharmā anādayaḥ
vidyate na hi nānātvaṃ teṣāṃ kvacana kiñcana

4.91All things are to be known as beginningless and by nature like space. There is not the least manifoldness in them anywhere at all.

आदिशान्ता ह्यनुत्पन्नाः प्रकृत्यैव सुनिर्वृताः ।
सर्वे धर्माः समाभिन्ना अजं साम्यं विशारदम् ॥ ९३ ॥
ādiśāntā hyanutpannāḥ prakṛtyaiva sunirvṛtāḥ
sarve dharmāḥ samābhinnā ajaṃ sāmyaṃ viśāradam

4.93All things are at peace from the first, unarisen, by their nature quite free, alike and undivided, unborn, the same, and clear.

अलब्धावरणाः सर्वे धर्माः प्रकृतिनिर्मलाः ।
आदौ बुद्धास्तथा मुक्ता बुध्यन्त इति नायकाः ॥ ९८ ॥
alabdhāvaraṇāḥ sarve dharmāḥ prakṛtinirmalāḥ
ādau buddhāstathā muktā budhyanta iti nāyakāḥ

4.98All things are free of covering, clean by nature, awake from the beginning and released. So the leaders say that they awaken.

क्रमते न हि बुद्धस्य ज्ञानं धर्मेषु तायिनः ।
सर्वे धर्मास्तथा ज्ञानं नैतद्बुद्धेन भाषितम् ॥ ९९ ॥
kramate na hi buddhasya jñānaṃ dharmeṣu tāyinaḥ
sarve dharmāstathā jñānaṃ naitadbuddhena bhāṣitam

4.99The knowledge of the awakened one, the protector, does not pass into things. All things are so, and so is knowledge. This was not spoken by the Buddha.

दुर्दर्शमतिगम्भीरमजं साम्यं विशारदम् ।
बुद्ध्वा पदमनानात्वं नमस्कुर्मो यथाबलम् ॥ १०० ॥
durdarśamatigambhīramajaṃ sāmyaṃ viśāradam
buddhvā padamanānātvaṃ namaskurmo yathābalam

4.100Having awoken to the state that is hard to see, very deep, unborn, the same everywhere, clear and without manifoldness, we salute it as best we can.

Verse 98 draws the last consequence of ajāti and it is the one that removes the work of the spiritual life: there is no veil, there never was, and what the teachers call awakening is a way of speaking, as one speaks of the sun shining when shining is all the sun is, or of a mountain standing when it has never done anything else. Then verse 99 says outright that this is not what the Buddha said. Śaṅkara's gloss is the most interesting sentence in his commentary on the chapter, because it concedes the neighbourhood while refusing the identity: the view that rejects external objects and keeps ideas alone, he says, is close to the truth of the non-dual self, and the non-duality that is the final truth is reached through the Upaniṣads and not by that road. The last verse bows, and Śaṅkara points out what the bow costs: salutation needs two, the knower is not two, and the author has dragged the thing he knows onto the relative plane in order to honour it, as an example to those who cannot do without one. At the very end of the commentary Śaṅkara makes his own three salutations: to Brahman, which though unborn appears to be born; to the teacher who brought this up from the depths of the ocean of the Vedas by churning it with his reason; and to his own teacher, who destroyed the darkness in his mind.

Since the seventh century

The Kārikā's position inside its own tradition was settled by Śaṅkara and has not moved: it is a prakaraṇa, a short manual, and it keeps the company Śaṅkara chose to comment on. The Upaniṣad and the verses have been taught together ever since; Ānandagiri's subcommentary explains Śaṅkara; Nikhilananda's English edition of 1936 carries all four layers, and Gambhirananda's of 1987 gives the verses plainly for readers who want the text without the apparatus. What did move, and is still moving, is everything to do with the author.

Vidhushekhara Bhattacharya's edition of 1943 argued that the four treatises are four independent works, later collected under one title, the Āgamaśāstra; that the first is not a systematic exposition of the Upaniṣad at all; and that the author's mind is closer to Buddhist philosophy than to Vedānta, a case built on the fourth treatise's vocabulary — its dharmas for individual souls, its saṃvṛti, its awakened ones who are ādibuddha by nature, its salutation to the best of bipeds. He also supplied the dating anchor that is still used: Bhāvaviveka, the sixth-century Madhyamaka author, quotes a verse all but identical to the third treatise's fifth, and Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla quote others, which puts the verses before about 550 and makes the author's supposed proximity to Śaṅkara a matter of tradition rather than of record.

Richard King's study of 1995 took the question apart differently. He set the treatises against the Abhidharma, the Madhyamaka and the Yogācāra in turn, and concluded that the Buddhist debt is concentrated almost entirely in the fourth, which he thinks was written for a different audience and possibly by another hand; the first three, on his reading, are Vedāntic through and through and use Buddhist arguments the way any philosopher uses the arguments available to him. That is close to what Nikhilananda had said in 1936 from inside the tradition — that shared terms prove only that certain terms were the common property of Indian thought, and that the fourth treatise borrows the idealists' arguments in order to defeat the realists and then turns them on the idealists — and it is a long way from Śaṅkara, who did not admit that the chapter contained anything Buddhist at all beyond the opinions it was refuting. The one thing the argument has never been able to do is settle itself, because the evidence is a vocabulary, and a vocabulary is exactly what a philosopher borrows from the people he is arguing with.

The text is taught now much as it was taught then, verse by verse, with the commentary taken up as each verse is reached. Swami Sarvapriyananda's series at the Vedanta Society of New York goes through the twelve sentences and then through all four treatises in order, and it is the form in which most readers now meet the text: a small book that spends twelve sentences describing the mind, and two hundred and fifteen verses removing every reason anyone ever had for thinking that the mind, or anything else, came into being.

opinionĀgama — verses 1.11–1.16

Dreamless sleep is not liberation because the world regrows from a seed

In dreamless sleep there is no split between knower and known, which makes it look like liberation. The Karika says it is not: it still holds a seed, and the world grows back from that seed the moment it stirs.

Becausethe distinction rests on the term bīja-nidrā, seed-sleep, and on Śaṅkara's reason that the knower does not return because what he has known is not a cause; Prājña and turīya agree in not grasping duality, which is what makes deep sleep tempting as a picture of liberation
LimitsThe difference between the two conditions is stipulated by the commentary rather than shown, and it is the point at which a practitioner has no way to tell which one they were in.
PracticeSitting practice — a session that felt blank is not a session that went well; the mark being asked for is knowing, not the absence of contents.
After AIThe distinction between a system with no contents and a system that knows is the same distinction, and it is the one nobody has an instrument for when the question is asked about a machine.
opinionAlātaśānti: the quenching of the firebrand — verses 4.20–22

Gaudapada argues that no account of cause and effect holds together

Gaudapada, in the Mandukya Karika, argues that any account of one thing causing another contradicts itself, whichever way you set it up. If that holds, no story of how the world came to be survives examination.

Becausethe argument is a trilemma: if cause and effect are the same the cause is born and is not a cause; if different, nothing connects them; if mutual, the order cannot be stated, a father born from his son — and the seed-and-sprout example offered in proof is itself the disputed case
LimitsIt defeats a substance account of causation and not the working notion science uses, which is about dependence between events rather than about what passes from one to the other. Sapolsky Determined takes the causal chain as exhaustively real and reaches the opposite conclusion from the same premises.
PracticeNo practice follows; the verse is a demolition, and what it leaves is the refusal to accept an origin story because it is the only one on offer.
After AIAn account of why a model produced an answer runs into a version of the same trilemma — the weights are not the answer, they are not separate from it, and the order of dependence cannot be stated.
opinionVaitathya — verse 2.6

Gaudapada says a thing missing before and after is missing in between

Verse 2.6 of the Mandukya Karika states that whatever is not there at the beginning and not there at the end is not there in the middle either. Shankara applies this to everything we perceive.

Becauseit is a principle asserted and then applied by Śaṅkara to everything perceived, on the ground that whatever changes was not there before its change and will not be there after it
LimitsThe inference from absent at the ends to absent in the middle holds for the analysis the text wants and not in general; a thing can begin and end and still be there in between, which is what ordinary language means by existing.
PracticeWitness practice in review — when a judgment about your work arrives, ask where it was ten minutes ago and where it will be tomorrow before acting on it.
After AIIt is the same move a defusion exercise makes at the scale of one thought, and Hayes ACT Get Out of Your Mind supplies the trial evidence the verse does not have.
opinionĀgama — verse 1.18; Alātaśānti — verse 4.74

Gaudapada treats his own teaching as a ladder to be thrown away

Gaudapada says his own teaching is a device for getting somewhere, not a description of what is there. Even the word non-dual only means something as the opposite of dual, so it goes too.

Becauseverse 1.18 calls the talk of quarters, states, sounds and seats upadeśa, and verse 4.74 says the self is not even to be called unborn, since unborn is the correlative of born
LimitsA teaching that disowns itself cannot be argued with, and Gauḍapāda's ladder is discarded rather than refined — there is no outcome against which to check whether it was the right ladder.
PracticeHold the vocabulary you are using as a vocabulary: it is doing work now and is not the thing, and the sign that it has been mistaken for the thing is that you start defending it.
After AIEvery framework for describing what these systems are is upadeśa in exactly this sense, including the ones in this vault; the test is whether it can be put down when it stops working.
opinionAdvaita — the rope and the snake

Not seeing the rope is the whole of the snake mistake

A rope is taken for a snake only because the rope was never properly seen. Nothing extra has been added that must later be removed: when the rope is seen, the snake does not go anywhere, having never been there to go.

Becausethe case is carried by one analogy with Śaṅkara's condition attached: nobody mistakes his own fingers for something else, because they are known, and when the rope is seen the snake does not go anywhere, having never been there to go
LimitsThe analogy assumes there is a rope — a substrate that was there all along — which is exactly what Anattalakkhana Sutta declines to grant, and the two texts disagree about precisely that residue.
PracticeWitness practice in review — when a reaction to feedback grips, look for what has not been seen rather than for something to add; on this account nothing has to be removed.
After AIKegan's subject–object move is the same mechanism with an interview method and a rater manual behind it, and it is the version that can be checked.
opinionVaitathya — verse 2.29 and the ten verses preceding it

Philosophers looking for the self each find what they already believed

The Mandukya Karika says that every school of philosophy, looking for the self, finds its own founding principle — breath, the elements, the void. A man sees whatever view he has been shown.

Becausethe list is the argument: every school's ultimate principle appears on it — breath, elements, the three strands, the counted categories, merit and demerit, the void — and the schools cannot agree on the count, 25, 26 or 31, which is the reason for thinking they are not reading it off the thing
LimitsDisagreement among schools is consistent with one of them being right, which the verse does not consider. And the argument applies to itself: non-duality is another item that appeared to someone who was looking for it.
PracticeProjection log — record what you found when you looked, and what you already believed before you looked; the verse's claim is that these will match.
After AIIt is the sharpest short statement of why the machine-consciousness question stays open: each discipline finds in the system the principle it brought, and the disagreement is about what would count as evidence at all.
opinionAdvaita: nothing is ever born — verses 2.32 and 3.48

The Mandukya Karika says nothing is ever born so nobody is freed

The Mandukya Karika holds that nothing is ever born, so there is no one in bondage, no one seeking and no one set free. Being set free would be an event, and an event needs something real to happen to.

Becauseverse 2.32 carries the longest bhāṣya in the chapter, and verse 1.17 runs the conditional the other way: if the world were real its ending would be a real change, and there is no such change
LimitsIt is self-undercutting by design and says so — the teaching that removes birth is itself something that happens. Anattalakkhana Sutta takes the opposite road, ending in a release that does occur, to five men, while the discourse is being spoken.
PracticeNo practice follows, and that is the claim's content: anything you could do would be an event, and the verse denies that events occur.
After AIRead after the alignment literature it is the limit case of the specification problem: a description so complete that nothing is left for it to be a description of.
opinionVaitathya: waking is worth no more than dream — verse 2.7

The Mandukya Karika says waking is worth no more than dreaming

The Mandukya Karika argues that waking has no better claim to reality than dreaming. The test that real things work fails, because each state cancels the other's currency: a man eats his fill awake and dreams himself starving.

Becausethe argument is a symmetry rather than an observation: a man eats his fill awake and dreams himself starving, a man feasts in dream and wakes hungry, and the one difference anyone points to, that dream space is cramped, is noticed only from waking
LimitsThompson Why I Am Not a Buddhist is the direct objection: the symmetry between waking and dream is bought by defining reality so that nothing could satisfy it. Waking is also publicly checkable in a way dream is not, which the argument sets aside rather than answers.
PracticeDomain audit — treat the feeling that something is obviously so as evidence about the state you are in, and require a check that does not come from inside it.
After AIThe strongest modern version is Metzinger's world-model the system cannot recognise as a model, which reaches the same conclusion about waking with a metabolic explanation the Kārikā does not have.
opinionAdvaita — verses 3.34–35 and 3.42–45

The Mandukya Karika warns a calm session can be a failed one

The Mandukya Karika distinguishes a mind that has come to rest from one that has merely gone dull or to sleep. Some ways meditation fails feel like success, including the pleasure taken in deep absorption.

Becausethe text names the failure modes rather than arguing for the distinction: torpor, distraction, a mind still stained but looking from outside exactly like a mind at rest, and the pleasure taken in absorption, which is the mark of an enquiry that has given up
LimitsThe four failure modes are named and not defined, and the one that matters — a stained mind that looks like a still one — is by construction indistinguishable from the inside, which leaves the practitioner without a test.
PracticeSitting practice — judge a session by whether the enquiry is still running, not by whether it was peaceful; verse 3.41 concedes the difficulty as emptying an ocean with a blade of grass.
After AIIt is the oldest version of the warning Fleming Know Thyself makes with data: the state that feels best is the one least diagnostic of whether anything is working.
opinionĀgama: what the scripture says — mantra 7

The Mandukya says awareness is what the three states are made of

The Mandukya says awareness is not a fourth state sitting alongside waking, dreaming and deep sleep. It is what those three are made of, and the text can point to it only by denying everything you could take hold of.

Becausethe text argues by exclusion rather than demonstration: mantra 7 is written almost entirely in negations — six closing off the three states, six removing sight, transaction, grasp, mark, thought and reference — before three positive words arrive, śānta, śiva, advaita
LimitsAn argument by exhaustive denial cannot be checked, since anything offered against it is another content to be denied. Śaṅkara insists turīya means only the fourth and is not a fourth item, which is a reading the word itself does not force.
PracticeSitting practice — sit without trying to reach a state; on this account there is no state to reach, and every candidate you can name is one of the three you already have.
After AIA system that reports its own states supplies contents and nothing that distinguishes having them from modelling them — the distinction this mantra is built to make and cannot make from outside.

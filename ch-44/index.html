44दृग्दृश्यविवेक · Dṛg‑Dṛśya‑Viveka

Vidyāraṇya or Bhāratī Tīrtha, c. 1400 · lectures by Swami Sarvapriyananda, Vedanta Society of New York, 2016

Dṛg-Dṛśya-Viveka, दृग्दृश्यविवेक, means the discrimination between the seer and the seen, and the whole text is an exercise in one operating principle: whatever is seen is not the seer. Forms are seen by the eye, so the eye is not a form. The eye's defects are seen by the mind, so the mind is not the eye. The mind's moods are seen by something, so that something is not the mind. The text calls it the witness, and its first claim is that the witness is never itself seen. Everything after verse 1 is an unpacking of what follows if that is true: how a witness that never changes comes to feel like a person who is born, sleeps, suffers and dies; what power produces the appearance; how the appearance can be seen through in practice; and, in a postscript, who exactly gets free when the appearance goes. Sarvapriyananda's lectures follow the verses in order, one verse in the first evening and then about four an evening, and the chapter follows them, giving each verse in transliteration with a plain rendering before the commentary. It is a short text taught slowly, and its difficulty is not in the argument, which is simple, but in the claim that the argument describes what you already are.

The text names no author. Sarvapriyananda offers two candidates, Bhāratī Tīrtha and Vidyāraṇya, the author of the Pañcadaśī, and thinks Vidyāraṇya the likelier; the printed Sanskrit text used here ends with a colophon naming Bhāratī Tīrtha. Either way the text comes from South India, near what is now Mysore, six or seven hundred years ago. He has heard of versions that end at verse 31, and treats verses 32 to 46 as the author's own postscript, a revision of the same material from a different side.

Verses 1 to 5: the regress of seers

Sarvapriyananda skips the customary introductory lecture, for two reasons: nobody in the room is new to Vedanta, and the text is itself an introduction, a prakaraṇa grantha written to be studied before the Upanishads, the Gītā and the Brahma Sūtras with Śaṅkara's commentaries. He delivers the deferred introduction in the second evening, after verse 5. His method for every verse is in three stages: repeat what it says, understand it, then check whether it is so.

रूपं दृश्यं लोचनं दृक् तद्दृश्यं दृक्तु मानसम् ।
दृश्या धीवृत्तयः साक्षी दृगेव न तु दृश्यते ॥ १ ॥
rūpaṃ dṛśyaṃ locanaṃ dṛk tad dṛśyaṃ dṛk tu mānasam
dṛśyā dhīvṛttayaḥ sākṣī dṛg eva na tu dṛśyate

1Form is the seen and the eye its seer. The eye is seen and the mind its seer. The mind's movements are seen and the witness their seer. But the witness is never seen.

Verse 1 runs the regress in four quarters. Colours and shapes are seen; the eye sees them. The eye, with its sharpness or dullness or blindness, is seen; the mind sees it. The mind's contents, desire, doubt, faith, resolve, are seen; the witness sees them. The witness is seen by nothing. Sarvapriyananda draws three lessons from the first quarter and applies them at every step: the seer and the seen are different; the seer is one and the seen are many; the seer is relatively unchanging and the seen keep changing. He also reads the fourth quarter as a nesting rather than a ladder: the witness with the mind is the seer of the body and senses, and the witness with mind, body and senses is the seer of the world.

The last quarter, dṛg eva na tu dṛśyate, is the one he spends longest on. The witness is not an object of knowledge, but it is not therefore unknown. He quotes Vivekananda: the self cannot be known, but you must not go away thinking it is unknown; it is more than known, since in every knowledge the self reveals itself. The Kena Upaniṣad says the same in its own way, different from the known and different from the unknown, known in every act of knowing. Months later, in the tenth lecture, answering a question about consciousness and thought, he put the verse's claim in a sentence.

The witness never becomes the witnessed.1

नीलपीतस्थूलसूक्ष्मह्रस्वदीर्घादिभेदतः ।
नानाविधानि रूपाणि पश्येल्लोचनमेकधा ॥ २ ॥
nīlapītasthūlasūkṣmahrasvadīrghādibhedataḥ
nānāvidhāni rūpāṇi paśyel locanam ekadhā

2Blue and yellow, gross and subtle, short and long: the forms differ in many ways. The eye sees them by itself.

आन्ध्यमान्द्यपटुत्वेषु नेत्रधर्मेषु चैकधा ।
संकल्पयेन्मनः श्रोत्रत्वगादौ योज्यतामिदम् ॥ ३ ॥
āndhyamāndyapaṭutveṣu netradharmeṣu caikadhā
saṃkalpayen manaḥ śrotratvagādau yojyatām idam

3Blindness, dullness, sharpness are states of the eye; the mind registers them by itself. Apply the same to hearing, touch and the rest.

Verses 2 and 3 fill in the first two quarters with the same three lessons. One eye sees many forms, which change while it stays; that is how you know the eye is not a form. The mind knows when the eye fails; that is how you know the mind is not the eye. He glosses ekadhā in both verses as "by itself", the eye or the mind doing its work unaided, and draws the oneness from the second lesson. The rule is meant to be extended to every sense.

कामः संकल्पसन्देहौ श्रद्धाऽश्रद्धे धृतीतरे ।
ह्रीर्धीर्भीरित्येवमादीन् भासयत्येकधा चितिः ॥ ४ ॥
kāmaḥ saṃkalpasandehau śraddhā'śraddhe dhṛtītare
hrīr dhīr bhīr ity evamādīn bhāsayaty ekadhā citiḥ

4Desire, thoughts and doubt, faith and its absence, fortitude and its lack, modesty, understanding, fear: all these consciousness lights up, by itself.

The list is the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad's (1.5.3) list of what the mind does, and he pauses on two items. Dhṛti, fortitude, the ability to hold to something, is among the qualities the Gītā's thirteenth chapter names as leading to self-knowledge. And doubt and faithlessness are on the list for a reason: they too are lit up by consciousness, so the one who denies the self is using the self to deny it. He gives Śaṅkara's line that the denier is himself what he denies, and his own comparison: it is like saying, out loud, that you have no tongue. Consciousness illumines these states directly, without introspection; you do not have to look at your fear to be aware of it, and the lecture's first memorable line is that what is aware of fear is not fearful. He adds a warning that recurs through the series. A swami in Uttarakhand told monks who felt they had become the witness that they had dug a hole and fallen into it: the moment they left and met a traffic jam, that witness would go. A witness held in place by effort is not the witness; the real one is there whether or not you attend to it.

नोदेति नास्तमेत्येषा न वृद्धिं याति न क्षयम् ।
स्वयं विभात्यथानन्यानि भासयेत् साधनं विना ॥ ५ ॥
nodeti nāstam ety eṣā na vṛddhiṃ yāti na kṣayam
svayaṃ vibhāty athānyāni bhāsayet sādhanaṃ vinā

5It neither rises nor sets, neither grows nor wanes. It shines of itself, and lights up other things without any instrument.

Verse 5 gives the witness its character: self-luminous, neither rising nor setting, present in deep sleep as consciousness without an object. Sarvapriyananda qualifies the last phrase. To know the world you need the senses, and to know the body you need the mind; what consciousness lights without any instrument is the mind's own contents, and itself. His image for deep sleep is deep space, which looks dark not because there is no light but because there is nothing there to reflect it, and the image is chosen because reflection is where verse 6 goes next. He recalls a debate at a conference in which a neuroscientist said that on the usual definition there is no consciousness in deep sleep, and a philosopher of Indian thought answered that from that tradition's point of view deep sleep is the one state in which there is only consciousness. And he adds the caveat he makes in the first hour and repeats across the series: this is not anaesthesia. Awareness of pain shows you are not the pain; it does not remove the pain. He tells the story of a monk who quoted Vedanta before minor surgery and shouted like anyone else during it: the Vedanta had stayed in the books.

Verses 6 to 12: how the witness comes to feel like a person

If the witness is unchanging and the body is an object, why does the body feel conscious and why do we say "I" of it?

चिच्छायाऽऽवेशतो बुद्धौ भानं धीस्तु द्विधा स्थिता ।
एकाहंकृतिरन्या स्यादन्तःकरणरूपिणी ॥ ६ ॥
cicchāyāveśato buddhau bhānaṃ dhīs tu dvidhā sthitā
ekāhaṃkṛtir anyā syād antaḥkaraṇarūpiṇī

6By the entry of consciousness's reflection into the intellect there is knowing. The intellect stands in two parts: one is the I-sense, the other the rest of the inner instrument.

The text's answer is reflection. Consciousness is reflected in the mind as cit-chāyā, the shadow of consciousness, and Sarvapriyananda's term is reflected consciousness. His image is the sun reflected only in polished surfaces: a pool, a metal disc, a mirror, in each of which a little sun forms. The subtle body, the mind, is such a surface; a book is not. What the reflection produces, bhānam, he glosses as knowledge: every seeing, hearing, thinking, wanting and hating is possible because of it. He also flags that the verse divides the mind on purpose in a non-standard way. Vedanta usually counts four inner faculties, mind, intellect, ego and memory; here the author sets the I-sense on one side and everything else on the other, because the I-sense is what the argument needs to isolate.

छायाऽहंकारयोरैक्यं तप्तायःपिण्डवन्मतम् ।
तदहंकारतादात्म्यद्देहश्चेतनतामगात् ॥ ७ ॥
chāyāhaṃkārayor aikyaṃ taptāyaḥpiṇḍavan matam
tad ahaṃkāratādātmyād dehaś cetanatām agāt

7The reflection and the I-sense are held to be one, like a red-hot ball of iron. Through identity with that I-sense the body came to seem conscious.

He assigns the parts of the mirror image first: the face is the witness, the reflection is reflected consciousness, and the mirror is the I-sense. Then the iron ball: fire and iron look like one thing, and you cannot say where one stops.

From the I-sense the reflection is passed on, in that order: the I-sense catches it, transmits it to the rest of the mind, the mind to the senses, the senses to the body, as the moon lends the sun's light to the night, or as heat passes from fire to pan to water to vegetables. At every stage except the witness, consciousness is borrowed.

अहंकारस्य तादात्म्यं चिच्छायादेहसाक्षिभिः ।
सहजं कर्मजं भ्रान्तिजन्यं च त्रिविधं क्रमात् ॥ ८ ॥
ahaṃkārasya tādātmyaṃ cicchāyādehasākṣibhiḥ
sahajaṃ karmajaṃ bhrāntijanyaṃ ca trividhaṃ kramāt

8The I-sense identifies with three things, in order: with the reflection of consciousness, by nature; with the body, by karma; with the witness, by error.

संबन्धिनोः सतोर्नास्ति निवृत्तिः सहजस्य तु ।
कर्मक्षयात् प्रबोधाच्च निवर्तेते क्रमादुभे ॥ ९ ॥
saṃbandhinoḥ sator nāsti nivṛttiḥ sahajasya tu
karmakṣayāt prabodhāc ca nivartete kramād ubhe

9While the two related things exist, the natural one does not cease. The other two cease in order: through the exhaustion of karma, and through awakening.

The "I" is related toByHow it ends
Reflected consciousnessNature; a face and its reflectionIt does not end while both exist; in deep sleep the "I" itself is absent
The bodyKarma; the portion that produced this bodyWhen that karma is exhausted and the body dies
The witnessError; a face that thinks it is related to the mirrorKnowledge

The last row is the one the text is about. The face is in no way related to the mirror; the mirror can break and the face is untouched; yet we feel "I am that". That felt relation is not natural and not earned; it is ignorance, and, in his phrase, it can be cut only by the sword of knowledge. He adds a point the chapter would otherwise lose: the "I" is not simply false but a mixture. "I exist", "I shine", "I am bliss" are knowledge; "I am this body", "I am so-and-so", "I am happy" are error. What knowledge removes is the identifications, not the I that exists.

And he draws the consequence bluntly, in the third lecture: it is not the ego that gets liberated. Liberation is from the ego. Asked what remains afterwards, he says the mind, the ego and the body go on doing what they did before; and in answer to a question on this verse he first introduces the threefold division of karma that verse 31 will need.

अहंकारलये सुप्तौ भवेद्देहोऽप्यचेतनः ।
अहंकारविकासार्धः स्वप्नः सर्वस्तु जागरः ॥ १० ॥
ahaṃkāralaye suptau bhaved deho'py acetanaḥ
ahaṃkāravikāsārdhaḥ svapnaḥ sarvas tu jāgaraḥ

10In deep sleep, when the I-sense dissolves, even the body becomes unconscious. The I-sense half-unfolded is dream; fully unfolded, waking.

अन्तःकरणवृत्तिश्च चितिच्छायैक्यमागता ।
वासनाः कल्पयेत् स्वप्ने बोधेऽक्षैर्विषयान् बहिः ॥ ११ ॥
antaḥkaraṇavṛttiś ca citicchāyaikyam āgatā
vāsanāḥ kalpayet svapne bodhe'kṣair viṣayān bahiḥ

11The mind's movement, united with the reflection of consciousness, fashions dream objects out of impressions, and in waking, through the senses, objects outside.

मनोऽहंकृत्युपादानं लिङ्गमेकं जडात्मकम् ।
अवस्थात्रयमन्वेति जायते म्रियते तथा ॥ १२ ॥
mano'haṃkṛtyupādānaṃ liṅgam ekaṃ jaḍātmakam
avasthātrayam anveti jāyate mriyate tathā

12The subtle body, made of mind and I-sense, is one and inert. It runs through the three states, and it is born and dies.

Verses 10 to 12 use the same apparatus on the three states. Consciousness does not brighten in waking and dim in sleep; the mind does. With the I-sense fully unfolded and joined to the reflection, the senses bring in objects and that is waking; with it half unfolded, the mind makes a film out of the impressions stored from waking, and that is dream; when it dissolves, there is no surface to reflect, and that is deep sleep, in which even the body is unconscious while consciousness is exactly what it was. He borrows a senior monk's story of the stationmaster who watches the express arrive and depart without moving. Birth and death, likewise, are undergone by the subtle body, the bundle of vital force, mind and intellect, and never by consciousness; he is careful that the subtle body experiences death, with the death of the physical body, but does not itself die. The individual, the jīva, is consciousness limited by one subtle body, as one sun is reflected in many buckets. The lecture on these verses also fields the objection about blindness, with Śaṅkara's reply that the blind man is not blind but mistaken about who he is.

Verses 13 to 19: the power that produces the appearance

शक्तिद्वयं हि मायाया विक्षेपावृत्तिरूपकम् ।
विक्षेपशक्तिर्लिङ्गादिब्रह्माण्डान्तं जगत् सृजेत् ॥ १३ ॥
śaktidvayaṃ hi māyāyā vikṣepāvṛtirūpakam
vikṣepaśaktir liṅgādibrahmāṇḍāntaṃ jagat sṛjet

13Māyā has two powers, projecting and veiling. The projecting power creates the world, from the subtle body up to the whole cosmos.

सृष्टिर्नाम ब्रह्मरूपे सच्चिदानन्दवस्तुनि ।
अब्धौ फेनादिवत् सर्वनामरूपप्रसारणा ॥ १४ ॥
sṛṣṭir nāma brahmarūpe saccidānandavastuni
abdhau phenādivat sarvanāmarūpaprasāraṇā

14Creation is the spreading of all names and forms over the reality that is Brahman, existence-consciousness-bliss, as foam and the rest spread over the sea.

Where do mind, body and world come from? Verse 13 names Māyā, the power of Brahman, neither different from it nor the same, as fire is to its power to burn. Māyā has two functions and the text's central diagnostic turns on keeping them apart. Vikṣepa projects: it puts a snake where there is a rope. Āvaraṇa veils. Sarvapriyananda is careful about what is veiled. Not the rope: the rope is what you are looking at. What is hidden is that it is a rope, the difference between what is there and what appears. In the same way Brahman is not hidden, and the witness is not hidden; the difference between Brahman and world, and between witness and mind, is. On this school's reading, he says, projection by itself is no problem at all; a world appearing is no more a fault than a film playing. Combined with the veil it becomes one, and the dream turns into a nightmare. Verse 14 has the line the series keeps returning to: Māyā spreads a net of name and form over Brahman. Wood becomes a table by acquiring a name and a shape and nothing else; gold becomes a ring; water becomes a wave. Two cautions go with the images. Ramakrishna's example of sand mixed with sugar, which an ant can separate, does not fit here, because there are not two things to separate. And the foam on the sea, which the text itself uses, is not to be stretched; it is a physical picture of layering, while the rope and the snake are a picture of error, and the verse means the second.

It was at this verse, not in answer to a student, that he raised modern physics himself: he had heard Lawrence Krauss describe the universe as quantum foam on an ocean of dark matter, and found the language uncanny, while insisting that nobody eight hundred years ago knew quantum mechanics. He then relayed Swami Smaranananda's warning against the parallel, on two grounds: the theories change, and the comparison needs a deep understanding of both sides, which almost nobody who draws it has.

अन्तर्दृग्दृश्ययोर्भेदं बहिश्च ब्रह्मसर्गयोः ।
आवृणोत्यपरा शक्तिः सा संसारस्य कारणम् ॥ १५ ॥
antar dṛgdṛśyayor bhedaṃ bahiś ca brahmasargayoḥ
āvṛṇoty aparā śaktiḥ sā saṃsārasya kāraṇam

15The other power veils the difference, within, between seer and seen, and, without, between Brahman and creation. That is the cause of saṃsāra.

Verse 15 says in the text's own words what he had been insisting on: the veil covers a difference, not a thing. Outside, it hides the difference between Brahman and the world. Inside, it hides the difference between the witness and the knower, the sākṣī and the pramātā, which is the witness taken together with the mind. This inner confusion, and not the world's appearing, is what the text means by bondage.

साक्षिणः पुरतो भाति लिङ्गं देहेन संयुतम् ।
चितिच्छायासमावेशाज्जीवः स्याद्व्यावहारिकः ॥ १६ ॥
sākṣiṇaḥ purato bhāti liṅgaṃ dehena saṃyutam
citicchāyāsamāveśāj jīvaḥ syād vyāvahārikaḥ

16Before the witness the subtle body appears, joined with the gross body. Through the entry of consciousness's reflection it becomes the empirical individual.

अस्य जीवत्वमारोपात् साक्षिण्यप्यवभासते ।
आवृतौ तु विनष्टायां भेदे भातेऽपयाति तत् ॥ १७ ॥
asya jīvatvam āropāt sākṣiṇy apy avabhāsate
āvṛtau tu vinaṣṭāyāṃ bhede bhāte'payāti tat

17This individuality, by superimposition, appears even in the witness. When the veil is destroyed and the difference shines, it goes away.

Verses 16 and 17 describe the result: a mind shining by the witness's proximity, joined to a body, producing the transactional individual, and that individual superimposed on the witness by the same error as the snake on the rope. Sarvapriyananda pauses here on the word adhyāsa, superimposition, and on the sentence in which Śaṅkara opens his commentary on the Brahma Sūtras with it, which a philosopher he knows calls the deepest sentence in the philosophical literature of East or West.

तथा सर्गब्रह्मणोश्च भेदमावृत्य तिष्ठति ।
या शक्तिस्तद्वशाद्ब्रह्म विकृतत्वेन भासते ॥ १८ ॥
tathā sargabrahmaṇoś ca bhedam āvṛtya tiṣṭhati
yā śaktis tadvaśād brahma vikṛtatvena bhāsate

18Likewise a power stands veiling the difference between creation and Brahman. Under its sway Brahman appears as if changed.

अत्राप्यावृतिनाशेन विभाति ब्रह्मसर्गयोः ।
भेदस्तयोर्विकारः स्यात् सर्गे न ब्रह्मणि क्वचित् ॥ १९ ॥
atrāpy āvṛtināśena vibhāti brahmasargayoḥ
bhedas tayor vikāraḥ syāt sarge na brahmaṇi kvacit

19Here too, when the veil is destroyed, the difference between Brahman and creation shines forth. Of the two, change belongs to creation, never to Brahman.

Verses 18 and 19 grade reality, and he gives the argument for grading rather than denying. Everything in the world undergoes six changes, birth, existence, growth, alteration, decay, death; Brahman undergoes none and only appears to. Two things can be in the same place at the same time only if one of them is false: a mirage can lie on a dry desert, a snake on a rope, but real water and dry sand cannot share the ground. So the changing world is not denied but graded. It is real in the transactional sense, vyāvahārika, because its existence is borrowed, as a table's is borrowed from wood; Brahman is real in the absolute sense, pāramārthika, because its existence is its own. The world, in the word he reaches for, supervenes on Brahman.

Verses 20 and 21: the five factors

अस्ति भाति प्रियं रूपं नाम चेत्यंशपञ्चकम् ।
आद्यत्रयं ब्रह्मरूपं जगद्रूपं ततो द्वयम् ॥ २० ॥
asti bhāti priyaṃ rūpaṃ nāma cety aṃśapañcakam
ādyatrayaṃ brahmarūpaṃ jagadrūpaṃ tato dvayam

20It exists, it shines, it is dear, it has form, it has a name: five parts. The first three are the nature of Brahman; the two that follow, the nature of the world.

Verse 20 is one Sarvapriyananda calls among the most beautiful and most quoted in the text. Every experience has five factors. It exists: asti. It appears, or shines: bhāti. It is dear: priyam, which he glosses through joy, since a thing is dear only in so far as it gives some pleasure, and that pleasure is the ānanda aspect showing through. It has a name: nāma. It has a form: rūpa. The first three belong to Brahman; the last two belong to the world and are what differ.

The obvious objection comes under this verse: we do not find joy everywhere, or consciousness in a stone. He gives two ways the verse has been read, and a third of his own. The traditional reading is that all three are present everywhere but manifest unequally: existence in everything, consciousness only in minds, bliss only in calm, sāttvika minds. He commends this reading twice because it follows experience. The second reading keeps all three manifest everywhere and rethinks what consciousness is, so that even so-called matter has it in some sense. The third is that the enlightened find all three in every experience.

खवाय्वग्निजलोर्वीषु देवतिर्यङ्नरादिषु ।
अभिन्नाः सच्चिदानन्दाः भिद्यते रूपनामनि ॥ २१ ॥
khavāyvagnijalorvīṣu devatiryaṅnarādiṣu
abhinnāḥ saccidānandāḥ bhidyete rūpanāmanī

21In space, air, fire, water and earth, in gods, animals, humans and the rest, existence-consciousness-bliss are undivided. Form and name differ.

Verse 21 extends the split to everything made of the elements, from gods to bacteria, with the difference between them entirely in name and form.

Verses 22 to 31: six meditations and what they end in

उपेक्ष्य नामरूपे द्वे सच्चिदानन्दतत्परः ।
समाधिं सर्वदा कुर्याद्धृतये वाऽथवा बहिः ॥ २२ ॥
upekṣya nāmarūpe dve saccidānandatatparaḥ
samādhiṃ sarvadā kuryād dhṛdaye vā'thavā bahiḥ

22Setting aside the two, name and form, intent on existence-consciousness-bliss, one should practise samādhi always, in the heart or outside.

सविकल्पो निर्विकल्पः समाधिर्द्विविधो हृदि ।
दृश्यशब्दानुवेधेन सविकल्पः पुनर्द्विधा ॥ २३ ॥
savikalpo nirvikalpaḥ samādhir dvividho hṛdi
dṛśyaśabdānuvedhena savikalpaḥ punar dvidhā

23Samādhi in the heart is twofold, with a construct and without. The kind with a construct is again twofold, threaded through the seen or through a word.

The text now turns from analysis to practice, and Sarvapriyananda distinguishes this practice, nididhyāsana, from two neighbours it is often confused with. It is not yogic concentration, which fixes attention on one object; and it is not mindfulness, which watches phenomena arise and pass. It is a third thing: using experience to notice what is having the experience. Verses 22 and 23 announce six exercises, three inward and three outward, each set in three steps.

कामाद्याश्चित्तगा दृश्यास्तत्साक्षित्वेन चेतनम् ।
ध्यायेद्दृश्यानुविद्धोऽयं समाधिः सविकल्पकः ॥ २४ ॥
kāmādyāś cittagā dṛśyās tatsākṣitvena cetanam
dhyāyed dṛśyānuviddho'yaṃ samādhiḥ savikalpakaḥ

24Desire and the rest, arising in the mind, are the seen. Contemplate consciousness as their witness. This is the samādhi with a construct, threaded through the seen.

असंगः सच्चिदानन्दः स्वप्रभो द्वैतवर्जितः ।
अस्मीति शब्दविद्धोऽयं समाधिः सविकल्पकः ॥ २५ ॥
asaṅgaḥ saccidānandaḥ svaprabho dvaitavarjitaḥ
asmīti śabdaviddho'yaṃ samādhiḥ savikalpakaḥ

25"Unattached, existence-consciousness-bliss, self-luminous, free of duality, am I": this is the samādhi with a construct, threaded through the word.

स्वानुभूतिरसावेशाद्दृश्यशब्दावुपेक्ष्य तु ।
निर्विकल्पः समाधिः स्यान्निवातस्थितदीपवत् ॥ २६ ॥
svānubhūtirasāveśād dṛśyaśabdāv upekṣya tu
nirvikalpaḥ samādhiḥ syān nivātasthitadīpavat

26Absorbed in the taste of one's own experience, setting aside both the seen and the word: this is the samādhi without a construct, like a lamp in a windless place.

The lamp that does not flicker is the Gītā's image for the settled mind, and he says so. The three inward steps are not alternatives but stages: a thought is used as a mirror to notice the awareness in which it appears; the thought is dropped and a sentence, the text's own line in verse 25, is used as the mirror instead; then the sentence is dropped too.

हृदीव बाह्यदेशेऽपि यस्मिन् कस्मिंश्च वस्तुनि ।
समाधिराद्यः सन्मात्रान्नामरूपपृथक्कृतिः ॥ २७ ॥
hṛdīva bāhyadeśe'pi yasmin kasmiṃś ca vastuni
samādhir ādyaḥ sanmātrān nāmarūpapṛthakkṛtiḥ

27As in the heart, so outside, on any object whatever: the first samādhi is the separating of name and form from pure existence.

अखण्डैकरसं वस्तु सच्चिदानन्दलक्षणम् ।
इत्यविच्छिन्नचिन्तेयं समाधिर्मध्यमो भवेत् ॥ २८ ॥
akhaṇḍaikarasaṃ vastu saccidānandalakṣaṇam
ity avicchinnacinteyaṃ samādhir madhyamo bhavet

28"The reality is one undivided essence, marked by existence-consciousness-bliss": this unbroken thought is the middle samādhi.

स्तब्धीभावो रसास्वादात्तृतीयः पूर्ववन्मतः ।
एतैः समाधिभिः षड्भिर्नयेत् कालं निरन्तरम् ॥ २९ ॥
stabdhībhāvo rasāsvādāt tṛtīyaḥ pūrvavan mataḥ
etaiḥ samādhibhiḥ ṣaḍbhir nayet kālaṃ nirantaram

29Absorption from the taste of that bliss is the third, as before. With these six samādhis one should pass the time without a break.

The outward set runs the same three steps with the eyes open. Take any object and separate its existence from its name and form, as wood from table; Śaṅkara, commenting on Gītā 2.16, says every experience is really two, "pot" and "exists", and this step is attending to the second. The point Sarvapriyananda presses, and the reason for running the outward set at all, is that the existence you separate out cannot be held as an object. It remains as your own existence, and turns out to be one with the witness reached by the inward set; a novice who said "but that pure existence is me" was told he was right. The second step drops the object and holds a sentence, the text's line in verse 28, or the Gītā's line (4.24) in which offering, fire, offerer and act are all Brahman, the sama-darśana or same-sightedness of the Gītā. The third drops the words; he quotes Swami Ashokananda's description of it, as if someone were erasing the names and forms in front of you. Rasa in verse 29 he reads as bliss, and stabdhī-bhāva as absorption, not stillness.

With a thought or objectWith a sentenceWithout either
Inward (verses 24 to 26)Let any thought arise and notice the awareness in which it appearsDrop the thought; hold the sentence of verse 25Drop the sentence; rest in what remains
Outward (verses 27 to 29)Take any object and separate its existence from its name and formDrop the object; hold the thought of verse 28, or the Gītā's lineDrop the words; rest with eyes open

Within each set the three are steps, not options; the thought and the sentence are upāya, means, and the lectures return several times to the warning that a means held onto becomes an obstacle. Among the six, one may take up whichever one likes, move to another when bored and come back, but, as verse 29 says, without break.

देहाभिमाने गलिते विज्ञाते परमात्मनि ।
यत्र यत्र मनो याति तत्र तत्र समाधयः ॥ ३० ॥
dehābhimāne galite vijñāte paramātmani
yatra yatra mano yāti tatra tatra samādhayaḥ

30When the conceit of the body has dissolved and the supreme Self is known, wherever the mind goes, there is samādhi.

Verse 30 states the aim: that the knowledge "I am the witness, one with the existence in everything" should become a living fact rather than a conclusion. Sarvapriyananda compares the need for continued practice after a first insight to a transplant patient's medicines; the insight fades otherwise. As it stabilises, identification with body and mind thins, but body and mind do not stop. They go on functioning while Brahman, in his phrase, shines forth in every act, and this, not a trance, is what the text means by samādhi.

भिद्यते हृदयग्रन्थिश्छिद्यन्ते सर्वसंशयाः ।
क्षीयन्ते चास्य कर्माणि तस्मिन् दृष्टे परावरे ॥ ३१ ॥
bhidyate hṛdayagranthiś chidyante sarvasaṃśayāḥ
kṣīyante cāsya karmāṇi tasmin dṛṣṭe parāvare

31The knot of the heart is cut, all doubts are cut, and one's karmas waste away, when That is seen which is both the higher and the lower.

Verse 31 is not the author's own. It is Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 2.2.8, quoted, Sarvapriyananda says, as śruti-pramāṇa: instead of composing a verse on the result, the author shows that the Upaniṣad already says it. He takes the verse phrase by phrase. The heart, on Śaṅkara's reading, is the buddhi, and the knot is ignorance, which sits in the mind and is destroyed there when knowledge dawns, as darkness is by light; this is not a change of heart but a change of understanding. Doubts end because the knowledge is not a thought held in place by effort. An actor playing a beggar does not keep reminding himself that he is an actor; he plays the beggar with his whole effort, because being the actor is knowledge and being the beggar is imagination. One who has drunk milk cannot be talked into believing that black cows give black milk. And karmas waste away because they are products of ignorance; destroy the root and the fruit goes with it.

That raises the question the lecture spends longest on. If all karma is destroyed, why does the body of the liberated person not fall at once? The traditional answer divides karma in three: āgāmi, still to come; sañcita, stored; and prārabdha, the portion that has already begun to bear fruit as this life and this body. The first two are destroyed; the third runs until it is exhausted, which is why the liberated go on living, and, he adds, why there are teachers.

What continues affects the body and not the person. Enlightenment is not anaesthesia; there is pain, and there is also the clear knowledge of being the witness of the body in pain. His examples are from the end of Ramakrishna's life, with throat cancer: told by a disciple that he seemed to be in great bliss, Ramakrishna laughed and said the rascal had found him out; and when the attendant who cleaned the wound with neem leaves offered to stop because it hurt, he asked for a moment to lift his mind from the body, and then lay smiling while it was done.

The last word of the verse, parāvare, he singles out. It means the higher and the lower, the transcendent and the immanent: Brahman without attributes and Brahman with them, the absolute of Advaita and the God of religion, named in one word to show they are one reality.

With verse 31 the text is complete in the traditional shape, practice then result then close, and he has heard of versions that stop there. What follows he treats as the author's own postscript, fourteen verses that go over the same material from a different side. He kept them because they answer a question that had reached him repeatedly by letter from the lectures' online audience, and that was raised again from the floor: if I am already Brahman, who is bound, and who is seeking?

Verses 32 to 41: three selves

He opens the postscript with two answers older than the text. Asked what an enlightened person sees, since we see one walking about like anyone else, Swami Bhūteśānanda replied, "that is what you see", and then, "who sees?". And Śaṅkara, in his commentary on the Gītā's thirteenth chapter, meets the question "who is the one in ignorance?" with "why do you ask?"; "because I do not know"; "then you are the one".

अवच्छिन्नश्चिदाभासस्तृतीयः स्वप्नकल्पितः ।
विज्ञेयस्त्रिविधो जीवस्तत्राद्यः पारमार्थिकः ॥ ३२ ॥
avacchinnaś cidābhāsas tṛtīyaḥ svapnakalpitaḥ
vijñeyas trividho jīvas tatrādyaḥ pāramārthikaḥ

32The individual is to be known as threefold: the delimited one, the reflection of consciousness, and a third fashioned in dream. Of these the first is the absolute.

अवच्छेदः कल्पितः स्यादवच्छेद्यं तु वास्तवम् ।
तस्मिन् जीवत्वमारोपाद्ब्रह्मत्वं तु स्वभावतः ॥ ३३ ॥
avacchedaḥ kalpitaḥ syād avacchedyaṃ tu vāstavam
tasmin jīvatvam āropād brahmatvaṃ tu svabhāvataḥ

33The delimiting is imagined; what is delimited is real. In it, individuality is by superimposition; Brahman-hood is by nature.

The text's answer is to distinguish three selves. The pāramārthika self is the witness, the real one. The vyāvahārika self is the waking individual who transacts with the world. The prātibhāsika self is the individual in a dream, made nightly out of waking impressions. The witness is said to be avacchinna, delimited by a mind, and the lecture insists this means associated, not limited: space inside a glass of water is not shortened by the glass or moved when the glass is carried. Verse 33 says why the limiter cannot limit: it is itself imagined, and, in the commentator's phrase, the water of a mirage cannot wet a single grain of the desert, nor does the snake make the rope poisonous. Individuality is superimposed; Brahman-nature is intrinsic.

अवच्छिन्नस्य जीवस्य पूर्णेन ब्रह्मणैकताम् ।
तत्त्वमस्यादिवाक्यानि जगुर्नेतरजीवयोः ॥ ३४ ॥
avacchinnasya jīvasya pūrṇena brahmaṇaikatām
tattvamasyādivākyāni jagur netarajīvayoḥ

34The sentences "That thou art" and the rest declare the oneness of the delimited individual with the full Brahman, not of the other two.

Verse 34 fixes the reference of the great sayings, tat tvam asi and the rest: they are said of the witness-self only, never of the waking or dream self. He stresses the word pūrṇena, "with the whole". It rules out the two softer readings people reach for, that "I am Brahman" means I belong to Brahman, or that I am a spark or part of God. The identity is complete, and it runs in both directions: there is no God apart from the real you.

ब्रह्मण्यवस्थिता माया विक्षेपावृतिरूपिणी ।
आवृत्यखण्डतां तस्मिन् जगज्जीवौ प्रकल्पयेत् ॥ ३५ ॥
brahmaṇy avasthitā māyā vikṣepāvṛtirūpiṇī
āvṛtyākhaṇḍatāṃ tasmin jagajjīvau prakalpayet

35Māyā, resting in Brahman, projecting and veiling, veils its undividedness and fashions in it the world and the individual.

जीवो धीस्थचिदाभासो भवेद्भोक्ता हि कर्मकृत् ।
भोग्यरूपमिदं सर्वं जगत् स्याद्भूतभौतिकम् ॥ ३६ ॥
jīvo dhīsthacidābhāso bhaved bhoktā hi karmakṛt
bhogyarūpam idaṃ sarvaṃ jagat syād bhūtabhautikam

36The individual, the reflection of consciousness in the intellect, becomes the experiencer and the doer. This whole world, elements and their products, becomes what is experienced.

अनादिकालमारभ्य मोक्षात् पूर्वमिदं द्वयम् ।
व्यवहारे स्थितं तस्मादुभयं व्यावहारिकम् ॥ ३७ ॥
anādikālam ārabhya mokṣāt pūrvam idaṃ dvayam
vyavahāre sthitaṃ tasmād ubhayaṃ vyāvahārikam

37From beginningless time until liberation these two stand in transaction. Therefore both are called empirical.

Verses 35 to 37 retell the mechanism with the two powers: the veiled witness becomes the transactional self, becomes the experiencer and the doer, and so is bound by karma into a round that has no beginning but has an end. Asked when it began, he answers that before and after are questions that need time, and time is one of Māyā's projections.

चिदाभासस्थिता निद्रा विक्षेपावृतिरूपिणी ।
आवृत्य जीवजगती पूर्वे नूत्ने तु कल्पयेत् ॥ ३८ ॥
cidābhāsasthitā nidrā vikṣepāvṛtirūpiṇī
āvṛtya jīvajagatī pūrve nūtne tu kalpayet

38Sleep, resting in the reflection of consciousness, projecting and veiling, veils the former individual and world and fashions new ones.

प्रतीतिकाल एवैते स्थितत्वात् प्रातिभासिके ।
न हि स्वप्नप्रबुद्धस्य पुनः स्वप्ने स्थितिस्तयोः ॥ ३९ ॥
pratītikāla evaite sthitatvāt prātibhāsike
na hi svapnaprabuddhasya punaḥ svapne sthitis tayoḥ

39Since these exist only while they are perceived, they are apparent. For one who has woken from a dream, the two do not persist into another dream.

Sleep does at the level of the individual what Māyā does at the level of Brahman: it veils the waking self and world and projects a dream self and world, which last only as long as they are perceived. The point of verse 39 is that the dream self is not carried over; each night makes a new one.

प्रातिभासिकजीवो यस्तज्जगत् प्रातिभासिकम् ।
वास्तवं मन्यतेऽन्यस्तु मिथ्येति व्यावहारिकः ॥ ४० ॥
prātibhāsikajīvo yas tajjagat prātibhāsikam
vāstavaṃ manyate'nyas tu mithyeti vyāvahārikaḥ

40The apparent individual takes the apparent world as real; the empirical one takes it as false.

व्यावहारिकजीवो यस्तज्जगद्व्यावहारिकम् ।
सत्यं प्रत्येति मिथ्येति मन्यते पारमार्थिकः ॥ ४१ ॥
vyāvahārikajīvo yas tajjagad vyāvahārikam
satyaṃ pratyeti mithyeti manyate pāramārthikaḥ

41The empirical individual takes the empirical world as true; the absolute one takes it as false.

Verses 40 and 41 are the text's own account of how the three selves stand to one another. Each self takes its own world as real and the world below it as false, and the relation is the same at both steps. Sarvapriyananda corrects a common misreading here: Advaita does not say the world is true until you are enlightened and false afterwards. The rope was never a snake; the mistake was only ever ours. What "takes it as false" means in practice he gives in a sādhu's image: sometimes the sun and the moon are in the sky together by day; the awareness of Brahman blazes like the sun, and the world of name and form is there like the faint moon. What was jagat-dṛṣṭi, seeing the world, becomes brahma-dṛṣṭi, seeing Brahman.

Verses 42 to 46: the water they are made of

पारमार्थिकजीवस्तु ब्रह्मैक्यं पारमार्थिकम् ।
प्रत्येति वीक्षते नान्यद्वीक्षते त्वनृतात्मना ॥ ४२ ॥
pāramārthikajīvas tu brahmaikyaṃ pāramārthikam
pratyeti vīkṣate nānyad vīkṣate tv anṛtātmanā

42The absolute individual knows its absolute oneness with Brahman and sees nothing else; or, if it sees, sees it as unreal.

The lecture offers two readings of verse 42: the literal one, of absorption in which nothing else is seen, and the one it prefers, of a person with open eyes who sees the world and recognises it as not other than the self.

माधुर्यद्रवशैत्यानि नीरधर्मास्तरङ्गके ।
अनुगम्याथ तन्निष्टे फेनेऽप्यनुगता यथा ॥ ४३ ॥
mādhuryadravaśaityāni nīradharmās taraṅgake
anugamyātha tanniṣṭhe phene'py anugatā yathā

43Sweetness, fluidity and coolness, the properties of water, pass into the wave, and then into the foam that rests on the wave;

साक्षिस्थाः सच्चिदानन्दाः संबन्धाद्व्यावहारिके ।
तद्द्वारेणानुगच्छन्ति तथैव प्रातिभासिके ॥ ४४ ॥
sākṣisthāḥ saccidānandāḥ saṃbandhād vyāvahārike
taddvāreṇānugacchanti tathaiva prātibhāsike

44so existence-consciousness-bliss, resting in the witness, pass by relation into the empirical individual, and through it into the apparent one.

लये फेनस्य तद्धर्मा द्रवाद्याः स्युस्तरङ्गके ।
तस्यापि विलये नीरे तिष्ठन्त्येते यथा पुरा ॥ ४५ ॥
laye phenasya taddharmā dravādyāḥ syus taraṅgake
tasyāpi vilaye nīre tiṣṭhanty ete yathā purā

45When the foam dissolves, its properties, fluidity and the rest, remain in the wave. When that too dissolves, they rest in the water as before.

प्रातिभासिकजीवस्य लये स्युर्व्यावहारिके ।
तल्लये सच्चिदानन्दाः पर्यवस्यन्ति साक्षिणि ॥ ४६ ॥
prātibhāsikajīvasya laye syur vyāvahārike
tallaye saccidānandāḥ paryavasyanti sākṣiṇi

46When the apparent individual dissolves, they remain in the empirical one. When that dissolves, existence-consciousness-bliss come to rest in the witness.

Foam, wave and water are one substance under three names; the properties of water run unbroken through all three; and foam is not destroyed when it is known to be water. The dream self merges into the waking self, the waking self into the witness, and nothing has happened except a correction of knowledge. Sarvapriyananda's warning here is against picturing enlightenment as an event, the person melting into a puddle or the body vanishing in a flash of light or smoke. The person continues exactly as before. What changes is that the world, which was seen as world, is seen as Brahman appearing.

Freedom, mokṣa, is not for the person. Freedom, or mokṣa, is from the person.2

Method, not conclusion

The lectures make three admissions that a reader should carry.

The first is the text's own status. Sarvapriyananda says, in the sixth lecture, that the whole apparatus of seer and seen, witness and knower, veiling and projecting, is prakriyā, a method of teaching, and not siddhānta, the conclusion, which is only that Brahman, existence-consciousness-bliss without a second, alone exists. The distinctions are ladders. A reader who takes the three selves or the two powers as an inventory of what there is has mistaken the pedagogy for the doctrine, which is the same mistake the text warns against in the meditations.

The second is a question the method cannot answer from inside. Asked in the same lecture why Māyā exists, he replies that "why" asks for a cause, and causation is itself a product of Māyā; asked later when it began, that before and after need time, which Māyā projects; and to a young monk who wanted ignorance proved before he would try to remove it, that you cannot see darkness by any light. Readers from outside the tradition will notice that these moves can be made against any objection, and the lectures do not pretend otherwise. What they offer instead is the deep-sleep argument of verse 5 and the five-factors analysis of verse 20, both of which are claims about experience that can be checked in experience, and a practice for checking them.

The third is the gap between assent and realisation, which the first lecture illustrates before the text has properly begun: the monk who shouted under the knife, the scholar distressed at losing his learning after a stroke, the businessman told that he is not peaceful but the knower of his peace. The text asks for the third of Sarvapriyananda's three stages, checking whether the argument is so, and in the eleventh lecture he says plainly that most of us, having read Vedanta and said "I am Brahman", go on behaving as if the world were true until it becomes a reality. The postscript exists for that.

The renderings of the verses above are made from the Sanskrit and are meant to be plain rather than elegant; Nikhilananda's translation, with his notes, remains the one to read with the lectures. The chapter says little about the text's relation to modern accounts of consciousness, because the lectures say little. Apart from the Krauss remark under verse 14 and its warning, the one other mention is Sean Carroll's The Big Picture, which Sarvapriyananda cites as the best a physicalist can do about meaning, that we must manufacture it for ourselves, and as the exact opposite of what this text claims.

opinionVerses 1 to 5: the regress of seers — verse 4

Anyone who denies the witness is using it to deny it

Not being seen does not mean not being known. The text says the witness is known in every act of knowing, and someone who denies it has to use it in order to deny it.

Becauseverse 4 puts doubt and faithlessness on the list of what consciousness lights up, and Shankara's line is that the denier is himself what he denies — the lecture's comparison is saying out loud that you have no tongue.
LimitsA performative argument: it shows that denial is awkward to state, not that the thing denied exists. Metzinger and Seth both grant the phenomenology and explain it as a property of a self-model rather than of a witness.
PracticeWitness practice in review — stop looking for the observer and check instead whether anything has ever been known without one.
After AIIt is the oldest form of the argument now being run about machines: that a system reporting its states is using something to report them. The text's move works on oneself and does not transfer to a third party, which is exactly why it says nothing about the model.
opinionVerses 1 to 5: the regress of seers — verse 1

Anything you can see is not the one seeing

Verse 1 of the Drig-Drishya Viveka says that whatever can be seen is not the seer: the eye sees form, the mind sees the eye, the witness sees the mind, and nothing sees the witness.

Becauseverse 1 runs in four quarters — form seen by the eye, the eye by the mind, the mind's movements by the witness, the witness by nothing — and *dṛg eva na tu dṛśyate* is asserted rather than shown; the regress is an argument, its terminus is a claim.
LimitsA regress that stops where the text needs it to stop. Hofstadter's account has the self as a loop the system builds out of its own observations, with no terminus required, and Seth's has it as a controlled hallucination; neither needs an unseen seer.
PracticeWitness practice in review — in a review, notice the movement of the mind judging the work as one more thing appearing, rather than as the one doing the judging.
After AINothing in the claim is affected by what the delegate is. Its use is procedural: it is the move that lets a reviewer notice defensiveness as an appearance instead of acting on it.
opinionVerses 22 to 31: six meditations and what they end in — verses 22 to 29

Drig-Drishya Viveka's meditations point back to whatever is having the experience

The practice here is not concentrating on an object, and not watching thoughts come and go. It takes an experience and uses it to notice what is having that experience.

Becauseverses 22 to 29 give six meditations as two runs of the same three steps — a thought or object, then a sentence, then neither — with the warning that a means held onto becomes an obstacle.
LimitsThe distinction from mindfulness is doctrinal and the text offers no way to tell from outside which practice someone is doing. The warning about means becoming obstacles applies to the six meditations themselves.
PracticeWitness practice in review — use whatever is actually happening in the review, including the irritation, as the object; there is nothing to clear away first.
After AIIt requires no quiet room and no setup, which is the only reason it survives contact with a working day. The object is supplied by the work, including the work a model did.
opinionVerses 22 to 31: six meditations and what they end in

Enlightenment does not stop pain, it changes who is watching it

Drig-Drishya Viveka says enlightenment is not a painkiller: the pain goes on exactly as before. What changes is that the person knows themselves as the witness of a body in pain rather than as the body.

BecauseRamakrishna with throat cancer asked for a moment to lift his mind from the body and then lay smiling while the wound was cleaned, and the monk who quoted Vedanta before surgery shouted like anyone else during it; both are teaching anecdotes.
LimitsAnecdote in both directions, and the second anecdote is the text's own admission that assent and realisation come apart. ACT measures the same separation by outcome; this offers no measure.
PracticeWitness practice in review — do not expect the sting of criticism to stop; expect only that acting on it becomes optional.
After AIIt sets the right expectation for the practice this vault runs. Nothing about working with a model removes the reaction to having one's work found wanting; the claim is only that the reaction need not be the reviewer.
opinionMethod, not conclusion

Sarvapriyananda treats the witness and the veil as teaching devices

Sarvapriyananda says the whole apparatus, witness, veil and the three selves, is a way of teaching and not the conclusion. Reading it as a list of things that exist is the mistake the text itself warns against.

BecauseSarvapriyananda states *prakriyā* against *siddhānta* in the sixth lecture — the conclusion is only that Brahman alone exists, and the distinctions are ladders.
LimitsIt insulates the apparatus from objection: anything that fails is pedagogy rather than doctrine. The questions the method cannot answer from inside — why Māyā exists, when it began — are all deflected by the same move, and the lectures do not pretend otherwise.
PracticeIf you believed it you would use the seer-and-seen distinction as a move to make in a review and refuse to argue about whether the witness exists, because the text does not claim it as an entity either.
After AIIt is the disclaimer to keep attached to every L4 claim in this vault. The texts are being used here as instruments, and this is the text saying that is the correct use.
opinionVerses 13 to 19: the power that produces the appearance — verses 13 and 15

The Drig-Drishya Viveka says error begins when a difference is forgotten

The Drig-Drishya Viveka says what gets hidden is never the thing itself but a difference: the rope is still seen, only its being a rope is hidden. An appearance on its own does no harm; forgetting the difference turns it into error.

Becauseverses 13 and 15 have the rope not hidden while its being a rope is — outside, the difference between Brahman and world, inside, between witness and knower — and a world appearing is no more a fault than a film playing.
LimitsThe rope and snake is an illustration, not evidence, and the two-power account is part of the teaching apparatus the text later says is method rather than conclusion.
PracticeProjection log — record not what you projected onto a system but what difference you stopped noticing while you projected it.
After AIIt is the most useful sentence in the text for working with a model. Treating output as if a mind produced it is harmless; losing track of the difference between a mind and a model is the whole error.
opinionVerses 6 to 12: how the witness comes to feel like a person — verses 8 and 9

The I makes three identifications and only one is a mistake

The Drig-Drishya Viveka says the I joins itself to three things: to its reflection by nature, to the body by past action, and to the witness by mistake. Only the third is an error, and only an error can be ended by knowing better.

Becauseverses 8 and 9 make the distinction and the mirror can break while the face is untouched, so "I exist" is knowledge and "I am this body" is error.
LimitsA taxonomy internal to the text, with the categories doing the work. What knowledge removes is the identification and not the I, which makes the claim hard to check even from inside.
PracticeWitness practice in review — separate the sentence "this work is wrong" from the sentence "I am wrong", and notice that only the second is the identification the text is talking about.
After AIA review is where the identification shows itself fastest, and the delegate's output is a third thing in the room that belongs to nobody, which makes the separation easier to practise and no easier to hold.
opinionVerses 6 to 12: how the witness comes to feel like a person — verses 6 and 7

The body seems aware only because awareness is passed down a chain

The Drig-Drishya Viveka says awareness is reflected first in the sense of 'I', which lends it to the mind, the mind to the senses, and the senses to the body. Only the witness actually owns it.

Becauseverses 6 and 7 give the reflection and the I-sense held to be one like a red-hot iron ball, with the moon lending the sun's light and heat passing from fire to pan to water; the mechanism is analogical throughout.
LimitsA chain of borrowed analogies with no independent test at any link. Metzinger and Seth explain the same appearance by construction rather than by reflection, and their accounts have measurements behind them.
PracticeProjection log — when you attribute a mind to something, record which link in the chain you are actually observing.
After AIThe chain is now longer by one link. Whatever the model produces reaches a person already looking as though something meant it, and the lending of apparent interiority happens in the reader rather than in the machine.
contestedVerses 20 and 21: the five factors — verse 20

Vedanta says everything has five aspects and only two of them change

Drig-Drishya Viveka holds that anything you experience has five aspects: it exists, it shows itself, it is dear, it has a name and it has a form. Only the name and form change from one thing to the next.

Becauseverse 20 states it and 21 extends it to everything made of the elements; the objection that we do not find joy everywhere or consciousness in a stone is answered three different ways in the lectures, which is itself the sign that the claim is not settled.
LimitsThree incompatible answers to the same objection is a weak position, and the text's own later admission that the apparatus is method rather than conclusion applies here first.
PracticeSitting practice — in sitting, separate the two factors that change from the three that do not, and check whether the separation survives contact with an ordinary afternoon.
After AIIt is the analysis on which the Vedanta-and-information correspondence rests, which means the weakest claim in the text is load-bearing for the most modern-sounding argument built on it.
opinionVerses 1 to 5: the regress of seers — verse 4

Whatever notices fear is not itself afraid

The Drig-Drishya Viveka says that whatever is aware of a state — fear, doubt, faith — is not itself in that state, and this awareness is already there without looking for it. A witness held in place by effort is not it.

Becauseverse 4 has consciousness illumining desire, doubt, faith and fear directly, so you need not look at your fear to be aware of it; the swami told monks who felt they had become the witness that they would lose it at the first traffic jam.
LimitsACT holds the same stance functionally and measures the outcome; this text claims the witness is what you are, and has no outcome measure. Damasio's somatic markers run the other way, making the bodily state part of the judging.
PracticeWitness practice in review — when criticism lands, notice that the stinging is registered by something not stinging, and do not try to hold the noticing in place.
After AIIt is the operational half of the L4 material, the part that changes what happens in a code review, and it needs none of the metaphysics around it.

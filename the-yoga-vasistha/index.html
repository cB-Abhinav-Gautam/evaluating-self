79योगवासिष्ठ · The Yoga Vāsiṣṭha

attributed to Vālmīki, Kashmir, c. 950–1250 · Swami Venkatesananda, translator, The Concise Yoga Vāsiṣṭha, State University of New York Press, 1985

Linked to 18 chapters: supports 4 · contradicts 5 · mechanism 6 · same claim 5 · pairing 3

A prince comes home from a pilgrimage and stops eating. He is sixteen, the heir of Ayodhyā, and he will not say what is wrong. His chamberlain reports that he goes through the motions of bathing and resting like an automaton, that he spurns the palace dancers as tormentors, and that he is neither deluded nor enlightened. Summoned to court, Rāma delivers a speech that arraigns wealth, the life-span, egotism, the mind, craving, the body, childhood, youth, old age and time itself, and ends by saying that he has given up everything and gained nothing, like a tree cut but not severed from its root. Vasiṣṭha answers him, and the answer runs to twenty-nine thousand verses over thirty-two days. It is made almost entirely of stories, more than fifty of them, and they are the argument rather than illustrations of one. A queen meditates her way out of her own bedroom and finds that her kingdom is an event inside a dead brahmin's hut. A king shuts his eyes for less than an hour, lives seven years as an outcaste, and rides out the next day to meet his own children. A crow on Mount Meru explains where in the breath the thing that dies is not. What holds them together is one claim: the world is a thought, and the thinking of it is what is called creation. The renderings of verse here are made from the Sanskrit of the critical edition, whose chapter numbers differ here and there from the printed Yoga Vāsiṣṭha.

The text states a qualification that excludes people at both ends: he is fit to study it who feels "I am bound, I should be liberated". It also refuses to rest on authority. Even a young boy's words are to be accepted if they are words of wisdom; otherwise reject them like straw, though Brahmā the creator utter them.

On dispassion

Rāma's speech is constructed, not vented. He takes the goods of life in order and shows each consuming what it promises. Wealth is night to the lotus of right action and wind to the lamp of clear insight, and it seeks the man death has already chosen. The life-span is a droplet on a leaf, gnawed by the rat of time, watched by death as a cat watches a rat. Childhood, which people call happy, is subjection and nothing else, and a child's anguish at being refused is worse than a dying man's. Youth, dismissed in a litany of images each ending with the line that it does not please him, is a flash of lightning, a swell of wine, an embrace in a dream, a whirling firebrand. Old age is the royal attendant who walks in front of the king, death. Behind them stands Time, a gourmet who waits until the sun has ripened a thing and then eats it.

1.11.7 किं नामेदं वत सुखं योऽयं संसारसंसृतिः । जायते मृतये लोको म्रियते जननाय च ॥ kiṃ nāmedaṃ vata sukhaṃ yo 'yaṃ saṃsārasaṃsṛtiḥ · jāyate mṛtaye loko mriyate jananāya ca What is this happiness, then, this going round and round? The world is born in order to die, and dies in order to be born.

Two features decide what follows. Rāma names the mind as the cause rather than the sufferer: examined, the mind itself appears unreal; the three worlds exist because of it, and when it vanishes they vanish. He has reached the book's thesis before the sage opens his mouth, and lacks only a way to live in it. And his dispassion is not disgust at a misfortune — Vasiṣṭha marks the distinction and praises it, since dispassion born of discrimination is superior to dispassion born of a circumstance.

On the behaviour of the seeker

The second book establishes that knowledge worked out alone still needs confirming, and then destroys fatalism.

Vyāsa's son Śuka had reached the truth by his own contemplation and could not bring himself to say of it, this is the truth. He asked his father how the diversity of the world came into being; Vyāsa answered at length, and Śuka thought, all of this I knew already. Vyāsa sent him to king Janaka, who ignored him for a week at the gate, entertained him for a second week with dancers, and then said in one sentence what Vyāsa had said at length: this diversity arises from mental modifications and ceases when they cease. Śuka, hearing his own conclusion from someone else, was at peace. The moral is not that a teacher supplies knowledge, but that self-acquired knowledge lacks a certain finality, which is got socially.

The attack on fate is blunter than anything in the older Vedānta. What people call fate is the self-effort of a previous life, arriving as consequence.

2.4.17 प्राक्तनं चैहिकं चेति द्विविधं विद्धि पौरुषम् । प्राक्तनोऽद्यतनेनाशु पुरुषार्थेन जीयते ॥ prāktanaṃ caihikaṃ ceti dvividhaṃ viddhi pauruṣam · prāktano 'dyatanenāśu puruṣārthena jīyate Know human effort to be of two kinds, the past and the present. The past is quickly defeated by present effort.

The argument is empirical in the ordinary sense. No one has ever seen a fate; everyone has seen an action followed by its result. A man goes abroad by travelling and appeases his hunger by eating. If an astrologer predicts that a young man will become a great scholar, does he become one without studying.

2.11.59 मोक्षद्वारे द्वारपालाश्चत्वारः परिकीर्तिताः । शमो विचारस्सन्तोषश्चतुर्थस्साधुसङ्गमः ॥ mokṣadvāre dvārapālāś catvāraḥ parikīrtitāḥ · śamo vicāras santoṣaś caturthas sādhusaṅgamaḥ Four gatekeepers are named at the door of freedom: calm, enquiry, contentment, and fourth, the company of the good.

Each definition is behavioural rather than devotional. Self-control is being neither elated nor depressed by what the senses bring. Contentment is renouncing all craving for what is not obtained unsought. Enquiry is the question who am I, and how has this come to be — and Venkatesananda's parenthesis is the sharpest gloss in the book: enquiry is not reasoning and not analysis, it is directly looking into oneself. Vasiṣṭha attaches a guarantee: practise any one of the four diligently and the others will be found in you.

On creation

The third book carries the doctrine, and states it before illustrating it. The world has not come into being, does not exist, and will not be destroyed, because nothing ever happened for it to be the result of. There is no cause and no effect; these are words, not facts. When Rāma objects that a bracelet is at least made of gold, Vasiṣṭha turns the image: the bracelet is made of gold, but gold is not made of bracelet.

3.3.30 मनसा तन्यते सर्वमसदेवेदमाततम् । यथा सङ्कल्पनगरं यथा गन्धर्वपत्तनम् ॥ manasā tanyate sarvam asad evedam ātatam · yathā saṅkalpanagaraṃ yathā gandharvapattanam All this, spread out and unreal, is spun out by the mind, like a city of the imagination, like a city of the gandharvas.

The order of appearance is a sequence rather than a creation. Infinite consciousness regards its own intelligence in its own heart; at that moment an unreal division arises, and with it the notion of space, which then appears as the element space; that itself believes itself to be air, then fire, then water, then earth. At the same moment consciousness holds the notion of a unit of time equal to a millionth of the twinkling of an eye, and the whole scale up to an epoch unrolls from it. The jīva is a name for consciousness that has taken itself to be atomic; when it wishes to see, eyes are formed. Causation is replaced by coincidence, for which the text has a standing image it uses in three separate books: a crow alights on a coconut palm at the moment a coconut falls, and the two events have nothing to do with one another.

3.103.18 सकर्तृकर्मकरणं यदिदं चेत्यमाततम् । द्रष्टृदर्शनदृश्याढ्यं तत्सर्वं चित्तमेव वः ॥ sakartṛkarmakaraṇaṃ yad idaṃ cetyam ātatam · draṣṭṛdarśanadṛśyāḍhyaṃ tat sarvaṃ cittam eva vaḥ Doer, deed and instrument, all this spread-out thinkable thing, rich with seer and seeing and seen — all of it is only mind.

The inherited image is the rope taken for a snake, and the text uses it with one adjustment: both terms of the mistake are manufactured, and what replaces them is not a corrected object but a different kind of seeing.

3.114.59 रज्जुसर्पविकल्पौ द्वावज्ञानैकोपकल्पितौ । ज्ञेन त्वेकैव निर्णीता ब्रह्मदृष्टिरकृत्रिमा ॥ rajjusarpavikalpau dvāv ajñānaikopakalpitau · jñena tv ekaiva nirṇītā brahmadṛṣṭir akṛtrimā Rope and snake are two alternatives, and ignorance alone has fashioned both. For the knower there is one settled seeing, unmanufactured, and it is of Brahman.

Līlā

Queen Līlā, wanting her husband Padma to live forever, asked Sarasvatī that when the king left his body his soul should remain in the palace. Padma died of a battle wound. Sarasvatī told her to cover the body with flowers so that it would not decay, and then that there are three kinds of space — psychological, physical, and the infinite space of consciousness — and that the last can be entered by meditation. Līlā entered it and found her husband on a throne in another kingdom, surrounded by kings who adored him, and none of them saw her.

What she learns next is the text's central manoeuvre. The kingdom she has ruled all her life is itself an event inside somebody else's hut. In a corner of the Creator's mind there was a dilapidated shrine with the fourteen worlds for rooms, and in it lived a holy man named Vasiṣṭha with his wife Arundhatī. One day he saw a royal procession and wished to ride such an elephant. He died; his wife asked for the same boon Līlā had asked for; and out of that one wish he became king Padma and she became Līlā. It is eight days since this happened.

Līlā objects that she and her husband have lived a long time, and the answer is what the book turns on: time has no fixed span any more than space does, and in the twinkling of an eye the jīva undergoes the illusion of dying, forgets what came before, and begins to think I am so many years old. When the two women fly out to look, they travel millions of miles and remain in the same room on another plane of consciousness. In the innermost world they find king Viduratha, who asks how he can have died only yesterday and lived seventy years in this body. On her first visit Līlā was invisible, because she still held the notion I am Līlā.

The stories that follow

Karkaṭī, a famine-demoness who cannot eat enough, performs a thousand years of penance in order to become a living steel needle and so enter every heart at once — and discovers that a needle cannot eat a continent. The ten sons of Indu, judging that being a creator was the only office not cut short by time, sat contemplating I am Brahmā seated on a full-blown lotus until their bodies withered, went on standing through the cloudburst that ended the epoch, and at the next dawn were the creators. Ahalyā is punished by ice water, boiling oil, the legs of an elephant and the whip, and Indra tells her husband, laughing, that one may punish a body but cannot alter a mind saturated with something: the body does not create the mind, "the mind creates the body." Then there are the three princes of a city that did not exist, two of them unborn and the third not yet conceived, who ate the fruit of three trees of which two did not exist and the third had not been planted, and cooked ninety-nine minus a hundred grams of rice for three holy men, two without bodies and one without a mouth. A nanny tells that one to a little boy, and Vasiṣṭha says the creation of the world is no more real than the nanny's story.

Lavaṇa

King Lavaṇa was in court when a juggler waved a bunch of peacock feathers and offered him a horse. The king shut his eyes and sat motionless; no one dared disturb him; when he opened them he was trembling and did not recognise his own ministers. He then described the intervening hour. The horse had carried him into a desert and run off; he begged food from a dark girl who gave it on condition of marriage, and became a member of her tribe. He fathered four children, traded in meat, grew old and black as soot, and spent seven years at it. Then came a famine in which people chewed pebbles for meat, and his youngest child asked him for food, and he answered, eat my flesh, and the child said, give me; and he built a pyre to end it, and as he climbed on he found himself in court being hailed by everyone.

The ministers point out that the juggler took no payment and therefore cannot have been a juggler. Vasiṣṭha, who says he was in the court that day, explains that Lavaṇa had performed a great rite entirely in his mind for a year while sitting in his garden, that mental performance earns the fruits of the rite, and that the visitor was a messenger sent to test him. But the story does not end there. The next day the king rode south to see whether the places existed, found the very scenes and the very people, met his own destitute children, and met an old woman weeping for a husband and for a daughter who had married a handsome king. The text does not smooth this over. It offers three possibilities in a row — that the tribespeople had it in their own minds, that each saw what the other experienced, and that hallucinations become reality when enough people have them — and then asks the only question that matters: what is the truth of anything, except how it is experienced in one's own consciousness.

3.118.5 ज्ञानभूमिश्शुभेच्छाख्या प्रथमा समुदाहृता । विचारणा द्वितीयात्र तृतीया तनुमानसा ॥ jñānabhūmiś śubhecchākhyā prathamā samudāhṛtā · vicāraṇā dvitīyātra tṛtīyā tanumānasā The first ground of knowledge is called the good wish. Enquiry is the second here, and the thinned mind the third.

3.118.6 सत्त्वापत्तिश्चतुर्थी स्यात्ततोऽसंसक्तिनामिका । पदार्थाभावनी षष्ठी सप्तमी तुर्यगा स्मृता ॥ sattvāpattiś caturthī syāt tato 'saṃsaktināmikā · padārthābhāvanī ṣaṣṭhī saptamī turyagā smṛtā Arriving at clarity is the fourth, then the one called non-attachment. The sixth is where objects stop appearing, and the seventh is called the one that has gone to the fourth state.

The first stage is a question a person asks himself — why do I continue to be a fool — and the resolve to seek holy men and scriptures. The fourth is where turning away from sense-pleasure stops being an effort. Vasiṣṭha adds that the highest can be reached by anyone, including animals, because it involves only the rise of wisdom.

3.122.17 संसारोग्रारघट्टेऽस्मिन्या दृढा यन्त्रवाहिनी । रज्जुस्तां वासनामेतां छिन्द्धि राघव यत्नतः ॥ saṃsārograraghaṭṭe 'smin yā dṛḍhā yantravāhinī · rajjus tāṃ vāsanām etāṃ chinddhi rāghava yatnataḥ In this cruel waterwheel of the world there is a stout rope that drives the machine. That rope is conditioning: cut it, Rāghava, and take trouble over it.

On existence

If the third book asks how a world appears, the fourth asks why it stays. Rāma proposes that the universe rests in seed form in the supreme being between epochs, and Vasiṣṭha rejects it: a seed and a sprout are both objects apprehended by the senses, and nothing subtler than space can house a seed. How can a tree spring out of the empty space in a jar. What holds the world up instead is repetition: as air passing over different flowers takes their scent, the mind entertaining different notions takes on those moods and makes bodies suitable to them. The conviction determines the action, and the action strengthens the conviction.

4.17.19 मनोमात्रं जगच्चक्रं मनः पर्वतमण्डलम् । मनो व्योम मनो देहो मनो मित्रं मनो रिपुः ॥ manomātraṃ jagaccakraṃ manaḥ parvatamaṇḍalam · mano vyoma mano deho mano mitraṃ mano ripuḥ The wheel of the world is mind only, and the ring of mountains is mind. Mind is the sky, mind is the body, mind is the friend and mind is the enemy.

Śukra, a boy attending his father Bhṛgu through a long penance, sees a nymph in the sky, closes his eyes and follows her, and lives with her for a period equal to eight world-cycles before falling into a long chain of births. Through all of it the original body sits in front of his father, dried to skin and bone. Bhṛgu rises after a hundred celestial years, finds the corpse with worms breeding in its eye-sockets, and resolves in a rage to curse Time. Time appears in person, six-faced and six-armed: I am the consumer and you are our food, and this is ordained by nature, not by likes and dislikes. Every being has two bodies, physical and mental, and only mental actions are actions.

The most exact mechanism in the book is the pair of demon stories. Śambara, a magician-king, creates three fighters, Dama, Vyāla and Kaṭa. Having had no previous incarnation, they have no conditioning whatever; they do not know the meaning of victory or defeat, are not afraid of death, and cannot be killed. Brahmā gives the gods their order: in the course of fighting the ego-sense will arise in them, and then they will develop tendencies; without conditioning, even a mosquito becomes immortal; so do what you can to create in them the feelings of I and mine. It works, in a chain the text sets out step by step: I am, then this is mine, then a wish to prolong it, then fear, then flight. Śambara then makes a second three, identical except in one habit: whenever the ego-sense raised its head, they subdued it with the question who am I. They win, and when they are finally killed they are liberated on the spot, because there was no one there to die.

On dissolution

The fifth book is about quieting, and it distinguishes two ways of reaching it: following the path a teacher indicates, and having self-knowledge drop into one's lap. The central story is the second kind.

Janaka — the same king who had confirmed Śuka two books earlier — is walking in a pleasure-garden when he overhears perfected beings singing. They contemplate the self as what appears when seer and object meet without division, as the light that illumines everything that shines, and as the ground of all language, covering the alphabet from a to ha and indicated by the word aham, I. Then the reproach: people run after other things, having given up the Lord who dwells in the cave of the heart.

Janaka goes back to the palace, dismisses his attendants and shuts himself in. The soliloquy that follows is the finest writing in the book. I am bound without a cord, he says; I am tainted without impurity; I am fallen though remaining at the top. The pores of the earth are filled with the dead bodies of beings, and that is why it looks solid. Intention is the seed of the world-appearance, and I shall dry it up. I shall slay this thief, the mind, who has stolen my wisdom.

Then the bodyguard comes in and says that the bath is prepared, the priests are waiting, and noble men are never unpunctual. Janaka ignores him. What he concludes, after considering renunciation and rejecting it, is the position the whole book has been building towards: what difference does it make whether I am ceaselessly active or idle, since nothing here endures either way. And when the mind stops saying I do this and I enjoy this, action becomes non-action. He rises, goes to his duties, and performs them as if in deep sleep though wide awake. Nothing outward changes. Vasiṣṭha's conclusion is the most self-undermining sentence a scripture can contain: self-knowledge is not had from a teacher, nor from scripture, nor from good works, but only by enquiry inspired by the company of the wise.

5.11.8 चिन्ताजालविलासोत्था इमास्संसारसृष्टयः । तस्मात्त्यक्त्वा विचित्रां त्वं चिन्तामुपशमं व्रज ॥ cintājālavilāsotthā imās saṃsārasṛṣṭayaḥ · tasmāt tyaktvā vicitrāṃ tvaṃ cintām upaśamaṃ vraja These creations of the world rise from the play of a net of thinking. Let go of that many-coloured thinking and come to quiet.

The other stories test the claim from other sides. Puṇya and Pāvana are brothers whose parents die on the same day; the one who does not grieve recites what his brother has been — a swan with swan-relatives, a tree with tree-relatives, a prince, a donkey, a fly, an ant — and asks whom he is supposed to mourn. Bali remembers his father's allegory of a king who sits silent while his minister does everything: the king is the self and the minister the mind, and the practice must be twofold at once, because until the king is seen the minister is not conquered and until the minister is conquered the king is not seen. Gādhi, immersed in a river for a moment, lives an outcaste's whole life and eight years on the throne of Kīra; afterwards a passing guest tells him the same story as news, and two populations he travels to confirm it — and the Lord's explanation is that the confirmation was illusion too, and also that the man Kaṭañja probably did exist and had merely got reflected in him.

Vītahavya, leaving the body after three hundred years of samādhi, says farewell in turn to attraction, hate, pleasure, sorrow, craving, delusion, the cave and his walking staff, thanking each; he thanks sorrow for having driven him to the quest. Suraghu, a hill-king troubled by the thought that the rewards and punishments he hands down will come back to him, subtracts the mountain, the tribe, the kingdom, the family, the body, the mind and the ego-sense until only the knower is left — and then subtracts the knower too, because whatever is an object of knowledge is not the self. He gives the book's definition of samādhi: the enlightened are forever in it even when engaged in the world, and one whose mind is not at peace does not get it by sitting in the lotus posture.

On liberation

The sixth book is nearly as large as the other five together, and it changes register. Vasiṣṭha stops arguing and hands over instruments.

The first is Bhuśuṇḍa, a crow on a wish-fulfilling tree on Mount Meru who has outlasted everyone in heaven or on earth. His origin is deliberately squalid — attendant goddesses got drunk, swans mated with a blue crow, twenty-one eggs were laid — and his memory is long: eleven thousand years in which lava covered the earth, ten thousand in which it was covered with the corpses of demons, ten complete creations remembered. He survives each dissolution by a counter-elemental contemplation: when twelve suns burn, he contemplates Varuṇa, lord of waters; when the wind uproots mountains, the mountain; when the flood comes, the wind.

Asked how he has lasted, he answers with the breath, and the account is precise. Prāṇa is a flame and rises from the heart-lotus, ending twelve finger-breadths outside the body at a point called the dvādaśānta. Apāna is water, and arises exactly where prāṇa ended, coming down again into the heart. Between them are two junctures where neither is moving: when prāṇa has ceased and apāna has not risen, and when apāna has ceased and prāṇa has risen only a little. Those are the kumbhakas, and they happen by themselves in every breath of every person.

6.26.61 प्राणक्षयपदान्तस्थमपानक्षयकोटिगम् । अपानप्राणयोर्मध्यं चिदात्मानमुपास्महे ॥ prāṇakṣayapadāntastham apānakṣayakoṭigam · apānaprāṇayor madhyaṃ cidātmānam upāsmahe Standing at the end of the place where prāṇa dies away, reaching the tip where apāna dies away, in the middle between apāna and prāṇa — that self which is awareness, we worship.

His own summary is not technical at all: he does not contemplate past or future, does what has to be done without thinking of results, praises and censures nobody, sees one substratum in wood, a beautiful woman, a mountain, ice, fire and space, and looks at a worn-out thing with fresh eyes as if it were new. That, he says, is the secret of his longevity.

The second instrument is a demolition of worship from inside it. Vasiṣṭha asks Śiva how the Lord is to be worshipped and is told that God is not Viṣṇu or Śiva or Brahmā, not the wind or the sun or the moon, not the brahmin or the king, not I or you, but that splendour which is not made and has neither beginning nor end. Then the part that bites: the Lord is to be worshipped with whatever arrives unsought — with the enjoyments of the body, with illnesses and every sort of suffering, with life and death and dreams, with poverty and prosperity, with fights and quarrels as well as with sports, with pleasures whether the scriptures sanction them or forbid them. The discipline attached is one sentence: never make the least effort to attain what you do not possess.

The image the book relies on is a rock, soft, omnipresent and eternal, inside which countless lotuses blossom; their petals sometimes touch and sometimes do not, some face down and some face up, some have roots intertwined and some have no roots at all; all things exist within it, though they do not. The rock is consciousness, and it is rocklike in being homogeneous. Even if a sculptor carves forms in a rock it is still rock; the names and forms of this universe exist in consciousness as figures exist in uncut stone.

Rāma points out that Vasiṣṭha had told him earlier to enquire into ignorance and now says ignorance does not exist. Vasiṣṭha admits the pedagogy: you were not fully awakened then, and words like ignorance and jīva were invented as aids for the unawakened. The word Venkatesananda renders as aids, the text glosses as also meaning trick.

Śikhidhvaja and Cūḍālā

The longest story in the book is about a teacher who cannot be heard. King Śikhidhvaja of Mālva married Cūḍālā of Sauraṣṭra, and both began to ask what there is in the world which, once had, keeps the mind from sorrow again. Cūḍālā worked it out first, alone, by elimination: the body is inert, the senses depend on the mind, the mind is prompted by the intellect, the intellect is directed by the ego-sense, the ego-sense is conjured up by the jīva as a child conjures a ghost, and the jīva is consciousness aware of itself as its own object.

6.82.30 सद्वासद्वा यदाभासि चित्समाचामति स्वतः । स्वं रूपमलमुत्सृज्य तदेव भवति क्षणात् ॥ sad vāsad vā yad ābhāsi cit samācāmati svataḥ · svaṃ rūpam alam utsṛjya tad eva bhavati kṣaṇāt Whatever shines, real or unreal, awareness sips up of itself; letting its own form go entirely, in a moment it becomes that very thing.

She told her husband, and he laughed. You are childish and ignorant, my dear, and surely you are prattling. Then, worse: never mind, enjoy the pleasures afforded to you. He went out of the inner apartments, and Cūḍālā thought it a pity and went back to her work. Everything that follows is the consequence of that refusal.

She acquires the ability to fly and to enter other bodies, and judges it unwise to show him. He announces that he is going to the forest; he tells her she is a child and orders her to rule. That night he leaves and builds a hut on Mandara mountain with a bamboo stick, a water pot, a rosary and a deer skin. She finds him, looks ahead with psychic vision, sees everything as it is destined to happen, bows to it, and flies home to run the state. They do not see each other for eighteen years. When his mind has ripened she comes to him at night, finds him emaciated and darkened as if he had bathed in a river of ink, and takes the form of a boy brahmin named Kumbha.

Kumbha tells him two stories. In the first, a rich man performs austerities for the wish-fulfilling jewel; it appears almost at once, within reach, and he cannot believe it, because the scriptures say it takes a lifetime of striving; he does not pick it up; it goes; the celestials throw a piece of glass in front of him instead, and he snatches that up and goes to the forest to suffer. In the second, a caged elephant breaks out, its rider falls in front of it, and the elephant, overcome by pity, spares him; the rider digs a pit, and the elephant is back in chains within days. The jewel is total renunciation, which Śikhidhvaja had when he left the empire, and the glass is the asceticism he picked up instead. The elephant is him, the rider is ignorance, and the cage is desire — an iron cage decays with time, but the cage of desire grows stronger. Then the sentence the story exists for: why did you not listen to the wise words of your wife Cūḍālā, who is a knower of the truth.

There follows a staircase. He says he has renounced kingdom, palace, country and wife; those were never his, so renouncing them is not renunciation. He renounces the forest; not yours. He cleanses his heart of the idea that the hermitage is his; not yours. So he burns everything in the hut, the rosary first, because he is done with the delusion that repeating a mantra is holy. Kumbha says: still not everything. He resolves to destroy the body and is stopped. The one thing to be renounced is the mind, and the instrument is the question who am I. Kumbha adds the qualification that keeps the book from nihilism: only creation is negated, not what is — the false thing is the limiting adjunct, the mind.

The last movement is a test, and it is domestic. Kumbha invents a curse from the sage Durvāsa which turns him into a woman every night, and Śikhidhvaja's reaction is the point: one's fate affects only the body, not the embodied one. They marry. Then she conjures Indra with an invitation to heaven, and he declines — to me heaven is everywhere and nowhere. Then she conjures a pleasure garden and a young man more beautiful than himself, and appears in his embrace where her husband will find her. He finds them, is not agitated, and turns to go. She drops the form and stands there as Cūḍālā. His speech of gratitude is long; she deflects it. When I saw you performing meaningless austerities my heart was pained, and I relieved that pain by waking you; it was for my own joy, and I do not deserve any praise for it. Asked what they should do now, she says they should be what they are at the beginning, in the middle and at the end — king and queen — and abandon the one thing that remains, which is delusion. She crowns him emperor; he rules ten thousand years and goes out like a lamp without oil.

The book ends with Rāma absorbed and the assembly unable to rouse him. Viśvāmitra asks Vasiṣṭha to bring him back, because he still has a great deal to do for the three worlds. Vasiṣṭha enters Rāma's heart through the suṣumṇā, the breath begins to move, and Rāma opens his eyes and says that there is nothing he should do or should not do, but that his teacher's words are to be honoured. The book that spent thirty-two days proving that the world is a thought ends by sending its pupil back to govern one.

Since 1985

The largest change since this abridgement appeared has been to the text's own history, and it reverses what Christopher Chapple told readers in his introduction. He reported the view, taken from T. G. Mainkar, that an original work of Vasiṣṭha had been expanded first into the Laghu-Yogavāsiṣṭha, also called the Mokṣopāya, and then into the large Yoga Vāsiṣṭha. A project at Halle-Wittenberg and Marburg, led by Walter Slaje and Jürgen Hanneder, has since produced a critical edition of the Mokṣopāya from Kashmirian manuscripts and established something different: it is not an abridgement of anything. It is an anonymous Kashmirian work of about the tenth century, and the Yoga Vāsiṣṭha is a later redaction of it, made between the eleventh and the fourteenth centuries, in which the text was fitted out with Vedāntic and devotional apparatus it did not originally have. The critical volumes appeared between 2011 and 2019, together with the commentary of the eighteenth-century Kashmiri Bhāskarakaṇṭha. What the abridged Yoga Vāsiṣṭha offers is therefore the orthodox recension of a work that was, in its first form, conspicuously unorthodox — which fits the text's own refusal to treat revelation as decisive.

The doctrine has always sat awkwardly in Vedānta. The text is an early statement of what later writers called dṛṣṭi-sṛṣṭi, the position that seeing is creating, and Prakāśānanda cited it in the sixteenth century in exactly that cause. Its resemblance to Yogācāra is close enough that Chapple noted the parallels with the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra: both make mind a creative force, both deny the independent reality of appearances, both hold that purifying past impressions is what liberation consists of. Whether that makes it a Hindu text with Buddhist borrowings or the reverse is unsettled, and the Kashmirian origin has since supplied a third candidate in the Trika Śaivism of that region. Compared with Gauḍapāda's Mandukya Karika, which argues the same non-origination from four states of consciousness and a handful of similes, the Yoga Vāsiṣṭha argues it from narrative, and gets further with the problem of how an unreal thing can be reliably shared. Compared with the Aṣṭāvakra Gītā, which denies that any practice could make one free, it is much more willing to prescribe. And compared with the Pañcadaśī, it declines to be systematic — Vasiṣṭha admits outright that the terms he used two hundred pages earlier were scaffolding for a pupil who was not ready.

What has held up best is the part that needs no metaphysics. The insistence that fate is a name for one's own past conduct, and that no one has ever observed a fate, reads now as it must have read then. So does Suraghu's claim that a man engaged all day in the world can be in samādhi while a man in the lotus posture may not be. So does Janaka's morning, which answers the objection that the doctrine must lead to withdrawal. What has aged is the machinery around it — the cosmologies of world-cycles, the psychic powers catalogued at length in the Cūḍālā story, the graded merits assigned to thirteen seconds of meditation. The text half-knows this. It says of its own illustrations that they were used with a definite purpose and a limited intention, that they are not to be taken literally, and that their significance is not to be stretched beyond the intention. And it says what it wants done with all of it in the plainest words in the book: "this world-appearance is like a long dream. Wake up, wake up."

opinionOn creation — Lavaṇa, and the table of outer and inner spans

A king lived seven years in under an hour of court time

King Lavaṇa shut his eyes in court for less than an hour and came out having lived seven years as an outcaste in the Vindhyas. The chapter tabulates seven such cases: felt duration is generated with the experience and measures nothing outside it.

Becauseseven invented cases measure nothing, though the phenomenon they point at is one everybody has nightly, and Vasiṣṭha states the general form to Līlā: in the twinkling of an eye the jīva begins to think I am so many years old.
LimitsNothing separates a long experience from a short one laid down with a long memory attached, which is what a psychologist would reach for first. The book leaves its own hole open: Lavaṇa rides out next day and finds the places and the people, and three incompatible accounts of that are offered in a row without a choice.
PracticeTreat how long a stretch of work felt as a report on the work rather than a quantity, and never estimate from it: check the clock, the commits, the log.
After AIAn afternoon of prompting can feel like a week of building and produce an hour's work, and the feeling is what gets reported in standups. METR 2025 found developers certain they had been sped up while the clock said nineteen per cent slower.
opinionOn liberation — Śikhidhvaja and Cūḍālā

Cudala's husband heard from a boy what he refused from his wife

Queen Cūḍālā worked the argument out first and alone and told her husband, who laughed and told her to go and enjoy herself. Eighteen years later she returned as a boy brahmin named Kumbha, said the same things, and was heard.

Becausethe story holds the content fixed and varies only the speaker, which is the shape of an experiment, but it is one invented case, and the eighteen years are also spent on the husband's mind ripening, so the two explanations are not separated.
LimitsThe text explains the failure as the husband's unripeness rather than his contempt, which quietly excuses the person who would not listen. And Cūḍālā's remedy is to impersonate someone with standing rather than to fix the hearing, which works in the story and is not available to anyone else.
PracticeWhen a proposal is rejected, re-run it later from a different mouth before concluding it was wrong, and keep a note of whose proposals have needed that.

opinionOn existence — Dama, Vyāla and Kaṭa, with the second three

Demons with no conditioning could not be killed until they said mine

The magician Śambara made three fighters with no previous life and so no conditioning: they did not know victory from defeat, were not afraid of death, and could not be killed. The gods were told to create in them the feelings of I and mine.

Becausethe chain is set out step by step — I am, this is mine, a wish to prolong it, fear, flight — and a second set of three, identical but for the habit of putting down the ego-sense, wins; the variable is invented along with the demons.
LimitsThe promise shifts between the halves. No conditioning is said to make a being unkillable; the enquiry habit does not, since the second three are killed like anyone else and what changes is that nobody is there to mind.
PracticeWitness practice in review — catch the step where the work becomes mine, since everything the story warns about follows that word and not the criticism that arrives after it.
After AIThe reason a review of code you claimed lands differently from a review of code you merely accepted is the word mine, and the story says that is the whole of the difference — along with what follows it: the wish to prolong it, then defensiveness.
opinionOn the behaviour of the seeker — Śuka at Janaka's gate

Suka knew the answer already and needed a king to say it

Vyāsa's son Śuka had worked out the truth alone and could not bring himself to say of it, this is the truth. He was at peace only when king Janaka gave him his own conclusion back in one sentence: finality comes from someone else.

Becauseit is a single invented case with the moral stated by the narrator, though it is built so that the content is held fixed and only the speaker changes — Vyāsa had said the same thing at length, and Śuka's response was that he knew all of it already.
LimitsThe book withdraws it two books later, where Vasiṣṭha says self-knowledge is had neither from a teacher nor from scripture nor from good works. What Janaka supplies is confirmation rather than knowledge, and the story never separates confirmation from agreement.

contestedOn dissolution — Suraghu's subtraction, with Janaka's soliloquy

Suraghu says the lotus posture gives nothing and daily work prevents nothing

Suraghu's definition of samādhi is that the awakened are in it at all times, including while engaged in the world, and that a man whose mind is not at peace does not get it by sitting in the lotus posture.

BecauseJanaka is the demonstration — he considers renunciation, rejects it and goes to his duties with nothing outward changed — but the sitting traditions hold that a posture and a protected hour are how the state is trained, and Suraghu reached his own definition by subtracting his way through it in seclusion.
LimitsIf the state leaves no mark in posture or conduct, nothing in conduct can confirm it either, which makes it unfalsifiable in the convenient direction. The book undercuts it too: asked the same question, Bhuśuṇḍa answers with habits — does what has to be done without thinking of results, praises and censures nobody, sees a worn-out thing as though it were new.

opinionOn existence — the rejection of the seed, with verse 3.122.17 on conditioning as the rope that drives the waterwheel

Vasistha says conviction makes the action and the action strengthens the conviction

Nothing stores the world between times, since nothing subtler than space could house a seed. What keeps a world standing is repetition: as air passing over flowers takes their scent, the conviction determines the action and the action strengthens the conviction.

Becausethe loop is stated as doctrine and illustrated by births and moods rather than measured, though the half of it that says acting on a belief entrenches the belief is the half that has since been tested.
LimitsGauḍapāda's seed is the position being rejected here, so the tradition does not agree about what persists. A loop with no outside cannot say how the first notion got in, and Vasiṣṭha does not try: the regress is accepted rather than answered.
PracticeManual flying hours — a conviction that you can no longer do something is kept alive by not doing it, so break the loop at the action: one protected hour by hand, for the belief rather than for the output.
After AIDeliberative offloading works this way. Handing over a judgement makes the next handover easier to justify, and the justification is produced by the handover rather than by any assessment of it — no seed, no decision, just the rope turning the wheel.

Vasistha says the doer, the deed and the seen are all mind

Verse 3.103.18 says that doer, deed and instrument, and the whole spread-out thinkable thing with its seer, seeing and seen, are only mind. The world is not made first and perceived after; the perceiving is the making.

Becauseit is asserted and then illustrated by stories rather than argued from anything observable, and the defence offered — that nothing ever happened for the world to be the result of — is what puts it beyond checking.

contestedOn the behaviour of the seeker — verse 2.4.17, with the astrologer argument

Vasistha says what people call fate is their own past effort

Human effort is of two kinds, past and present, and the past is quickly defeated by the present. What people call fate is only the effort of a previous life arriving as consequence: no one has ever seen a fate.

Becausethe case is made from ordinary observation — everyone has seen an action followed by its result, and an astrologer's prediction of scholarship does not spare the young man his studies — and Sapolsky Determined denies exactly the step it turns on.
LimitsSapolsky's objection is that present effort is made of the same antecedents as the fate it is said to defeat, so the division is between causes you remember and causes you do not. The book then contradicts itself: book six tells the reader never to make the least effort to attain what he does not possess.
PracticeAutomation ironies audit — read what you can no longer do as a record of what you stopped doing rather than as a verdict on what you are, and name the hour that would start reversing it.
After AIA skill that decayed since you handed the work over is fate in Vasiṣṭha's exact sense: it presents as a fixed fact about your capacity and is a ledger of what you chose not to do.

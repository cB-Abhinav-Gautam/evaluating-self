13भगवद्गीता · The Bhagavad Gita

Eknath Easwaran, translator, Nilgiri Press, second edition 2007

Linked to 25 chapters: supports 9 · contradicts 5 · mechanism 9 · same claim 12 · pairing 6

The Gita is seven hundred verses of a conversation on a battlefield. Arjuna, the finest archer of his age, has his charioteer drive between the two armies before the battle that will decide his family's claim to a throne, sees his teachers, cousins and friends on the other side, and puts down his bow. The charioteer is Krishna, and the eighteen chapters that follow are Krishna's answer to a man who has decided, on the best of grounds, that he cannot act. Easwaran's translation is the one to read first because it makes a single decision about how to read the whole and holds to it. The battlefield, he says, is a backdrop; the subject of the Gita is the war within. Krishna is not an external god who happens to be driving, but the Self at the core of every person, and the dialogue takes place in the depths of consciousness, between the ordinary self that Arjuna stands for and the deepest Self that Krishna speaks from. He does not offer this as conjecture. Krishna says it in the tenth chapter: I am the Self seated in the heart of every creature. Read that way, the Gita is not a philosophy but a handbook, and, as Easwaran says, a handbook makes most sense when it is practised.

This chapter follows Easwaran's edition as a whole: his long introduction, the short essay that stands before each of the eighteen chapters, signed D.M. in this edition, and the translation itself, read straight through as the reading plan asks. Every chapter of the Gita has its own section below. The verses are given in Sanskrit with a plain rendering, about a hundred of the seven hundred, chosen where the introduction and the essays point and where the argument turns; the renderings are made from the Sanskrit and are meant to be plain rather than elegant, and Easwaran's own translation is the one to read with them.

The setting and the reading

Easwaran places the Gita inside the Mahabharata, the vast epic on which every Hindu child is raised, and dates its war to about 1000 BC. But he argues the Gita is not native to the epic. It is an Upanishad in form and spirit, set down by a seer and inserted into the story, and the evidence is in the text: each chapter ends with the same formula naming it an Upanishad, and its counsel, compassion to friend and enemy alike, is at odds with the slaughter that follows in the epic. That is also his answer to the old question of whether the Gita justifies war. Gandhi's answer, which he adopts, is that anyone who practises the Gita sincerely will find out whether harming others remains compatible with it.

Three obstacles stand between a Western reader and the text, and the introduction clears them in order: Hindu religious ideas, the technical vocabulary of yoga, and the many names of God. The devas of the Vedas are the forces of nature personified; the Rig Veda's line that truth is one and people call it by various names is the licence for reading Krishna as he does. From the Upanishads the Gita takes its foundation. Brahman is the changeless ground beneath the flux of name and form. Atman is the Self, a core of consciousness beyond time and change, found when awareness withdraws to a point where the separate ego disappears, and the Upanishads' claim, "That thou art", is that the two are one. Maya, originally a magician's power to make one thing look like another, is the power by which the one appears as many; the world is not unreal, Easwaran insists, the illusion is the sense of separateness. Dharma, from a root meaning to support, is what holds a thing up, its essential nature and, on the largest scale, the order of the whole. Karma is simply what is done, with the law that every act, mental as well as physical, returns to its doer, which he reads as education rather than punishment. From Sankhya the Gita takes its analytic vocabulary without joining the school: prakriti, the field of everything that can be known, mind included; purusha, the knower, consciousness itself; and the three gunas, tamas, rajas and sattva, inertia, activity and harmony, which he illustrates as ice, a river and harnessed steam, and as the unconscious, the racing ordinary mind and the higher mind. He warns that his English for the twenty-four constituents of Sankhya is approximate and can be skipped.

One more thing about the text's shape is worth having before the chapters. It is a dialogue, and Arjuna does not stop talking after chapter 1. He asks for teaching in chapter 2, objects that the teaching is inconsistent in chapter 3, asks what drives a man to wrong against his will, doubts Krishna's claim to have taught the ancients, asks which of two paths is better, says the mind cannot be held, asks what happens to one who fails, asks for the meaning of six technical words, asks to be shown, asks whether to love the formless or the personal, asks how to recognise one who has gone beyond the strands, asks about faith outside the scriptures, and opens the last chapter with the question he began with. The essays note that he resists throughout and that Krishna adapts rather than abandons him.

Chapter 1: the war within

The first chapter is the bridge from the epic, and the essay calls it a perilous one that need not detain the reader long. It opens on the blind king Dhritarashtra asking his minister what happened, and the whole Gita is the minister's report.

धर्मक्षेत्रे कुरुक्षेत्रे समवेता युयुत्सवः ।
मामकाः पाण्डवाश्चैव किमकुर्वत संजय ॥ १ ॥
dharmakṣetre kurukṣetre samavetā yuyutsavaḥ
māmakāḥ pāṇḍavāścaiva kimakurvata saṃjaya

1.1Dhritarashtra said: On the field of dharma, the field of Kuru, gathered and eager to fight, what did my people and the Pandavas do, Sanjaya?

Easwaran's introduction makes a point of the first word. The Gita opens not on Kurukshetra but on dharmakshetra, the field of dharma, and the reader is told before anything else what kind of field this is. The armies are named, the conches sound, and Arjuna asks to be driven between the lines to see whom he must fight. He sees fathers, teachers, uncles, sons and friends, and the essay observes that Duryodhana has already noted the irony that Drona taught both sides.

गाण्डीवं स्रंसते हस्तात्त्वक्चैव परिदह्यते ।
न च शक्नोम्यवस्थातुं भ्रमतीव च मे मनः ॥ ३० ॥
gāṇḍīvaṃ sraṃsate hastāttvakcaiva paridahyate
na ca śaknomyavasthātuṃ bhramatīva ca me manaḥ

1.30The bow Gandiva slips from my hand, and my skin burns all over. I cannot stand; my mind seems to reel.

एवमुक्त्वार्जुनः संख्ये रथोपस्थ उपाविशत् ।
विसृज्य सशरं चापं शोकसंविग्नमानसः ॥ ४७ ॥
evamuktvārjunaḥ saṃkhye rathopastha upāviśat
visṛjya saśaraṃ cāpaṃ śokasaṃvignamānasaḥ

1.47Sanjaya said: So spoke Arjuna in the midst of the battle, and casting aside his bow and arrows he sank down on the seat of the chariot, his mind overcome with grief.

Arjuna's reasons are good ones: the destruction of families, the loss of tradition, the futility of a kingdom won by killing those it would be enjoyed with. The essay reports the long dispute over whether the Gita condones war and gives Gandhi's position: to read it as a licence for violence is to weigh only its opening verses. Easwaran's own reading is that the war is the one every reader is in.

Chapter 2: the whole in outline

The essay calls the second chapter a kind of overview of the sixteen to come. Krishna's first words are a rebuke; Arjuna's reply is to ask, formally, to be taught, and with that the dialogue becomes instruction.

कार्पण्यदोषोपहतस्वभावः पृच्छामि त्वां धर्मसम्मूढचेताः ।
यच्छ्रेयः स्यान्निश्चितं ब्रूहि तन्मे शिष्यस्तेऽहं शाधि मां त्वां प्रपन्नम् ॥ ७ ॥
kārpaṇyadoṣopahatasvabhāvaḥ pṛcchāmi tvāṃ dharmasammūḍhacetāḥ
yacchreyaḥ syānniścitaṃ brūhi tanme śiṣyaste'haṃ śādhi māṃ tvāṃ prapannam

2.7My nature is overcome by the fault of pity, my mind confused about what is right. I ask you: tell me for certain what is better. I am your disciple; teach me, who have come to you.

Krishna does not begin with a graded path. He begins, the essay says, with the ultimate premise: the Self is never born and never dies.

अशोच्यानन्वशोचस्त्वं प्रज्ञावादांश्च भाषसे ।
गतासूनगतासूंश्च नानुशोचन्ति पण्डिताः ॥ ११ ॥
aśocyānanvaśocastvaṃ prajñāvādāṃśca bhāṣase
gatāsūnagatāsūṃśca nānuśocanti paṇḍitāḥ

2.11You grieve for those who should not be grieved for, and yet you speak words of wisdom. The wise grieve neither for the dead nor for the living.

देहिनोऽस्मिन्यथा देहे कौमारं यौवनं जरा ।
तथा देहान्तरप्राप्तिर्धीरस्तत्र न मुह्यति ॥ १३ ॥
dehino'sminyathā dehe kaumāraṃ yauvanaṃ jarā
tathā dehāntaraprāptirdhīrastatra na muhyati

2.13As the embodied one passes in this body through childhood, youth and age, so it passes into another body. The wise are not confused by this.

मात्रास्पर्शास्तु कौन्तेय शीतोष्णसुखदुःखदाः ।
आगमापायिनोऽनित्यास्तांस्तितिक्षस्व भारत ॥ १४ ॥
mātrāsparśāstu kaunteya śītoṣṇasukhaduḥkhadāḥ
āgamāpāyino'nityāstāṃstitikṣasva bhārata

2.14Contacts of the senses with their objects, Arjuna, give cold and heat, pleasure and pain. They come and go; they do not last. Bear them, Arjuna.

न जायते म्रियते वा कदाचिन्नायं भूत्वा भविता वा न भूयः ।
अजो नित्यः शाश्वतोऽयं पुराणो न हन्यते हन्यमाने शरीरे ॥ २० ॥
na jāyate mriyate vā kadācinnāyaṃ bhūtvā bhavitā vā na bhūyaḥ
ajo nityaḥ śāśvato'yaṃ purāṇo na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre

2.20It is never born and never dies; having been, it will not cease to be. Unborn, eternal, everlasting, ancient, it is not slain when the body is slain.

वासांसि जीर्णानि यथा विहाय नवानि गृह्णाति नरोऽपराणि ।
तथा शरीराणि विहाय जीर्णान्यन्यानि संयाति नवानि देही ॥ २२ ॥
vāsāṃsi jīrṇāni yathā vihāya navāni gṛhṇāti naro'parāṇi
tathā śarīrāṇi vihāya jīrṇānyanyāni saṃyāti navāni dehī

2.22As a man casts off worn-out clothes and takes new ones, so the embodied one casts off worn-out bodies and enters others that are new.

Easwaran's introduction reads 2.22 as saying that for the realised person death is no more traumatic than taking off an old coat, and 2.14 and 2.15 as the reason consciousness must be withdrawn from the pull of the senses: what they deliver does not stay. Then Krishna turns from the eternal to the practical, and announces that the reasoning so far has been Sankhya and what follows is yoga.

सुखदुःखे समे कृत्वा लाभालाभौ जयाजयौ ।
ततो युद्धाय युज्यस्व नैवं पापमवाप्स्यसि ॥ ३८ ॥
sukhaduḥkhe same kṛtvā lābhālābhau jayājayau
tato yuddhāya yujyasva naivaṃ pāpamavāpsyasi

2.38Holding pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat as the same, then gird yourself for battle. So you will incur no wrong.

नेहाभिक्रमनाशोऽस्ति प्रत्यवायो न विद्यते ।
स्वल्पमप्यस्य धर्मस्य त्रायते महतो भयात् ॥ ४० ॥
nehābhikramanāśo'sti pratyavāyo na vidyate
svalpamapyasya dharmasya trāyate mahato bhayāt

2.40Here no effort is lost and no setback is found. Even a little of this practice protects from great fear.

कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन ।
मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि ॥ ४७ ॥
karmaṇyevādhikāraste mā phaleṣu kadācana
mā karmaphalaheturbhūrmā te saṅgo'stvakarmaṇi

2.47Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits. Do not let the fruit of action be your motive; and do not let your attachment be to inaction.

योगस्थः कुरु कर्माणि सङ्गं त्यक्त्वा धनंजय ।
सिद्ध्यसिद्ध्योः समो भूत्वा समत्वं योग उच्यते ॥ ४८ ॥
yogasthaḥ kuru karmāṇi saṅgaṃ tyaktvā dhanaṃjaya
siddhyasiddhyoḥ samo bhūtvā samatvaṃ yoga ucyate

2.48Established in yoga, do your work, Arjuna, having let go of attachment, the same in success and failure. Evenness is called yoga.

The verse has four clauses and Easwaran, following Gandhi, reads all four: the right is to the work; not to its results; the results must not be the motive; and the conclusion must not be to do nothing. Renunciation, a bleak word in English, is renunciation of selfishness, not of things, and not of action. Kama is not desire, which is the fuel of life, but selfish desire. Work done without it is what Gandhi called nishkama karma, and Easwaran's test for it is not what is done but the state of mind in which it is done; philanthropy can fail it and a kitchen can pass. Two verses later Krishna defines yoga a second time.

बुद्धियुक्तो जहातीह उभे सुकृतदुष्कृते ।
तस्माद्योगाय युज्यस्व योगः कर्मसु कौशलम् ॥ ५० ॥
buddhiyukto jahātīha ubhe sukṛtaduṣkṛte
tasmādyogāya yujyasva yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam

2.50One joined to understanding leaves behind, here and now, both good and ill deeds. So yoke yourself to yoga: yoga is skill in action.

Arjuna then asks the question the second chapter is remembered for: what does such a person look like, how does he speak, sit, walk? Krishna's answer, verses 55 to 72, is the first of three portraits of the realised person that Easwaran finds at the beginning, middle and end of the Gita, and the one Gandhi said held the key to the whole.

प्रजहाति यदा कामान्सर्वान्पार्थ मनोगतान् ।
आत्मन्येवात्मना तुष्टः स्थितप्रज्ञस्तदोच्यते ॥ ५५ ॥
prajahāti yadā kāmānsarvānpārtha manogatān
ātmanyevātmanā tuṣṭaḥ sthitaprajñastadocyate

2.55When a man gives up all the desires that enter the mind, Arjuna, content in the Self by the Self alone, he is called one of steady wisdom.

दुःखेष्वनुद्विग्नमनाः सुखेषु विगतस्पृहः ।
वीतरागभयक्रोधः स्थितधीर्मुनिरुच्यते ॥ ५६ ॥
duḥkheṣvanudvignamanāḥ sukheṣu vigataspṛhaḥ
vītarāgabhayakrodhaḥ sthitadhīrmunirucyate

2.56One whose mind is untroubled in sorrows and without craving in pleasures, from whom passion, fear and anger have gone, is called a sage of steady wisdom.

यदा संहरते चायं कूर्मोऽङ्गानीव सर्वशः ।
इन्द्रियाणीन्द्रियार्थेभ्यस्तस्य प्रज्ञा प्रतिष्ठिता ॥ ५८ ॥
yadā saṃharate cāyaṃ kūrmo'ṅgānīva sarvaśaḥ
indriyāṇīndriyārthebhyastasya prajñā pratiṣṭhitā

2.58When he draws his senses in from their objects on every side, as a tortoise draws in its limbs, his wisdom stands firm.

The answer is not a list of virtues. It is the banishing of selfish desire, and everything else in the portrait follows from that. In the middle of it Krishna gives the mechanism of the opposite case, the sequence by which a mind comes apart, and the essay quotes it in full.

ध्यायतो विषयान्पुंसः सङ्गस्तेषूपजायते ।
सङ्गात्संजायते कामः कामात्क्रोधोऽभिजायते ॥ ६२ ॥
dhyāyato viṣayānpuṃsaḥ saṅgasteṣūpajāyate
saṅgātsaṃjāyate kāmaḥ kāmātkrodho'bhijāyate

2.62When a man dwells on objects, attachment to them is born; from attachment, desire; from desire, anger.

क्रोधाद्भवति संमोहः संमोहात्स्मृतिविभ्रमः ।
स्मृतिभ्रंशाद्बुद्धिनाशो बुद्धिनाशात्प्रणश्यति ॥ ६३ ॥
krodhādbhavati saṃmohaḥ saṃmohātsmṛtivibhramaḥ
smṛtibhraṃśādbuddhināśo buddhināśātpraṇaśyati

2.63From anger comes delusion; from delusion, confusion of memory; from confusion of memory, the loss of judgment; when judgment is lost, he is lost.

रागद्वेषवियुक्तैस्तु विषयानिन्द्रियैश्चरन् ।
आत्मवश्यैर्विधेयात्मा प्रसादमधिगच्छति ॥ ६४ ॥
rāgadveṣaviyuktaistu viṣayānindriyaiścaran
ātmavaśyairvidheyātmā prasādamadhigacchati

2.64But one who moves among objects with the senses under his own control, free of attraction and aversion, attains serenity.

आपूर्यमाणमचलप्रतिष्ठं समुद्रमापः प्रविशन्ति यद्वत् ।
तद्वत्कामा यं प्रविशन्ति सर्वे स शान्तिमाप्नोति न कामकामी ॥ ७० ॥
āpūryamāṇamacalapratiṣṭhaṃ samudramāpaḥ praviśanti yadvat
tadvatkāmā yaṃ praviśanti sarve sa śāntimāpnoti na kāmakāmī

2.70As the waters enter the sea, which is being filled and yet stands unmoved, so all desires enter him; he attains peace, not the desirer of desires.

विहाय कामान्यः सर्वान् पुमांश्चरति निःस्पृहः ।
निर्ममो निरहंकारः स शान्तिमधिगच्छति ॥ ७१ ॥
vihāya kāmānyaḥ sarvān pumāṃścarati niḥspṛhaḥ
nirmamo nirahaṃkāraḥ sa śāntimadhigacchati

2.71The man who gives up all desires and moves without longing, without "mine", without "I", attains peace.

एषा ब्राह्मी स्थितिः पार्थ नैनां प्राप्य विमुह्यति ।
स्थित्वास्यामन्तकालेऽपि ब्रह्मनिर्वाणमृच्छति ॥ ७२ ॥
eṣā brāhmī sthitiḥ pārtha naināṃ prāpya vimuhyati
sthitvāsyāmantakāle'pi brahmanirvāṇamṛcchati

2.72This is the state of Brahman, Arjuna. Having reached it, one is not deluded. Standing in it even at the last hour, one attains the extinction that is Brahman.

Chapter 3: selfless service

Arjuna's response to the overview is to change the subject. He does not want the nature of the soul; he wants to know what to do next, and complains that Krishna's words are inconsistent: if knowledge is higher, why fight? Easwaran calls him the faithful representative of you and me. Krishna's answer is that there are two paths, the contemplative and the active, and that neither begins with doing nothing.

न कर्मणामनारम्भान्नैष्कर्म्यं पुरुषोऽश्नुते ।
न च संन्यसनादेव सिद्धिं समधिगच्छति ॥ ४ ॥
na karmaṇāmanārambhānnaiṣkarmyaṃ puruṣo'śnute
na ca saṃnyasanādeva siddhiṃ samadhigacchati

3.4Not by refraining from action does a man reach freedom from action, and not by renunciation alone does he attain perfection.

न हि कश्चित्क्षणमपि जातु तिष्ठत्यकर्मकृत् ।
कार्यते ह्यवशः कर्म सर्वः प्रकृतिजैर्गुणैः ॥ ५ ॥
na hi kaścitkṣaṇamapi jātu tiṣṭhatyakarmakṛt
kāryate hyavaśaḥ karma sarvaḥ prakṛtijairguṇaiḥ

3.5No one ever remains even a moment without acting. Everyone is made to act, helplessly, by the strands born of nature.

नियतं कुरु कर्म त्वं कर्म ज्यायो ह्यकर्मणः ।
शरीरयात्रापि च ते न प्रसिद्ध्येदकर्मणः ॥ ८ ॥
niyataṃ kuru karma tvaṃ karma jyāyo hyakarmaṇaḥ
śarīrayātrāpi ca te na prasiddhyedakarmaṇaḥ

3.8Do the action that is your task, for action is better than inaction. Even the upkeep of your body cannot be managed without action.

Since action is unavoidable, the only question is its spirit, and the chapter's word for the right spirit is yajna, offering. Life turns on a cycle of giving, and one who takes from it without giving back is, in Krishna's word, a thief. The wise act for the holding-together of the world, as Janaka the royal sage did, and as Krishna himself does, having nothing to gain.

तस्मादसक्तः सततं कार्यं कर्म समाचर ।
असक्तो ह्याचरन्कर्म परमाप्नोति पूरुषः ॥ १९ ॥
tasmādasaktaḥ satataṃ kāryaṃ karma samācara
asakto hyācarankarma paramāpnoti pūruṣaḥ

3.19Therefore, unattached, always do the work that must be done; doing work without attachment, a man reaches the highest.

यद्यदाचरति श्रेष्ठस्तत्तदेवेतरो जनः ।
स यत्प्रमाणं कुरुते लोकस्तदनुवर्तते ॥ २१ ॥
yadyadācarati śreṣṭhastattadevetaro janaḥ
sa yatpramāṇaṃ kurute lokastadanuvartate

3.21Whatever the best man does, others do; whatever standard he sets, the world follows.

प्रकृतेः क्रियमाणानि गुणैः कर्माणि सर्वशः ।
अहंकारविमूढात्मा कर्ताहमिति मन्यते ॥ २७ ॥
prakṛteḥ kriyamāṇāni guṇaiḥ karmāṇi sarvaśaḥ
ahaṃkāravimūḍhātmā kartāhamiti manyate

3.27All actions are performed by the strands of nature. One whose self is deluded by the I-maker thinks, "I am the doer."

श्रेयान्स्वधर्मो विगुणः परधर्मात्स्वनुष्ठितात् ।
स्वधर्मे निधनं श्रेयः परधर्मो भयावहः ॥ ३५ ॥
śreyānsvadharmo viguṇaḥ paradharmātsvanuṣṭhitāt
svadharme nidhanaṃ śreyaḥ paradharmo bhayāvahaḥ

3.35Better one's own duty done imperfectly than another's done well. Better to die in one's own duty; another's duty brings fear.

Arjuna asks what force drives a man to wrong even against his will, as if compelled. The answer is the chapter's other half.

काम एष क्रोध एष रजोगुणसमुद्भवः ।
महाशनो महापाप्मा विद्ध्येनमिह वैरिणम् ॥ ३७ ॥
kāma eṣa krodha eṣa rajoguṇasamudbhavaḥ
mahāśano mahāpāpmā viddhyenamiha vairiṇam

3.37It is desire, it is anger, born of the strand of passion, all-devouring and greatly harmful. Know this to be the enemy here.

इन्द्रियाणि पराण्याहुरिन्द्रियेभ्यः परं मनः ।
मनसस्तु परा बुद्धिर्यो बुद्धेः परतस्तु सः ॥ ४२ ॥
indriyāṇi parāṇyāhurindriyebhyaḥ paraṃ manaḥ
manasastu parā buddhiryo buddheḥ paratastu saḥ

3.42The senses, they say, are high; higher than the senses is the mind; higher than the mind is the intellect; and what is higher than the intellect is he.

Selfish desire, the text says, hides knowledge as smoke hides fire and dust a mirror, and lodges in the senses, the mind and the intellect. The ladder of verse 42 is the strategy: know what is highest, and from there subdue the self by the Self.

Chapter 4: wisdom in action

Krishna says he taught this yoga at the beginning of time, and Arjuna, still stuck on his predicament, objects that Krishna was born long after the sages he claims to have taught. The reply is the doctrine of rebirth and of the avatar: Krishna remembers all his births, and whenever dharma declines he takes a form. The essay makes the move on which Easwaran's edition rests: when Krishna speaks of himself, he is often describing the Self in every person.

यदा यदा हि धर्मस्य ग्लानिर्भवति भारत ।
अभ्युत्थानमधर्मस्य तदात्मानं सृजाम्यहम् ॥ ७ ॥
yadā yadā hi dharmasya glānirbhavati bhārata
abhyutthānamadharmasya tadātmānaṃ sṛjāmyaham

4.7Whenever there is a decline of dharma, Arjuna, and a rise of its opposite, then I send myself forth.

ये यथा मां प्रपद्यन्ते तांस्तथैव भजाम्यहम् ।
मम वर्त्मानुवर्तन्ते मनुष्याः पार्थ सर्वशः ॥ ११ ॥
ye yathā māṃ prapadyante tāṃstathaiva bhajāmyaham
mama vartmānuvartante manuṣyāḥ pārtha sarvaśaḥ

4.11As they come to me, so I receive them. People follow my path in every way, Arjuna.

Verse 11 is the one the introduction cites for the compassionate view of evolution: every path is a path to the same place. Then the chapter's central riddle.

कर्मण्यकर्म यः पश्येदकर्मणि च कर्म यः ।
स बुद्धिमान्मनुष्येषु स युक्तः कृत्स्नकर्मकृत् ॥ १८ ॥
karmaṇyakarma yaḥ paśyedakarmaṇi ca karma yaḥ
sa buddhimānmanuṣyeṣu sa yuktaḥ kṛtsnakarmakṛt

4.18One who sees inaction in action and action in inaction is wise among men; he is in yoga, and has done all that is to be done.

Easwaran reads it with the mystics. It is action of the most tireless kind; the only thing inactive is the ego. The rest of the chapter is a survey of offerings, of wealth, of the senses, of breath, of study, all of them born of work and all leading to the same knowledge, and a statement of how that knowledge is got.

तद्विद्धि प्रणिपातेन परिप्रश्नेन सेवया ।
उपदेक्ष्यन्ति ते ज्ञानं ज्ञानिनस्तत्त्वदर्शिनः ॥ ३४ ॥
tadviddhi praṇipātena paripraśnena sevayā
upadekṣyanti te jñānaṃ jñāninastattvadarśinaḥ

4.34Learn it by prostration, by questioning, by service. The knowers who see the truth will teach you knowledge.

यथैधांसि समिद्धोऽग्निर्भस्मसात्कुरुतेऽर्जुन ।
ज्ञानाग्निः सर्वकर्माणि भस्मसात्कुरुते तथा ॥ ३७ ॥
yathaidhāṃsi samiddho'gnirbhasmasātkurute'rjuna
jñānāgniḥ sarvakarmāṇi bhasmasātkurute tathā

4.37As a kindled fire reduces its fuel to ashes, Arjuna, so the fire of knowledge reduces all actions to ashes.

Wisdom, the essay concludes, is the fruit of action: knowing comes from doing. The chapter closes with Krishna telling Arjuna to cut the doubt in his heart with the sword of knowledge and stand up.

Chapter 5: renounce and rejoice

Arjuna asks the obvious question: Krishna has praised both giving up action and doing it selflessly, so which is better? The essay contrasts leaving the world, with the Buddha as the classic case, and working in it unattached.

संन्यासः कर्मयोगश्च निःश्रेयसकरावुभौ ।
तयोस्तु कर्मसंन्यासात्कर्मयोगो विशिष्यते ॥ २ ॥
saṃnyāsaḥ karmayogaśca niḥśreyasakarāvubhau
tayostu karmasaṃnyāsātkarmayogo viśiṣyate

5.2Both renunciation and the yoga of action lead to the highest good. But of the two, the yoga of action is better than the renunciation of action.

नैव किंचित्करोमीति युक्तो मन्येत तत्त्ववित् ।
पश्यञ्श्रृण्वन्स्पृशञ्जिघ्रन्नश्नन्‌गच्छन्स्वपञ्श्वसन् ॥ ८ ॥
naiva kiṃcitkaromīti yukto manyeta tattvavit
paśyañśrṛṇvanspṛśañjighrannaśnangacchansvapañśvasan

5.8"I do nothing at all": so thinks the man of yoga who knows the truth, while seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, eating, walking, sleeping, breathing.

ब्रह्मण्याधाय कर्माणि सङ्गं त्यक्त्वा करोति यः ।
लिप्यते न स पापेन पद्मपत्रमिवाम्भसा ॥ १० ॥
brahmaṇyādhāya karmāṇi saṅgaṃ tyaktvā karoti yaḥ
lipyate na sa pāpena padmapatramivāmbhasā

5.10One who acts having placed his actions in Brahman and let go of attachment is not stained by evil, as a lotus leaf is not stained by water.

Only the immature, Krishna says, think knowledge and action are different; the two goals are one. The image for why selfless work cannot stain is the lotus leaf, dry in the water. Then the portrait of equal vision.

विद्याविनयसंपन्ने ब्राह्मणे गवि हस्तिनि ।
शुनि चैव श्वपाके च पण्डिताः समदर्शिनः ॥ १८ ॥
vidyāvinayasaṃpanne brāhmaṇe gavi hastini
śuni caiva śvapāke ca paṇḍitāḥ samadarśinaḥ

5.18In a brahmin endowed with learning and humility, in a cow, an elephant, a dog and an eater of dogs, the wise see the same.

यतेन्द्रियमनोबुद्धिर्मुनिर्मोक्षपरायणः ।
विगतेच्छाभयक्रोधो यः सदा मुक्त एव सः ॥ २८ ॥
yatendriyamanobuddhirmunirmokṣaparāyaṇaḥ
vigatecchābhayakrodho yaḥ sadā mukta eva saḥ

5.28With senses, mind and intellect controlled, intent on release, with desire, fear and anger gone, the sage is freed for ever.

The essay notes the word sada, always, in verse 28: what was a state to be entered has become permanent, which is what the Gita means by freedom.

Chapter 6: the practice of meditation

The essay calls this the most intriguing chapter, because meditation is explained plainly to the layperson, without the technical apparatus of Patanjali. Krishna first disposes of the yogi on his bed of nails: the true renouncer is the one who works without expecting reward. Then the principle Easwaran names as the centre of yoga.

उद्धरेदात्मनात्मानं नात्मानमवसादयेत् ।
आत्मैव ह्यात्मनो बन्धुरात्मैव रिपुरात्मनः ॥ ५ ॥
uddharedātmanātmānaṃ nātmānamavasādayet
ātmaiva hyātmano bandhurātmaiva ripurātmanaḥ

6.5Let a man raise himself by himself; let him not lower himself. The self alone is the self's friend; the self alone is the self's enemy.

बन्धुरात्मात्मनस्तस्य येनात्मैवात्मना जितः ।
अनात्मनस्तु शत्रुत्वे वर्तेतात्मैव शत्रुवत् ॥ ६ ॥
bandhurātmātmanastasya yenātmaivātmanā jitaḥ
anātmanastu śatrutve vartetātmaiva śatruvat

6.6For one who has conquered himself by himself, the self is a friend; for one who has not, the self remains hostile, like an enemy.

The essay points out that the verse plays on the one word atman, self, throughout, and Easwaran renders the first half as reshaping oneself through the will. Then the method: a clean place, a firm seat, the body straight, the gaze steady, and moderation in everything.

युक्ताहारविहारस्य युक्तचेष्टस्य कर्मसु ।
युक्तस्वप्नावबोधस्य योगो भवति दुःखहा ॥ १७ ॥
yuktāhāravihārasya yuktaceṣṭasya karmasu
yuktasvapnāvabodhasya yogo bhavati duḥkhahā

6.17For one moderate in food and recreation, moderate in effort in his actions, moderate in sleep and waking, yoga becomes the destroyer of sorrow.

यथा दीपो निवातस्थो नेङ्गते सोपमा स्मृता ।
योगिनो यतचित्तस्य युञ्जतो योगमात्मनः ॥ १९ ॥
yathā dīpo nivātastho neṅgate sopamā smṛtā
yogino yatacittasya yuñjato yogamātmanaḥ

6.19As a lamp in a windless place does not flicker: that is the likeness given for the yogi whose mind is controlled, practising the yoga of the self.

यत्रोपरमते चित्तं निरुद्धं योगसेवया ।
यत्र चैवात्मनात्मानं पश्यन्नात्मनि तुष्यति ॥ २० ॥
yatroparamate cittaṃ niruddhaṃ yogasevayā
yatra caivātmanātmānaṃ paśyannātmani tuṣyati

6.20Where the mind, restrained by the practice of yoga, comes to rest; where, seeing the Self by the self, one is content in the Self;

सर्वभूतस्थमात्मानं सर्वभूतानि चात्मनि ।
ईक्षते योगयुक्तात्मा सर्वत्र समदर्शनः ॥ २९ ॥
sarvabhūtasthamātmānaṃ sarvabhūtāni cātmani
īkṣate yogayuktātmā sarvatra samadarśanaḥ

6.29One whose self is joined in yoga, who sees the same everywhere, sees the Self in all beings and all beings in the Self.

आत्मौपम्येन सर्वत्र समं पश्यति योऽर्जुन ।
सुखं वा यदि वा दुःखं स योगी परमो मतः ॥ ३२ ॥
ātmaupamyena sarvatra samaṃ paśyati yo'rjuna
sukhaṃ vā yadi vā duḥkhaṃ sa yogī paramo mataḥ

6.32One who sees everywhere the same, by likeness to himself, Arjuna, whether in pleasure or in pain, is held the highest yogi.

The lamp of verse 19 is the image that later texts, the Dṛg-Dṛśya-Viveka among them, take from here. Verse 32, responding to another's joy and sorrow as one's own, is for Easwaran the highest state of spiritual union and one of his two answers to the reading of the Gita as a licence for war. Arjuna interrupts twice.

चञ्चलं हि मनः कृष्ण प्रमाथि बलवद्‌दृढम् ।
तस्याहं निग्रहं मन्ये वायोरिव सुदुष्करम् ॥ ३४ ॥
cañcalaṃ hi manaḥ kṛṣṇa pramāthi balavaddṛḍham
tasyāhaṃ nigrahaṃ manye vāyoriva suduṣkaram

6.34For the mind is restless, Krishna, turbulent, strong and obstinate. To hold it, I think, is as hard as holding the wind.

असंशयं महाबाहो मनो दुर्निग्रहं चलम् ।
अभ्यासेन तु कौन्तेय वैराग्येण च गृह्यते ॥ ३५ ॥
asaṃśayaṃ mahābāho mano durnigrahaṃ calam
abhyāsena tu kaunteya vairāgyeṇa ca gṛhyate

6.35Without doubt, Arjuna, the mind is hard to hold and restless. But by practice and by dispassion it is held.

पार्थ नैवेह नामुत्र विनाशस्तस्य विद्यते ।
न हि कल्याणकृत्कश्चिद्‌दुर्गतिं तात गच्छति ॥ ४० ॥
pārtha naiveha nāmutra vināśastasya vidyate
na hi kalyāṇakṛtkaściddurgatiṃ tāta gacchati

6.40Arjuna, neither here nor hereafter is there destruction for him. No one who does good, my friend, comes to a bad end.

The second interruption is the fear of failing halfway and losing both worlds, like a cloud torn apart in the sky. The answer is that no effort is lost: the one who falls short is born again where the practice can resume, and is carried on by the strength of what was done before.

Chapter 7: wisdom from realisation

The middle six chapters turn from the seeker to the sought, and the essays are frank that they are the hardest for a Western reader. The seventh opens with the distinction its title carries: jnana, knowledge, and vijnana, realisation, which the essay illustrates with Ramakrishna's comparison of one who has read about fire and one who has cooked on it.

भूमिरापोऽनलो वायुः खं मनो बुद्धिरेव च ।
अहंकार इतीयं मे भिन्ना प्रकृतिरष्टधा ॥ ४ ॥
bhūmirāpo'nalo vāyuḥ khaṃ mano buddhireva ca
ahaṃkāra itīyaṃ me bhinnā prakṛtiraṣṭadhā

7.4Earth, water, fire, air, ether, mind, intellect and the sense of "I": this is my nature, divided eightfold.

अपरेयमितस्त्वन्यां प्रकृतिं विद्धि मे पराम् ।
जीवभूतां महाबाहो ययेदं धार्यते जगत् ॥ ५ ॥
apareyamitastvanyāṃ prakṛtiṃ viddhi me parām
jīvabhūtāṃ mahābāho yayedaṃ dhāryate jagat

7.5That is the lower. Know my other, higher nature, Arjuna, the living self by which this world is upheld.

मत्तः परतरं नान्यत्किंचिदस्ति धनंजय ।
मयि सर्वमिदं प्रोतं सूत्रे मणिगणा इव ॥ ७ ॥
mattaḥ parataraṃ nānyatkiṃcidasti dhanaṃjaya
mayi sarvamidaṃ protaṃ sūtre maṇigaṇā iva

7.7There is nothing higher than me, Arjuna. All this is strung on me like pearls on a thread.

Krishna's two natures are Sankhya's two categories in the first person: prakriti, here listed in eight parts, and the knower. What follows is a litany of immanence, the taste in water, the light in sun and moon, the life in all beings, the strength in the strong, and desire itself where it does not conflict with dharma. The gunas come from him and he is not in them; they are his maya, hard to cross, and crossed by those who take refuge in him.

चतुर्विधा भजन्ते मां जनाः सुकृतिनोऽर्जुन ।
आर्तो जिज्ञासुरर्थार्थी ज्ञानी च भरतर्षभ ॥ १६ ॥
caturvidhā bhajante māṃ janāḥ sukṛtino'rjuna
ārto jijñāsurarthārthī jñānī ca bharatarṣabha

7.16Four kinds of people who do good worship me, Arjuna: the one in distress, the one who seeks knowledge, the one who seeks some good, and the one who knows.

बहूनां जन्मनामन्ते ज्ञानवान्मां प्रपद्यते ।
वासुदेवः सर्वमिति स महात्मा सुदुर्लभः ॥ १९ ॥
bahūnāṃ janmanāmante jñānavānmāṃ prapadyate
vāsudevaḥ sarvamiti sa mahātmā sudurlabhaḥ

7.19At the end of many births the man of knowledge comes to me, seeing that all this is Vasudeva. Such a great soul is very hard to find.

इच्छाद्वेषसमुत्थेन द्वन्द्वमोहेन भारत ।
सर्वभूतानि संमोहं सर्गे यान्ति परन्तप ॥ २७ ॥
icchādveṣasamutthena dvandvamohena bhārata
sarvabhūtāni saṃmohaṃ sarge yānti parantapa

7.27By the delusion of the pairs, Arjuna, which arises from desire and aversion, all beings at birth go into confusion.

The essay glosses moha, the confusion of verse 27, as dreaming while awake, and notes that the delusion begins at birth, as a swoon one has to be woken from. The chapter's other verse the introduction singles out is 7.21, that whatever form a person worships with faith, Krishna makes that faith steady, which Easwaran calls the most compassionate insight into human evolution ever expressed.

Chapter 8: the eternal Godhead

Arjuna asks the meaning of six technical words from the end of the last chapter, and the real question underneath, how Krishna is to be known at the hour of death. The essay is the longest of the eighteen and the most technical, and it is candid that the anatomy of dying it contains is for those who can interpret it.

अन्तकाले च मामेव स्मरन्मुक्त्वा कलेवरम् ।
यः प्रयाति स मद्भावं याति नास्त्यत्र संशयः ॥ ५ ॥
antakāle ca māmeva smaranmuktvā kalevaram
yaḥ prayāti sa madbhāvaṃ yāti nāstyatra saṃśayaḥ

8.5And one who at the end of life goes forth leaving the body remembering me alone, comes to my being. Of this there is no doubt.

यं यं वापि स्मरन्भावं त्यजत्यन्ते कलेवरम् ।
तं तमेवैति कौन्तेय सदा तद्भावभावितः ॥ ६ ॥
yaṃ yaṃ vāpi smaranbhāvaṃ tyajatyante kalevaram
taṃ tamevaiti kaunteya sadā tadbhāvabhāvitaḥ

8.6Whatever state of being a man remembers when at the end he leaves the body, to that very state he goes, Arjuna, always formed by it.

तस्मात्सर्वेषु कालेषु मामनुस्मर युध्य च ।
मय्यर्पितमनोबुद्धिर्मामेवैष्यस्यसंशयम् ॥ ७ ॥
tasmātsarveṣu kāleṣu māmanusmara yudhya ca
mayyarpitamanobuddhirmāmevaiṣyasyasaṃśayam

8.7Therefore remember me at all times, and fight. With mind and understanding set on me, you will come to me without doubt.

The teaching is that what fills the mind at death directs where it goes, and the practical conclusion is that one should practise remembering now, since the last thought is the sum of all the others. The introduction cites 8.7 for its combination: remember, and fight. Then the instructions for the yogi at the moment itself.

सर्वद्वाराणि संयम्य मनो हृदि निरुध्य च ।
मूर्ध्न्याधायात्मनः प्राणमास्थितो योगधारणाम् ॥ १२ ॥
sarvadvārāṇi saṃyamya mano hṛdi nirudhya ca
mūrdhnyādhāyātmanaḥ prāṇamāsthito yogadhāraṇām

8.12Closing all the gates, confining the mind in the heart, placing his own breath in the head, established in the concentration of yoga,

परस्तस्मात्तु भावोऽन्योऽव्यक्तोऽव्यक्तात्सनातनः ।
यः स सर्वेषु भूतेषु नश्यत्सु न विनश्यति ॥ २० ॥
parastasmāttu bhāvo'nyo'vyakto'vyaktātsanātanaḥ
yaḥ sa sarveṣu bhūteṣu naśyatsu na vinaśyati

8.20But beyond that unmanifest there is another, eternal unmanifest being, which does not perish when all beings perish.

The chapter gives the days and nights of Brahma, each a thousand ages long, in which all forms come forth and are withdrawn, and beyond that cycle the unmanifest that does not dissolve, which is Krishna's home. It ends with the two paths after death, of light and of smoke, the northern and the southern course of the sun, one to liberation and one to return.

Chapter 9: the royal path

Krishna announces the most secret knowledge, royal and easy to practise, and the essay's summary is that all worship, even of lesser gods and even for gain, is worship of Krishna unknowing, and that the way back is always open.

मया ततमिदं सर्वं जगदव्यक्तमूर्तिना ।
मत्स्थानि सर्वभूतानि न चाहं तेष्ववस्थितः ॥ ४ ॥
mayā tatamidaṃ sarvaṃ jagadavyaktamūrtinā
matsthāni sarvabhūtāni na cāhaṃ teṣvavasthitaḥ

9.4By me, in my unmanifest form, all this world is pervaded. All beings rest in me, and I do not rest in them.

गतिर्भर्ता प्रभुः साक्षी निवासः शरणं सुहृत् ।
प्रभवः प्रलयः स्थानं निधानं बीजमव्ययम् ॥ १८ ॥
gatirbhartā prabhuḥ sākṣī nivāsaḥ śaraṇaṃ suhṛt
prabhavaḥ pralayaḥ sthānaṃ nidhānaṃ bījamavyayam

9.18I am the goal, the sustainer, the lord, the witness, the abode, the refuge, the friend; the origin, the dissolution, the ground, the resting place, the imperishable seed.

अनन्याश्चिन्तयन्तो मां ये जनाः पर्युपासते ।
तेषां नित्याभियुक्तानां योगक्षेमं वहाम्यहम् ॥ २२ ॥
ananyāścintayanto māṃ ye janāḥ paryupāsate
teṣāṃ nityābhiyuktānāṃ yogakṣemaṃ vahāmyaham

9.22To those who worship me thinking of nothing else, always joined in yoga, I bring what they lack and preserve what they have.

Those who follow the Vedic rituals for reward, Krishna says, reach heaven and, when their merit is spent, return; those who worship other gods worship him without knowing it and go where their worship takes them. The chapter's practical verses are the ones the introduction calls the refrain of the Gita.

पत्रं पुष्पं फलं तोयं यो मे भक्त्या प्रयच्छति ।
तदहं भक्त्युपहृतमश्नामि प्रयतात्मनः ॥ २६ ॥
patraṃ puṣpaṃ phalaṃ toyaṃ yo me bhaktyā prayacchati
tadahaṃ bhaktyupahṛtamaśnāmi prayatātmanaḥ

9.26Whoever offers me with devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water, that offering of devotion from a pure heart I accept.

यत्करोषि यदश्नासि यज्जुहोषि ददासि यत् ।
यत्तपस्यसि कौन्तेय तत्कुरुष्व मदर्पणम् ॥ २७ ॥
yatkaroṣi yadaśnāsi yajjuhoṣi dadāsi yat
yattapasyasi kaunteya tatkuruṣva madarpaṇam

9.27Whatever you do, whatever you eat, whatever you offer, whatever you give, whatever austerity you practise, Arjuna, do it as an offering to me.

समोऽहं सर्वभूतेषु न मे द्वेष्योऽस्ति न प्रियः ।
ये भजन्ति तु मां भक्त्या मयि ते तेषु चाप्यहम् ॥ २९ ॥
samo'haṃ sarvabhūteṣu na me dveṣyo'sti na priyaḥ
ye bhajanti tu māṃ bhaktyā mayi te teṣu cāpyaham

9.29I am the same to all beings; none is hateful to me and none dear. But those who worship me with devotion are in me, and I in them.

मन्मना भव मद्भक्तो मद्याजी मां नमस्कुरु ।
मामेवैष्यसि युक्त्वैवमात्मानं मत्परायणः ॥ ३४ ॥
manmanā bhava madbhakto madyājī māṃ namaskuru
māmevaiṣyasi yuktvaivamātmānaṃ matparāyaṇaḥ

9.34Fix your mind on me, be devoted to me, offer to me, bow to me. Having joined yourself to me thus, with me as your goal, you will come to me.

The essay pauses on the word sudurachara in verse 30, the one of very bad conduct who is nevertheless to be counted good if he turns wholly to Krishna, and on the verse after it, that no one devoted to him is lost. Verse 34 is repeated almost word for word at the end of chapter 18, and Easwaran sets it beside the commandments of Moses, Jesus and Mohammed.

Chapter 10: divine splendour

Krishna is the source of gods and sages alike, and the qualities of creatures, understanding, forgiveness, fear, courage, fame, come from him. Arjuna, no longer a warrior but a seeker, asks how to meditate on him and in what things he is to be found.

अहं सर्वस्य प्रभवो मत्तः सर्वं प्रवर्तते ।
इति मत्वा भजन्ते मां बुधा भावसमन्विताः ॥ ८ ॥
ahaṃ sarvasya prabhavo mattaḥ sarvaṃ pravartate
iti matvā bhajante māṃ budhā bhāvasamanvitāḥ

10.8I am the origin of all; from me everything proceeds. Knowing this, the wise worship me, filled with feeling.

तेषां सततयुक्तानां भजतां प्रीतिपूर्वकम् ।
ददामि बुद्धियोगं तं येन मामुपयान्ति ते ॥ १० ॥
teṣāṃ satatayuktānāṃ bhajatāṃ prītipūrvakam
dadāmi buddhiyogaṃ taṃ yena māmupayānti te

10.10To those who are always joined to me and worship me with love, I give the yoga of understanding by which they come to me.

अहमात्मा गुडाकेश सर्वभूताशयस्थितः ।
अहमादिश्च मध्यं च भूतानामन्त एव च ॥ २० ॥
ahamātmā guḍākeśa sarvabhūtāśayasthitaḥ
ahamādiśca madhyaṃ ca bhūtānāmanta eva ca

10.20I am the Self, Arjuna, seated in the heart of every creature; I am the beginning, the middle and the end of beings.

The list of manifestations that follows, Vishnu among gods, the sun among lights, the mind among senses, Om among words, the lion, the Ganges, time among the things that measure, the essay admits is hard going for the names. It notes that the Self is listed first, and that this is fitting, since the Self is the form in which Arjuna is being asked to meditate. It is the verse on which Easwaran's reading of the whole Gita depends. Krishna ends the list by dismissing it.

यद्यद्विभूतिमत्सत्त्वं श्रीमदूर्जितमेव वा ।
तत्तदेवावगच्छ त्वं मम तेजोंऽशसंभवम् ॥ ४१ ॥
yadyadvibhūtimatsattvaṃ śrīmadūrjitameva vā
tattadevāvagaccha tvaṃ mama tejoṃ'śasaṃbhavam

10.41Whatever being is glorious, prosperous or strong, know that it springs from a fragment of my splendour.

अथवा बहुनैतेन किं ज्ञातेन तवार्जुन ।
विष्टभ्याहमिदं कृत्स्नमेकांशेन स्थितो जगत् ॥ ४२ ॥
athavā bahunaitena kiṃ jñātena tavārjuna
viṣṭabhyāhamidaṃ kṛtsnamekāṃśena sthito jagat

10.42But what is the use to you, Arjuna, of knowing all this? I stand supporting this whole world with a single fragment of myself.

Chapter 11: the cosmic vision

Arjuna asks to see, and the essay calls this the most exalted chapter of the Gita. It explains why the vision comes now: Arjuna has been led step by step, and in the last chapter asked for the first time how to contemplate. What he is given he cannot see with his own eyes.

न तु मां शक्यसे द्रष्टुमनेनैव स्वचक्षुषा ।
दिव्यं ददामि ते चक्षुः पश्य मे योगमैश्वरम् ॥ ८ ॥
na tu māṃ śakyase draṣṭumanenaiva svacakṣuṣā
divyaṃ dadāmi te cakṣuḥ paśya me yogamaiśvaram

11.8But you cannot see me with this eye of yours. I give you a divine eye: behold my sovereign yoga.

दिवि सूर्यसहस्रस्य भवेद्युगपदुत्थिता ।
यदि भाः सदृशी सा स्याद्भासस्तस्य महात्मनः ॥ १२ ॥
divi sūryasahasrasya bhavedyugapadutthitā
yadi bhāḥ sadṛśī sā syādbhāsastasya mahātmanaḥ

11.12If the light of a thousand suns were to rise at once in the sky, that would be like the light of that great being.

Arjuna sees all beings in one form, without beginning, middle or end, and then sees the form's mouths and the warriors of both armies rushing into them like rivers into the sea and moths into a flame. He asks who this is, and the answer is the verse that came to Oppenheimer at the first nuclear test.

येऽवस्थिताः प्रत्यनीकेषु योधाः ॥ ३२ ॥
ye'vasthitāḥ pratyanīkeṣu yodhāḥ

11.32I am time, grown old, the destroyer of worlds, come forth here to consume the worlds. Even without you, none of the warriors drawn up in the opposing ranks will survive.

निमित्तमात्रं भव सव्यसाचिन् ॥ ३३ ॥
nimittamātraṃ bhava savyasācin

11.33Therefore stand up and win glory; conquer your enemies and enjoy a prosperous kingdom. They have already been slain by me. Be merely the instrument, Arjuna.

The essay notes that the word Oppenheimer rendered as death, kala, means time, and reads the chapter as Arjuna's samadhi, first with form, in which he sees God personally, and then without, in which forms dissolve into consuming fire. It also notes Arjuna's courage: he does not break down, but asks for the human face again, and is given it. The chapter ends with how such a vision is to be had, and it is not by study.

भक्त्या त्वनन्यया शक्य अहमेवंविधोऽर्जुन ।
ज्ञातुं द्रष्टुं च तत्त्वेन प्रवेष्टुं च परंतप ॥ ५४ ॥
bhaktyā tvananyayā śakya ahamevaṃvidho'rjuna
jñātuṃ draṣṭuṃ ca tattvena praveṣṭuṃ ca paraṃtapa

11.54But by undivided devotion, Arjuna, I can be known in this way, seen in truth, and entered.

मत्कर्मकृन्मत्परमो मद्भक्तः सङ्गवर्जितः ।
निर्वैरः सर्वभूतेषु यः स मामेति पाण्डव ॥ ५५ ॥
matkarmakṛnmatparamo madbhaktaḥ saṅgavarjitaḥ
nirvairaḥ sarvabhūteṣu yaḥ sa māmeti pāṇḍava

11.55One who does my work, holds me highest, is devoted to me, free of attachment and without enmity toward any being, comes to me, Arjuna.

Chapter 12: the way of love

Arjuna asks a plain question, whether it is better to worship the formless or the personal God, and the essay treats the answer as the Gita's departure from the Upanishads: the formless is real and reachable, but for beings with bodies the way to it is slow and hard, and love of a personal God is vastly easier.

मय्यावेश्य मनो ये मां नित्ययुक्ता उपासते ।
श्रद्धया परयोपेतास्ते मे युक्ततमा मताः ॥ २ ॥
mayyāveśya mano ye māṃ nityayuktā upāsate
śraddhayā parayopetāste me yuktatamā matāḥ

12.2Those who fix their mind on me and, always joined in yoga, worship me with supreme faith, I hold to be the most accomplished in yoga.

क्लेशोऽधिकतरस्तेषामव्यक्तासक्तचेतसाम् ।
अव्यक्ता हि गतिर्दुःखं देहवद्भिरवाप्यते ॥ ५ ॥
kleśo'dhikatarasteṣāmavyaktāsaktacetasām
avyaktā hi gatirduḥkhaṃ dehavadbhiravāpyate

12.5Greater is the toil of those whose minds are set on the unmanifest; for the unmanifest goal is hard for the embodied to reach.

Then a descending ladder of practice for those who cannot manage the first step, which the essay singles out as the chapter's practical core.

मय्येव मन आधत्स्व मयि बुद्धिं निवेशय ।
निवसिष्यसि मय्येव अत ऊर्ध्वं न संशयः ॥ ८ ॥
mayyeva mana ādhatsva mayi buddhiṃ niveśaya
nivasiṣyasi mayyeva ata ūrdhvaṃ na saṃśayaḥ

12.8Fix your mind on me alone, let your understanding enter me; you will dwell in me hereafter, there is no doubt.

अथ चित्तं समाधातुं न शक्नोषि मयि स्थिरम् ।
अभ्यासयोगेन ततो मामिच्छाप्तुं धनंजय ॥ ९ ॥
atha cittaṃ samādhātuṃ na śaknoṣi mayi sthiram
abhyāsayogena tato māmicchāptuṃ dhanaṃjaya

12.9But if you cannot hold your mind steadily on me, then seek to reach me by the practice of yoga, Arjuna.

अभ्यासेऽप्यसमर्थोऽसि मत्कर्मपरमो भव ।
मदर्थमपि कर्माणि कुर्वन्सिद्धिमवाप्स्यसि ॥ १० ॥
abhyāse'pyasamartho'si matkarmaparamo bhava
madarthamapi karmāṇi kurvansiddhimavāpsyasi

12.10If you are unable even to practise, be intent on doing work for me; even doing actions for my sake, you will reach perfection.

अथैतदप्यशक्तोऽसि कर्तुं मद्योगमाश्रितः ।
सर्वकर्मफलत्यागं ततः कुरु यतात्मवान् ॥ ११ ॥
athaitadapyaśakto'si kartuṃ madyogamāśritaḥ
sarvakarmaphalatyāgaṃ tataḥ kuru yatātmavān

12.11If you are unable to do even this, then, resorting to my yoga, give up the fruit of all action, with the self restrained.

श्रेयो हि ज्ञानमभ्यासाज्ज्ञानाद्ध्यानं विशिष्यते ।
ध्यानात्कर्मफलत्यागस्त्यागाच्छान्तिरनन्तरम् ॥ १२ ॥
śreyo hi jñānamabhyāsājjñānāddhyānaṃ viśiṣyate
dhyānātkarmaphalatyāgastyāgācchāntiranantaram

12.12Better than practice is knowledge; better than knowledge is meditation; better than meditation is giving up the fruit of action, for from that comes peace at once.

The essay notes the apparent contradiction between the ladder, which puts surrender of results last, and verse 12, which puts it highest, and resolves it as Easwaran does: the last resort is also the one that works. Then the second of the three portraits, the marks of the one Krishna calls dear.

अद्वेष्टा सर्वभूतानां मैत्रः करुण एव च ।
निर्ममो निरहंकारः समदुःखसुखः क्षमी ॥ १३ ॥
adveṣṭā sarvabhūtānāṃ maitraḥ karuṇa eva ca
nirmamo nirahaṃkāraḥ samaduḥkhasukhaḥ kṣamī

12.13Hating no creature, friendly and compassionate, without "mine", without "I", the same in pain and pleasure, patient,

संतुष्टः सततं योगी यतात्मा दृढनिश्चयः ।
मय्यर्पितमनोबुद्धिर्यो मद्भक्तः स मे प्रियः ॥ १४ ॥
saṃtuṣṭaḥ satataṃ yogī yatātmā dṛḍhaniścayaḥ
mayyarpitamanobuddhiryo madbhaktaḥ sa me priyaḥ

12.14content always, a yogi, self-controlled, of firm resolve, with mind and understanding given to me: such a devotee of mine is dear to me.

ये तु धर्म्यामृतमिदं यथोक्तं पर्युपासते ।
श्रद्दधाना मत्परमा भक्तास्तेऽतीव मे प्रियाः ॥ २० ॥
ye tu dharmyāmṛtamidaṃ yathoktaṃ paryupāsate
śraddadhānā matparamā bhaktāste'tīva me priyāḥ

12.20But those who honour this immortal dharma as I have told it, with faith, holding me highest, those devotees are exceedingly dear to me.

The essay sets verses 13 to 20 beside the Cloud of Unknowing and St John of the Cross, and quotes Dionysius on the God who can be gotten by love and by thought never. Its practical claim is that love of this kind is not a temperament some people are issued with at birth. The scriptures let God be loved as a father, a mother, a friend, a beloved or a child, and where the devotion is not forthcoming, as it is not in Arjuna, it is to be cultivated by the same regular practice that steadies anything else.

Chapter 13: the field and the knower

The chapter gives the Gita's two sweeping categories, and the essay likes the modern ring of the first word: a field of physical and mental forces, matter and mind no more separate than matter and energy.

इदं शरीरं कौन्तेय क्षेत्रमित्यभिधीयते ।
एतद्यो वेत्ति तं प्राहुः क्षेत्रज्ञ इति तद्विदः ॥ १ ॥
idaṃ śarīraṃ kaunteya kṣetramityabhidhīyate
etadyo vetti taṃ prāhuḥ kṣetrajña iti tadvidaḥ

13.1This body, Arjuna, is called the field. One who knows it, those who know call the knower of the field.

क्षेत्रज्ञं चापि मां विद्धि सर्वक्षेत्रेषु भारत ।
क्षेत्रक्षेत्रज्ञयोर्ज्ञानं यत्तज्ज्ञानं मतं मम ॥ २ ॥
kṣetrajñaṃ cāpi māṃ viddhi sarvakṣetreṣu bhārata
kṣetrakṣetrajñayorjñānaṃ yattajjñānaṃ mataṃ mama

13.2Know me as the knower of the field in all fields, Arjuna. Knowledge of the field and of its knower is what I hold to be knowledge.

महाभूतान्यहंकारो बुद्धिरव्यक्तमेव च ।
इन्द्रियाणि दशैकं च पञ्च चेन्द्रियगोचराः ॥ ५ ॥
mahābhūtānyahaṃkāro buddhiravyaktameva ca
indriyāṇi daśaikaṃ ca pañca cendriyagocarāḥ

13.5The great elements, the sense of "I", the intellect and the unmanifest; the ten senses and the one; and the five pastures of the senses;

The field is everything of prakriti, the elements, the senses and their objects, the mind, the intellect and the ego, and everything that arises in it, desire and aversion, pleasure and pain, the body, consciousness, resolve. The essay is careful that "body" in verse 1 is therefore imprecise: the field includes the mind. Then the list of what knowledge is, verses 7 to 11, which the essay calls an attractive picture of the modest, truly wise person, ending in a definition.

अध्यात्मज्ञाननित्यत्वं तत्त्वज्ञानार्थदर्शनम् ।
एतज्ज्ञानमिति प्रोक्तमज्ञानं यदतोऽन्यथा ॥ ११ ॥
adhyātmajñānanityatvaṃ tattvajñānārthadarśanam
etajjñānamiti proktamajñānaṃ yadato'nyathā

13.11Constancy in the knowledge of the Self, seeing the goal of knowledge of the truth: this is called knowledge; what is other than this is ignorance.

प्रकृतिं पुरुषं चैव विद्ध्यनादी उभावपि ।
विकारांश्च गुणांश्चैव विद्धि प्रकृतिसंभवान् ॥ १९ ॥
prakṛtiṃ puruṣaṃ caiva viddhyanādī ubhāvapi
vikārāṃśca guṇāṃścaiva viddhi prakṛtisaṃbhavān

13.19Know that nature and the knower are both without beginning; and know that the changes and the strands are born of nature.

उपद्रष्टानुमन्ता च भर्ता भोक्ता महेश्वरः ।
परमात्मेति चाप्युक्तो देहेऽस्मिन्पुरुषः परः ॥ २२ ॥
upadraṣṭānumantā ca bhartā bhoktā maheśvaraḥ
paramātmeti cāpyukto dehe'sminpuruṣaḥ paraḥ

13.22The supreme spirit in this body is called the witness, the approver, the supporter, the experiencer, the great lord, the highest Self.

समं सर्वेषु भूतेषु तिष्ठन्तं परमेश्वरम् ।
विनश्यत्स्वविनश्यन्तं यः पश्यति स पश्यति ॥ २७ ॥
samaṃ sarveṣu bhūteṣu tiṣṭhantaṃ parameśvaram
vinaśyatsvavinaśyantaṃ yaḥ paśyati sa paśyati

13.27One who sees the supreme lord standing the same in all beings, the imperishable among the perishing, sees.

प्रकृत्यैव च कर्माणि क्रियमाणानि सर्वशः ।
यः पश्यति तथात्मानमकर्तारं स पश्यति ॥ २९ ॥
prakṛtyaiva ca karmāṇi kriyamāṇāni sarvaśaḥ
yaḥ paśyati tathātmānamakartāraṃ sa paśyati

13.29One who sees that all actions are performed by nature alone, and that the Self is not the doer, sees.

यथा सर्वगतं सौक्ष्म्यादाकाशं नोपलिप्यते ।
सर्वत्रावस्थितो देहे तथात्मा नोपलिप्यते ॥ ३२ ॥
yathā sarvagataṃ saukṣmyādākāśaṃ nopalipyate
sarvatrāvasthito dehe tathātmā nopalipyate

13.32As the all-pervading ether is not tainted, being subtle, so the Self, though present in every body, is not tainted.

The introduction cites 13.29 as its text for actionless action, and 13.27 and 28 against the war reading: those who see the same lord everywhere do not harm themselves or others. Verse 22 gives the Self its titles, of which the first is witness, and the essay observes that the knower's relation to the field is permanent uninvolvement, watching the strands play.

Chapter 14: the forces of evolution

The fourteenth chapter is the gunas in detail, which Easwaran treats as the Gita's account of evolution. Sattva, rajas and tamas are born of nature and bind the embodied one to the body, each in its own way.

सत्त्वं रजस्तम इति गुणाः प्रकृतिसंभवाः ।
निबध्नन्ति महाबाहो देहे देहिनमव्ययम् ॥ ५ ॥
sattvaṃ rajastama iti guṇāḥ prakṛtisaṃbhavāḥ
nibadhnanti mahābāho dehe dehinamavyayam

14.5Sattva, rajas and tamas, the strands born of nature, bind the imperishable embodied one to the body, Arjuna.

सत्त्वं सुखे संजयति रजः कर्मणि भारत ।
ज्ञानमावृत्य तु तमः प्रमादे संजयत्युत ॥ ९ ॥
sattvaṃ sukhe saṃjayati rajaḥ karmaṇi bhārata
jñānamāvṛtya tu tamaḥ pramāde saṃjayatyuta

14.9Sattva binds to happiness, rajas to action; but tamas, veiling knowledge, binds to heedlessness.

ऊर्ध्वं गच्छन्ति सत्त्वस्था मध्ये तिष्ठन्ति राजसाः ।
जघन्यगुणवृत्तिस्था अधो गच्छन्ति तामसाः ॥ १८ ॥
ūrdhvaṃ gacchanti sattvasthā madhye tiṣṭhanti rājasāḥ
jaghanyaguṇavṛttisthā adho gacchanti tāmasāḥ

14.18Those who stand in sattva go upward; the passionate stay in the middle; those in the lowest strand's ways go down.

The essay characterises each in evolutionary terms: tamas the dead stability or the slide backwards, rajas ambiguous but the only force that gets a person out of tamas, sattva highest and still to be transcended. No mix of the three is ever stable, and every personality is a shifting balance of them.

Arjuna asks how to recognise one who has gone beyond them, and how they act.

गुणानेतानतीत्य त्रीन्देही देहसमुद्भवान् ।
जन्ममृत्युजरादुःखैर्विमुक्तोऽमृतमश्नुते ॥ २० ॥
guṇānetānatītya trīndehī dehasamudbhavān
janmamṛtyujarāduḥkhairvimukto'mṛtamaśnute

14.20Having gone beyond these three strands from which the body springs, the embodied one, freed from birth, death, age and sorrow, attains immortality.

उदासीनवदासीनो गुणैर्यो न विचाल्यते ।
गुणा वर्तन्त इत्येव योऽवतिष्ठति नेङ्गते ॥ २३ ॥
udāsīnavadāsīno guṇairyo na vicālyate
guṇā vartanta ityeva yo'vatiṣṭhati neṅgate

14.23One who sits as if indifferent, not moved by the strands, who stands and does not waver, thinking "the strands are at work";

मां च योऽव्यभिचारेण भक्तियोगेन सेवते ।
स गुणान्समतीत्यैतान्ब्रह्मभूयाय कल्पते ॥ २६ ॥
māṃ ca yo'vyabhicāreṇa bhaktiyogena sevate
sa guṇānsamatītyaitānbrahmabhūyāya kalpate

14.26And one who serves me with the unswerving yoga of devotion, having gone beyond these strands, is fit to become Brahman.

The answer is the same evenness as before, now explained: the illumined see thoughts and moods as the strands at play, not as themselves, and it is devotion, verse 26, that takes Arjuna past them.

Chapter 15: the supreme Self

The essay calls this a difficult, theological chapter. It opens with an image, the world as a fig tree growing upside down, its roots above and its branches below, on which the hymns are the leaves.

ऊर्ध्वमूलमधःशाखमश्वत्थं प्राहुरव्ययम् ।
छन्दांसि यस्य पर्णानि यस्तं वेद स वेदवित् ॥ १ ॥
ūrdhvamūlamadhaḥśākhamaśvatthaṃ prāhuravyayam
chandāṃsi yasya parṇāni yastaṃ veda sa vedavit

15.1They speak of an imperishable fig tree with its roots above and branches below, whose leaves are the hymns. One who knows it knows the Veda.

मसङ्गशस्त्रेण दृढेन छित्त्वा ॥ ३ ॥
masaṅgaśastreṇa dṛḍhena chittvā

15.3Its form is not seen here as it is, nor its end, nor its beginning, nor its base. Having cut this firm-rooted fig tree with the strong axe of detachment,

न तद्भासयते सूर्यो न शशाङ्को न पावकः ।
यद्गत्वा न निवर्तन्ते तद्धाम परमं मम ॥ ६ ॥
na tadbhāsayate sūryo na śaśāṅko na pāvakaḥ
yadgatvā na nivartante taddhāma paramaṃ mama

15.6Neither the sun nor the moon nor fire illumines it. Having gone there, they do not return; that is my supreme abode.

ममैवांशो जीवलोके जीवभूतः सनातनः ।
मनःषष्ठानीन्द्रियाणि प्रकृतिस्थानि कर्षति ॥ ७ ॥
mamaivāṃśo jīvaloke jīvabhūtaḥ sanātanaḥ
manaḥṣaṣṭhānīndriyāṇi prakṛtisthāni karṣati

15.7A fragment of myself, become a living self in the world of the living, eternal, draws to itself the senses, with the mind as the sixth, which rest in nature.

Verse 7 is the Gita's account of the individual: an eternal part of Krishna, carrying senses and mind from body to body as the wind carries scent. Verse 15 gives Krishna's presence in every heart as memory, knowledge and their loss, and the chapter closes on the doctrine the essay draws its line from.

वेदान्तकृद्वेदविदेव चाहम् ॥ १५ ॥
vedāntakṛdvedavideva cāham

15.15I am seated in the heart of all; from me come memory, knowledge and their removal. I alone am to be known by all the Vedas; I am the maker of the Vedanta and the knower of the Veda.

उत्तमः पुरुषस्त्वन्यः परमात्मेत्युदाहृतः ।
यो लोकत्रयमाविश्य बिभर्त्यव्यय ईश्वरः ॥ १७ ॥
uttamaḥ puruṣastvanyaḥ paramātmetyudāhṛtaḥ
yo lokatrayamāviśya bibhartyavyaya īśvaraḥ

15.17But there is another, the highest spirit, called the supreme Self, the imperishable lord who enters the three worlds and sustains them.

There are two orders of being, the perishable creatures and the changeless spirit, and beyond both the supreme Self. The essay draws the line Easwaran holds throughout: the liberated Self unites with Krishna and does not become God.

Chapter 16: two paths

The essay calls this the most unusual chapter, the one place the Gita gives the dark side equal time. It opens with the divine qualities, fearlessness first.

अभयं सत्त्वसंशुद्धिर्ज्ञानयोगव्यवस्थितिः ।
दानं दमश्च यज्ञश्च स्वाध्यायस्तप आर्जवम् ॥ १ ॥
abhayaṃ sattvasaṃśuddhirjñānayogavyavasthitiḥ
dānaṃ damaśca yajñaśca svādhyāyastapa ārjavam

16.1Fearlessness, purity of being, steadiness in the yoga of knowledge, giving, self-control, sacrifice, study, austerity, uprightness,

दम्भो दर्पोऽभिमानश्च क्रोधः पारुष्यमेव च ।
अज्ञानं चाभिजातस्य पार्थ संपदमासुरीम् ॥ ४ ॥
dambho darpo'bhimānaśca krodhaḥ pāruṣyameva ca
ajñānaṃ cābhijātasya pārtha saṃpadamāsurīm

16.4Hypocrisy, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness and ignorance, Arjuna, belong to one born to the demonic lot.

दैवी संपद्विमोक्षाय निबन्धायासुरी मता ।
मा शुचः संपदं दैवीमभिजातोऽसि पाण्डव ॥ ५ ॥
daivī saṃpadvimokṣāya nibandhāyāsurī matā
mā śucaḥ saṃpadaṃ daivīmabhijāto'si pāṇḍava

16.5The divine lot is held to lead to release, the demonic to bondage. Do not grieve, Arjuna: you are born to the divine lot.

The demonic personality is described at length and without disguise: it holds that the world has no truth and no order and is born of desire; it is insatiable, anxious, proud of its wealth and its gifts; its hell is of its own making, often in this life. The essay adds that even here no one is damned; the choice is always open and the Self cannot be stained.

असत्यमप्रतिष्ठं ते जगदाहुरनीश्वरम् ।
अपरस्परसंभूतं किमन्यत्कामहैतुकम् ॥ ८ ॥
asatyamapratiṣṭhaṃ te jagadāhuranīśvaram
aparasparasaṃbhūtaṃ kimanyatkāmahaitukam

16.8They say the world is without truth, without a basis, without a lord, not born of any order, caused by nothing but desire.

त्रिविधं नरकस्येदं द्वारं नाशनमात्मनः ।
कामः क्रोधस्तथा लोभस्तस्मादेतत्त्रयं त्यजेत् ॥ २१ ॥
trividhaṃ narakasyedaṃ dvāraṃ nāśanamātmanaḥ
kāmaḥ krodhastathā lobhastasmādetattrayaṃ tyajet

16.21Threefold is this gate of hell that destroys the self: desire, anger and greed. Therefore one should give up these three.

Chapter 17: the power of faith

Arjuna picks up the last verse of chapter 16, which told him to follow the scriptures, and asks about those who worship with faith but outside the rules. The answer sorts faith itself, and then worship, food, sacrifice, discipline and giving, by the three strands.

त्रिविधा भवति श्रद्धा देहिनां सा स्वभावजा ।
सात्त्विकी राजसी चैव तामसी चेति तां शृणु ॥ २ ॥
trividhā bhavati śraddhā dehināṃ sā svabhāvajā
sāttvikī rājasī caiva tāmasī ceti tāṃ śṛṇu

17.2Threefold is the faith of the embodied, born of their nature: of sattva, of rajas, of tamas. Hear of it.

सत्त्वानुरूपा सर्वस्य श्रद्धा भवति भारत ।
श्रद्धामयोऽयं पुरुषो यो यच्छ्रद्धः स एव सः ॥ ३ ॥
sattvānurūpā sarvasya śraddhā bhavati bhārata
śraddhāmayo'yaṃ puruṣo yo yacchraddhaḥ sa eva saḥ

17.3Everyone's faith accords with their nature, Arjuna. A person is made of faith: as his faith is, so is he.

Shraddha is a word Easwaran calls untranslatable and glosses as what is held in the heart, the sum of a person's values; the essay says it is the sum of our values and notes the obscure etymology. The chapter's middle is a set of lists, and the one the essay flags is the account of tapas, discipline, in three registers.

देवद्विजगुरुप्राज्ञपूजनं शौचमार्जवम् ।
ब्रह्मचर्यमहिंसा च शारीरं तप उच्यते ॥ १४ ॥
devadvijaguruprājñapūjanaṃ śaucamārjavam
brahmacaryamahiṃsā ca śārīraṃ tapa ucyate

17.14Honour to the gods, the twice-born, teachers and the wise; purity, uprightness, continence and non-harm: this is called the discipline of the body.

अनुद्वेगकरं वाक्यं सत्यं प्रियहितं च यत् ।
स्वाध्यायाभ्यसनं चैव वाङ्मयं तप उच्यते ॥ १५ ॥
anudvegakaraṃ vākyaṃ satyaṃ priyahitaṃ ca yat
svādhyāyābhyasanaṃ caiva vāṅmayaṃ tapa ucyate

17.15Speech that does not disturb, that is true, pleasant and beneficial, and the practice of recitation: this is called the discipline of speech.

मनः प्रसादः सौम्यत्वं मौनमात्मविनिग्रहः ।
भावसंशुद्धिरित्येतत्तपो मानसमुच्यते ॥ १६ ॥
manaḥ prasādaḥ saumyatvaṃ maunamātmavinigrahaḥ
bhāvasaṃśuddhirityetattapo mānasamucyate

17.16Serenity of mind, gentleness, silence, self-restraint, purity of being: this is called the discipline of the mind.

The chapter sorts six things by the three strands, and the sorting is the argument: faith is not one thing, and neither is any act of faith.

ब्राह्मणास्तेन वेदाश्च यज्ञाश्च विहिताः पुरा ॥ २३ ॥

The chapter ends on the formula Om tat sat and on the verdict that an act of sacrifice, discipline or giving done without faith is asat, without being and without worth, here or hereafter.

Chapter 18: freedom and renunciation

The final chapter, the essay says, roams over many subjects, and it opens with the question Arjuna began the dialogue with: what is the difference between giving up action and giving up its fruit?

काम्यानां कर्मणां न्यासं संन्यासं कवयो विदुः ।
सर्वकर्मफलत्यागं प्राहुस्त्यागं विचक्षणाः ॥ २ ॥
kāmyānāṃ karmaṇāṃ nyāsaṃ saṃnyāsaṃ kavayo viduḥ
sarvakarmaphalatyāgaṃ prāhustyāgaṃ vicakṣaṇāḥ

18.2The giving up of actions done from desire the poets know as renunciation; the giving up of the fruit of all actions the wise call relinquishment.

न हि देहभृता शक्यं त्यक्तुं कर्माण्यशेषतः ।
यस्तु कर्मफलत्यागी स त्यागीत्यभिधीयते ॥ ११ ॥
na hi dehabhṛtā śakyaṃ tyaktuṃ karmāṇyaśeṣataḥ
yastu karmaphalatyāgī sa tyāgītyabhidhīyate

18.11One who bears a body cannot give up actions entirely. But one who gives up the fruits of action is called a relinquisher.

अनिष्टमिष्टं मिश्रं च त्रिविधं कर्मणः फलम् ।
भवत्यत्यागिनां प्रेत्य न तु संन्यासिनां क्वचित् ॥ १२ ॥
aniṣṭamiṣṭaṃ miśraṃ ca trividhaṃ karmaṇaḥ phalam
bhavatyatyāgināṃ pretya na tu saṃnyāsināṃ kvacit

18.12Unwanted, wanted and mixed: threefold is the fruit of action after death for those who do not relinquish; for the renouncers, none at all.

Krishna rejects sannyasa as impossible for anyone with a body, and the essay states plainly that the Gita is aimed at people who live in the world. Sacrifice, giving and discipline are to be kept, but without desire for their reward; relinquishment itself comes in three kinds, and only the sattvic kind, doing what is one's to do because it is, wanting nothing, counts. Then an analysis of action into five factors, from Sankhya.

अधिष्ठानं तथा कर्ता करणं च पृथग्विधम् ।
विविधाश्च पृथक्चेष्टा दैवं चैवात्र पञ्चमम् ॥ १४ ॥
adhiṣṭhānaṃ tathā kartā karaṇaṃ ca pṛthagvidham
vividhāśca pṛthakceṣṭā daivaṃ caivātra pañcamam

18.14The seat, the doer, the various instruments, the many distinct movements, and providence as the fifth:

तत्रैवं सति कर्तारमात्मानं केवलं तु यः ।
पश्यत्यकृतबुद्धित्वान्न स पश्यति दुर्मतिः ॥ १६ ॥
tatraivaṃ sati kartāramātmānaṃ kevalaṃ tu yaḥ
paśyatyakṛtabuddhitvānna sa paśyati durmatiḥ

18.16That being so, one who sees himself alone as the doer, through an untrained understanding, does not see, being dull-witted.

Knowledge, action, doer, understanding, resolve and happiness are each sorted by the strands, and the essay calls the three happinesses a practical application of the abstract theory.

18.38That which is like nectar at first, from the contact of the senses with their objects, but like poison at the end, is held to be the happiness of rajas.

The chapter then treats the four social orders as functions of nature and the strands, a short and, the essay says, liberal treatment, and returns to the line from chapter 3.

यतः प्रवृत्तिर्भूतानां येन सर्वमिदं ततम् ।
स्वकर्मणा तमभ्यर्च्य सिद्धिं विन्दति मानवः ॥ ४६ ॥
yataḥ pravṛttirbhūtānāṃ yena sarvamidaṃ tatam
svakarmaṇā tamabhyarcya siddhiṃ vindati mānavaḥ

18.46By worshipping, through his own work, the one from whom beings proceed and by whom all this is pervaded, a man attains perfection.

श्रेयान्स्वधर्मो विगुणः परधर्मात्स्वनुष्ठितात् ।
स्वभावनियतं कर्म कुर्वन्नाप्नोति किल्बिषम् ॥ ४७ ॥
śreyānsvadharmo viguṇaḥ paradharmātsvanuṣṭhitāt
svabhāvaniyataṃ karma kurvannāpnoti kilbiṣam

18.47Better one's own duty done imperfectly than another's done well. Doing the work prescribed by one's own nature, one incurs no fault.

सहजं कर्म कौन्तेय सदोषमपि न त्यजेत् ।
सर्वारम्भा हि दोषेण धूमेनाग्निरिवावृताः ॥ ४८ ॥
sahajaṃ karma kaunteya sadoṣamapi na tyajet
sarvārambhā hi doṣeṇa dhūmenāgnirivāvṛtāḥ

18.48One should not give up the work one is born to, Arjuna, though it has faults; for every undertaking is wrapped in faults, as fire in smoke.

Then the third portrait of the realised person, verses 49 to 56, the one who has attained.

असक्तबुद्धिः सर्वत्र जितात्मा विगतस्पृहः ।

The last word is the minister's. Sanjaya, who has been reporting the whole conversation to the blind king, says what he believes follows wherever these two are together, and the essay reads the pairing as the point: the Self and the self, the charioteer and the archer, in one chariot.

What this edition chooses

Easwaran is candid about the choices his edition makes, and a reader should hold them as choices.

The first is the reading itself. Taking Krishna as the Self within and the battlefield as the mind is, he says, the only way to grasp the Gita as spiritual instruction, and it is the reading of the mystics and of Gandhi. It is not the only reading the text has had. Traditions that take Krishna as a personal God and the war as a war have read the same verses for as long, and the sixteenth and eighteenth chapters, with their talk of duty, social order and the warrior's nature, resist the allegory more than the second does. Easwaran's honest reply is Gandhi's: practise it and see.

The second is the translation's plainness. He renders the Sanskrit into clean prose, drops most of the technical apparatus, and admits that his English for the Sankhya terms and for the gunas is imprecise, that renunciation and shraddha have no adequate English, and that the conventional division of the eighteen chapters into three sixes is loose and hard to find in the text. The translation is made for reading straight through, and it should be read that way first; the middle chapters, which he expands with the longest essays, are the ones that reward a second pass.

The third is the claim that the three portraits describe one person. The Gita's texture is more varied than that; the second-chapter sage, the twelfth-chapter devotee and the eighteenth-chapter renouncer speak different vocabularies, and the essays themselves note that devotion gains ground chapter by chapter over knowledge and ritual. What Easwaran's reading gains is a single figure, active, compassionate and needing nothing but the chance to give, which he calls original goodness: not acquired, only uncovered. What it costs is the text's own arguments with itself, which Arjuna voices at the start of chapters 3, 5 and 18 and which the essays record with some sympathy.

The Gita traces ruin back to what the mind dwells on

In Gita 2.62–63 judgment is lost at the end of a chain that starts with nothing more than dwelling on something: dwelling leads to attachment, then desire, anger, delusion, confused memory, and finally the loss of judgment.

Becausethe verses give a sequence of mental events — dwelling, attachment, desire, anger, delusion, confusion of memory, loss of judgment — with no measurement behind any link
LimitsIt is a chain of introspectible states offered as a causal sequence, with no evidence that the links run in that order or that breaking an early one prevents the later ones.
PracticeIntervene at the first link rather than the last. What you keep returning to decides what you will want, and by the time judgement is at stake it is too late to argue.
After AIThe first link is now managed by other people: what you dwell on is largely what a feed or an assistant puts in front of you, which moves the intervention point outside the practitioner the verses address.
